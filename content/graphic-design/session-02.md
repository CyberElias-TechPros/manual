# Design Foundations Colour Type

## A. Session Identification

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain the colour wheel and identify primary, secondary and tertiary colours.
2. Create effective colour combinations using complementary, analogous and monochromatic schemes.
3. Explain colour psychology and how different colours create different moods.
4. Identify font categories: serif, sans-serif, script and display.
5. Pair fonts effectively using contrast and hierarchy.
6. Apply typographic hierarchy to create clear visual order.
7. Explain why most designs should use no more than 2–3 colours and 2 fonts.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"The Colour Mood Test"
Instructor Setup: Display 5 designs, each dominated by a different colour (red, blue, green, yellow, black). Do not tell the
learners what the designs are for.
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Look at these five designs. They are all for different businesses — a restaurant, a
bank, a children's party, a law firm and a nightclub. But I am not going to tell you which is which. Your task is to match each
design to the business based on the colour alone. You have 2 minutes."
- Give learners 2 minutes to match.]
"Now let us check. [Reveal the answers.] How did you do? [Pause.]
Here is the interesting thing: you did not need any design training to know that the bright yellow design was for the children's
party and the dark blue design was for the bank. Colour communicates mood and meaning instantly. Today, you will learn how
to use colour and typography deliberately — so that your designs communicate exactly what you want them to
communicate."
Expected Learner Response: Learners will correctly match most designs to their businesses based on colour. This activates
intuition and sets the context for the session.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Instructor Script: "Before we start, let us remember what we learned in the last session. Quick review."
Review Questions and Expected Answers:
1. "What are the six core design principles?"
Hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance, white space, purpose/audience.
2. "What is hierarchy?"
The arrangement of elements in order of importance.
3. "What is contrast?"
Difference — making elements stand out from each other.
4. "Why is white space important?"
It gives the viewer's eye a place to rest and makes the design easier to read.
5. "What is the 'less is more' principle?"
Use fewer elements, colours and fonts. Keep it simple.
How to Address Common Gaps:
If a learner cannot remember the principles, ask a partner to help.
If a learner forgot the "less is more" principle, remind them: "If everything is important, nothing is important."

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: The Colour Wheel (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The colour wheel is a circular diagram that shows how colours relate to each other. It is one of the most
important tools in design.
Primary Colours: Red, blue and yellow. These are the three colours that cannot be created by mixing other colours.
Secondary Colours: Orange, green and purple. These are created by mixing two primary colours:
Red + Yellow = Orange
Blue + Yellow = Green
Red + Blue = Purple
Tertiary Colours: Red-orange, yellow-orange, yellow-green, blue-green, blue-purple and red-purple. These are created by
mixing a primary colour with a secondary colour.
Warm and Cool Colours:
Warm colours: Red, orange, yellow. These colours feel energetic, warm and exciting.
Cool colours: Blue, green, purple. These colours feel calm, professional and trustworthy.
How to Use the Colour Wheel: The colour wheel helps you choose colours that work together. We will learn three common
colour combinations in the next topic."
Topic 2: Colour Combinations (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Here are three colour combinations that always work:
1. Complementary: Colours that are opposite each other on the colour wheel. For example, blue and orange, or red and green.
Complementary colours create high contrast and visual energy. They are great for call-to-action buttons and important
information.
2. Analogous: Colours that are next to each other on the colour wheel. For example, blue, blue-green and green. Analogous
colours create a harmonious, calming effect. They are great for backgrounds and designs that need to feel cohesive.
3. Monochromatic: Different shades, tints and tones of a single colour. For example, light blue, medium blue and dark blue.
Monochromatic schemes are elegant and sophisticated. They are great for professional designs.
The 60-30-10 Rule: When using multiple colours, follow the 60-30-10 rule:
60% dominant colour (usually a neutral or background colour)
30% secondary colour (supports the dominant colour)
10% accent colour (for call-to-action and important information)
Example: For a business flyer:
60% white (background)
30% dark blue (headings and main elements)
10% orange (call-to-action button)"
Topic 3: Colour Psychology (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Colours create emotional responses. Here are some common associations:
Red: Energy, urgency, passion, danger. Used for sales, food and entertainment.
Blue: Trust, calm, professionalism, stability. Used for banks, tech companies and healthcare.
Green: Nature, growth, health, money. Used for organic products, finance and environmental causes.
Yellow: Happiness, optimism, attention. Used for children's products, food and warnings.
Orange: Creativity, enthusiasm, affordability. Used for entertainment, food and call-to-action.
Black: Luxury, power, sophistication. Used for premium brands and formal events.
White: Cleanliness, simplicity, purity. Used for healthcare, technology and minimalist designs.
Important Note: Colour associations are not universal. They vary by culture and context. In Nigeria, for example, white is often
associated with purity and celebration, while in some other cultures it is associated with mourning. Always consider your
audience when choosing colours."
Topic 4: Typography — The Art of Text (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Typography is the art of arranging text so that it is readable, beautiful and appropriate. Good typography
makes text easy to read. Bad typography makes text hard to read — even if the words are the same.
Font Categories:
1. Serif: Serif fonts have small lines or "feet" at the ends of the letters. Examples: Times New Roman, Georgia, Garamond. Serif
fonts feel traditional, formal and trustworthy. They are good for body text in formal documents.
2. Sans-serif: Sans-serif fonts do not have the small lines at the ends. Examples: Arial, Calibri, Helvetica. Sans-serif fonts feel
modern, clean and simple. They are good for headings, digital designs and informal documents.
3. Script: Script fonts look like handwriting. Examples: Brush Script, Pacifico, Dancing Script. Script fonts feel elegant, personal
and creative. They are good for invitations, logos and decorative text. They are hard to read in large amounts.
4. Display: Display fonts are decorative and attention-grabbing. Examples: Impact, Bebas Neue, Lobster. Display fonts feel bold,
fun and dramatic. They are good for headlines and posters. They are not good for body text.
Font Pairing: Most designs use two fonts: one for headings and one for body text. The key to good font pairing is contrast. Pair
a serif with a sans-serif, or a display font with a clean sans-serif. Do not pair two fonts that look too similar — it looks like a
mistake.
Example of Good Pairing:
Heading: Montserrat (sans-serif, bold)
Body: Merriweather (serif, regular)
Example of Bad Pairing:
Heading: Arial (sans-serif)
Body: Helvetica (sans-serif) These two fonts look almost the same. It looks like a mistake."

What the Instructor Prepares:
A blank flyer template projected on the screen

2. Colour psychology: "Red and orange are associated with food and energy. I will use warm colours."
3. Colour combination: "I will use a complementary scheme: orange (dominant) and dark blue (contrast). Orange for the

4. 60-30-10 rule: "60% orange (background), 30% white (text areas), 10% dark blue (call-to-action button)."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor making deliberate colour choices based on principles.
Expected Result: Learners can explain why specific colours were chosen.
Demonstration 2: Choosing Fonts (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
The same flyer template with colours applied
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Now I will choose fonts for this flyer."
1. Purpose and audience: "The restaurant is casual but professional. I want the fonts to feel friendly but trustworthy."
2. Font categories: "I will use a display font for the restaurant name (to make it stand out) and a sans-serif font for the body
text (to make it easy to read)."
3. Font pairing: "Heading: Montserrat (sans-serif, bold, large). Body: Open Sans (sans-serif, regular, smaller). These two fonts
are both sans-serif but different enough to create hierarchy."
4. Hierarchy: "The restaurant name is the largest text. The tagline is second. The menu items are third. The contact
information is smallest."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor making deliberate font choices based on principles.
Expected Result: Learners can explain why specific fonts were chosen.
Demonstration 3: Common Colour and Font Mistakes (5 minutes)

Three designs with common mistakes
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Now let us look at some common mistakes."
1. Too many colours: "This design uses 7 different colours. It looks chaotic. The fix: use 2–3 colours maximum."
2. Poor contrast: "This design has yellow text on a white background. It is hard to read. The fix: use dark text on a light

3. Too many fonts: "This design uses 4 different fonts. It looks unprofessional. The fix: use 2 fonts maximum."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor identifying and explaining common mistakes.

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "The Restaurant That Changed Its Colours"
Situation: A restaurant in Port Harcourt has been struggling to attract customers. Their flyer is cluttered, with 8 different
colours, 5 different fonts and no clear message. They hire a designer to fix it. The designer simplifies the design to 2 colours (red
and white) and 2 fonts (a bold sans-serif for headings and a clean sans-serif for body text). The new flyer is clean, appetising and
professional. Within a month, customer visits increase by 40%.
The Problem: The original flyer failed because it had too many colours and fonts, poor contrast and no clear hierarchy.
Why It Matters: Colour and typography are not just aesthetic choices — they are communication tools. The right colours and
fonts can attract customers, build trust and communicate your brand. The wrong choices can drive customers away.
Relevant Concept: Colour theory and typography. If the original designer had understood these principles, the flyer would have
been effective from the start.
Worked Solution: The designer succeeded because they:
1. Chose colours based on colour psychology (red = food, energy).
2. Used a simple colour combination (red and white).
3. Chose fonts based on readability and tone (bold sans-serif for headings, clean sans-serif for body).
4. Created clear hierarchy (restaurant name largest, contact information smallest).
5. Left plenty of white space.
Reasoning: Good design is not about making things look fancy. It is about making things work. The right colours and fonts
make a design work.
Possible Mistake: The designer might have chosen colours and fonts based on personal preference rather than the brand and
audience. This is a common mistake.
Correction: Always choose colours and fonts based on the purpose, audience and brand — not on personal taste.
Transfer Exercise: "Your friend is designing a flyer for a gym. She wants to use pink and purple because they are her favourite
colours. What advice would you give her?"
Professional Connection: Every design you create will be judged by its colours and fonts. Whether you are designing for a
restaurant, a church, a school or a business, the same principles apply.

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Using Too Many Colours
Symptoms: The learner uses 5 or more colours in one design. Likely Cause: The learner thinks more colours make the design
more attractive. How to Investigate: Count the colours in the design. Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use the 60-
30-10 rule and a maximum of 2–3 colours. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses 2–3 colours. How to
Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Less is more."
Mistake 2: Poor Colour Contrast
Symptoms: The learner uses light text on a light background or dark text on a dark background. Likely Cause: The learner does
not understand the importance of contrast. How to Investigate: Check the text colour and background colour. Safe
Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use high contrast — dark text on a light background or light text on a dark background.
How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses high contrast. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner:
"If you cannot read it easily, fix the contrast."
Mistake 3: Using Too Many Fonts
Symptoms: The learner uses 3 or more fonts in one design. Likely Cause: The learner thinks more fonts make the design more
interesting. How to Investigate: Count the fonts in the design. Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use a maximum of
2 fonts — one for headings, one for body text. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses 2 fonts. How to
Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Two fonts maximum."
Mistake 4: Using Script Fonts for Body Text
Symptoms: The learner uses a script or display font for large amounts of text. Likely Cause: The learner thinks script fonts look
elegant. How to Investigate: Check the font used for body text. Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use script fonts for
headings only and serif or sans-serif fonts for body text. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses
appropriate fonts for body text. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Script fonts are for decoration, not
readability."
Mistake 5: Choosing Colours Based on Personal Preference
Symptoms: The learner chooses colours they like rather than colours that suit the brand and audience. Likely Cause: The learner
does not understand colour psychology. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "Why did you choose these colours?" Safe
Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to choose colours based on the purpose, audience and brand — not personal taste. How
to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner chooses colours based on principles. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind
the learner: "Choose colours for the audience, not for yourself."

## M. KNOWLEDGE CHECK (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
Questions
1. Multiple Choice: What are the three primary colours? a) Red, green, blue b) Red, blue, yellow c) Orange, green, purple d) Red,
yellow, green
Answer: b) Red, blue, yellow
2. Short Answer: What is the 60-30-10 rule in colour?
Answer: The 60-30-10 rule states that a design should use 60% dominant colour, 30% secondary colour and 10% accent colour.
3. Multiple Choice: What type of font is best for body text in a formal document? a) Script b) Display c) Serif d) Decorative
Answer: c) Serif
4. Explain in Your Own Words: Why is it important to limit the number of fonts in a design?
Answer: Using too many fonts makes a design look cluttered and unprofessional. Limiting fonts to 2 (one for headings, one for
body text) creates a clean, consistent and professional appearance.
5. Practical Identification: Look at this design example. Identify the colour combination (complementary, analogous or
monochromatic) and the font categories (serif, sans-serif, script, display).
Answer: The learner correctly identifies the colour combination and font categories.
6. Scenario-Based: Your friend is designing a business card for a law firm. She wants to use a script font and bright pink colours.
What advice would you give?
Answer: A script font and bright pink are not appropriate for a law firm. Law firms need to project professionalism, trust and
stability. I would recommend using a serif or sans-serif font and professional colours like dark blue, grey or black.
7. Error-Detecting: A learner writes in their analysis: "The design uses complementary colours, which is good." What is wrong
with this statement, and what should they write instead?
Answer: The statement is too vague. The learner should specify which colours are complementary and why they work (or do
not work) for the design. For example: "The design uses blue and orange, which are complementary colours. This creates high
contrast and visual energy, which is appropriate for a sports event."

Plain-English Summary
Today you learned about colour and typography — two of the most powerful tools in a designer's toolkit. You learned about
the colour wheel, colour combinations (complementary, analogous, monochromatic), colour psychology, the 60-30-10 rule,
font categories (serif, sans-serif, script, display) and font pairing. You also learned to analyse colour and font choices in real
designs.
Five Important Takeaways
1. Use the colour wheel to choose colours that work together.
2. Follow the 60-30-10 rule: 60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent.
3. Choose colours based on psychology and audience, not personal preference.
4. Use a maximum of 2 fonts — one for headings, one for body text.
5. Script and display fonts are for decoration, not readability.
