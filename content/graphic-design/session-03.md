## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Select appropriate images for different design contexts.
2. Crop and frame images effectively using the rule of thirds.
3. Apply the rule of thirds to create balanced compositions.
4. Understand visual flow and how readers scan designs.
5. Identify and fix common composition mistakes.
6. Analyse real designs for image quality and composition.

image.
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Look at these two flyers. They have the exact same text, the same colours and the
same fonts. The only difference is the image. Which one looks more professional? [Pause.]
The one on the right, right? Why? [Pause for answers — the image is better, it is positioned well, it is not stretched.]
The image on the left is stretched, poorly cropped and positioned awkwardly. The image on the right is well-composed,
properly cropped and positioned to support the text.
Today, you will learn how to select, crop and compose images effectively. You will learn the rule of thirds, visual flow and how
to avoid common composition mistakes. By the end of this session, you will be able to look at any design and know
immediately whether the image is working or not."
Expected Learner Response: Learners will correctly identify the well-composed design. This activates visual intuition and sets
the context for the session.

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: Image Selection (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The right image can make a design. The wrong image can break it. Here is how to choose the right image.
Criteria for Good Images:
1. Relevance: The image must be relevant to the message. A picture of food on a restaurant flyer is relevant. A picture of a car
is not.
2. Quality: The image must be high-resolution and sharp. Blurry or pixelated images look unprofessional.
3. Mood: The image must match the mood of the design. A serious image for a serious topic. A fun image for a fun topic.
4. Composition: The image must be well-composed. The subject should be clear and well-positioned.
5. Colour: The image colours should work with the design's colour scheme. An image with clashing colours will look out of
place.
Where to Find Good Images:
Your own photographs (best option — unique and free)
Free stock photo websites (Unsplash, Pexels, Pixabay)
Canva's built-in image library (free tier)
Important Note: Do not use images from Google Images without checking the copyright. Using copyrighted images without
permission can get you into legal trouble. Always use your own photos or images from free stock photo websites."
Topic 2: The Rule of Thirds (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The rule of thirds is one of the most important composition principles in design. It is simple and it always
works.
How It Works: Imagine dividing your image into nine equal parts with two horizontal lines and two vertical lines. This creates a
3x3 grid. The rule of thirds says that you should place the most important elements of your image along these lines or at their
intersections.
Why It Works: Placing the subject in the centre of the image often looks static and boring. Placing the subject off-centre (along
the rule of thirds lines) creates visual interest and balance.
How to Apply It:
1. When taking a photograph, position the subject off-centre.
2. When cropping an image in Canva, use the rule of thirds grid to position the subject.
3. When designing a layout, place key elements along the rule of thirds lines.
Example: On a business flyer, the main image should be positioned along one of the vertical rule of thirds lines, not in the
centre. The text should be positioned along the other vertical line. This creates a balanced, dynamic layout."
Topic 3: Visual Flow and Reading Patterns (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Visual flow is the path the viewer's eye follows when looking at a design. Understanding visual flow helps
you arrange elements so that the viewer sees them in the right order.
Common Reading Patterns:
F-Pattern: The eye starts at the top-left, moves across to the top-right, then down to the middle-left, across to the middle-right,
then down to the bottom-left and across to the bottom-right. This is common for text-heavy designs like newsletters and
reports.
Z-Pattern: The eye starts at the top-left, moves diagonally to the bottom-right, then across to the bottom-left. This is common
for designs with a clear call-to-action, like flyers and social media posts.
How to Use Visual Flow:
Place the most important element where the eye starts (usually top-left or centre).
Place the call-to-action where the eye ends (usually bottom-right or centre).
Use lines, arrows and colour to guide the eye along the desired path.
Example: On a business flyer, the event name should be at the top (where the eye starts). The date and time should be in the
middle. The call-to-action ("Register Now") should be at the bottom (where the eye ends)."
Topic 4: Common Composition Mistakes (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Here are the most common composition mistakes and how to avoid them:
1. Stretching images: Dragging the corner of an image without holding Shift distorts it. The fix: always hold Shift when
resizing, or use the corner handles.
2. Centring everything: Placing all elements in the centre creates a static, boring design. The fix: use the rule of thirds and off-
centre positioning.
3. Ignoring the background: Placing text over a busy background makes it hard to read. The fix: use a solid background or a
semi-transparent overlay behind the text.
4. Too many focal points: Having multiple elements competing for attention confuses the viewer. The fix: create a clear
hierarchy with one main focal point.
5. Cropping too tightly: Cutting off important parts of the image (e.g., someone's head) looks unprofessional. the fix: leave
breathing room around the subject."

4. A business card with three competing images.

5. Subject's head cropped off: The cropping is too tight. Fix: Leave breathing room around the subject.
6. No clear focal point: Multiple elements compete for attention. Fix: Create a clear hierarchy with one main focal point.

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "The Flyer That Did Not Sell"
Situation: A small business in Port Harcourt creates a flyer to promote a sale. They use a low-resolution image from Google,
stretch it to fit the flyer, place all the text in the centre over the busy background, and print 1,000 copies. Almost no one comes
to the sale.
The Problem: The flyer failed because of poor image selection and composition. The image was low-quality and stretched, the
text was hard to read, and there was no clear focal point.
Why It Matters: A flyer is often the first impression a business makes. A poorly composed flyer suggests the business is
unprofessional. A well-composed flyer suggests the business is trustworthy and worth visiting.
Relevant Concept: Image selection and composition. If the designer had understood these principles, the flyer would have
been effective.
Worked Solution: If the designer had taken this course, here is what they would do:
1. Use a high-quality image from their own photos or a free stock photo website.
2. Resize the image proportionally (holding Shift).
3. Position the image along the rule of thirds line.
4. Add a semi-transparent overlay behind the text for readability.
5. Create a clear focal point — the sale percentage in large, bold text.
6. Use the Z-pattern to guide the eye from the headline to the call-to-action.
Reasoning: Good composition is not about making things look fancy. It is about making things work. A well-composed flyer
communicates clearly and attracts customers.
Possible Mistake: The designer might think that using a low-resolution image is acceptable because "it is just a flyer." In reality,
low-quality images make the entire design look unprofessional.
Correction: Always use high-quality images. If you cannot find a high-quality image, use a simple graphic or shape instead.
Transfer Exercise: "Your friend is designing a poster for a school event. She found an image on Google but it is blurry when she
zooms in. What advice would you give her?"
Professional Connection: Every design you create will be judged by its image composition. Whether you are designing a flyer, a
poster, a social media post or a business card, the same principles apply.

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Using Low-Quality Images
Symptoms: The learner uses blurry or pixelated images. Likely Cause: The learner does not understand image resolution. How
to Investigate: Zoom in on the image. Is it sharp or blurry? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use high-quality
images from their own photos or free stock photo websites. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses high-
quality images. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "If it looks blurry when you zoom in, do not use it."
Mistake 2: Stretching Images
Symptoms: The learner stretches images, distorting the subjects. Likely Cause: The learner does not hold Shift when resizing.
How to Investigate: Look at the image. Do the subjects look distorted? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to always
hold Shift when resizing images. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner resizes images proportionally. How to
Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Always hold Shift when resizing."
Mistake 3: Placing Text Over Busy Backgrounds
Symptoms: The learner places text over busy backgrounds, making it hard to read. Likely Cause: The learner does not
understand the importance of readability. How to Investigate: Can you read the text easily? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the
learner to use a semi-transparent overlay behind the text. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner ensures text is
readable. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "If you cannot read it easily, fix it."
Mistake 4: Centring Everything
Symptoms: The learner centres all elements, creating a static design. Likely Cause: The learner does not understand the rule of
thirds. How to Investigate: Look at the layout. Is everything in the centre? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use the
rule of thirds and off-centre positioning. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses off-centre positioning.
How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Centre is static. Off-centre is dynamic."
Mistake 5: Cropping Too Tightly
Symptoms: The learner crops images too tightly, cutting off important parts. Likely Cause: The learner does not leave breathing
room around the subject. How to Investigate: Look at the image. Is the subject cut off? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the
learner to leave breathing room around the subject. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner leaves breathing
room. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Leave breathing room around the subject."

## M. KNOWLEDGE CHECK (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
Questions
1. Multiple Choice: What is the rule of thirds? a) Dividing an image into three equal parts b) Dividing an image into nine equal
parts with two horizontal and two vertical lines c) Using three colours in a design d) Placing three images on a page
Answer: b) Dividing an image into nine equal parts with two horizontal and two vertical lines
2. Short Answer: What are the five criteria for selecting a good image?
Answer: Relevance, quality, mood, composition and colour.
3. Multiple Choice: What is the Z-pattern in visual flow? a) The eye moves in a Z shape from top-left to bottom-right b) The eye
moves in a circle around the design c) The eye moves from bottom to top d) The eye moves randomly
Answer: a) The eye moves in a Z shape from top-left to bottom-right
4. Explain in Your Own Words: Why should you not place text over a busy background?
Answer: Text over a busy background is hard to read. The background competes with the text for attention. To fix this, use a
solid background or a semi-transparent overlay behind the text.
5. Practical Identification: Look at this design example. Identify the visual flow — where does the eye start and end?
Answer: The learner correctly traces the visual flow.
6. Scenario-Based: Your friend is designing a flyer and wants to use an image she found on Google. The image is blurry when
she zooms in. What advice would you give?
Answer: Do not use the blurry image. Use a high-quality image from your own photos or a free stock photo website like
Unsplash or Pexels. A blurry image will make the entire design look unprofessional.
7. Error-Detecting: A learner writes in their analysis: "The image is well-composed because it is in the centre." What is wrong
with this statement, and what should they write instead?
Answer: Centring the image creates a static, boring composition. The learner should write: "The image is positioned in the
centre, which creates a static layout. It would be more dynamic if it were positioned along the rule of thirds line."

## N. REFLECTION AND RECAP (5 minutes)

Plain-English Summary
Today you learned about images and composition. You learned how to select appropriate images, apply the rule of thirds,
understand visual flow and avoid common composition mistakes. You also learned to analyse image composition in real
designs. These are the skills that make designs look professional and polished.
Five Important Takeaways
1. Choose images that are relevant, high-quality and appropriate for the mood.
2. Use the rule of thirds to create balanced, dynamic compositions.
3. Understand visual flow — where the eye starts and ends.
4. Never place text over a busy background without an overlay.
5. Always resize images proportionally — never stretch them.
Questions That Help You Recognise What You Can Now Do
Can you select appropriate images for a design?
Can you apply the rule of thirds?
Can you identify and fix common composition mistakes?
Can you analyse image composition in a design?
Connection to the Next Session
This is the last session on design foundations. You now have all the theoretical knowledge you need. In the next session, you will
start using Canva — the tool you will use to create designs throughout the rest of the course. You now know the principles.
Next, you will learn the tool.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable Signs of Competence
The learner selects appropriate images.
The learner applies the rule of thirds.
The learner traces visual flow.
The learner identifies and fixes composition mistakes.
The learner analyses image composition in designs.
Common Misconceptions
"Any image will do." Some learners think image quality does not matter. Correct this by teaching the five criteria for good
images.
"Centring is the safest choice." Some learners centre everything because it feels balanced. Correct this by teaching the rule
of thirds.
"Visual flow is too advanced." Some learners think visual flow is an advanced concept. Correct this by showing them how
simple it is to trace.
Suggested Remedial Exercises
For learners who use low-quality images: Have them find high-quality alternatives on Unsplash or Pexels.
For learners who centre everything: Have them redesign a centred layout using the rule of thirds.
For learners who cannot trace visual flow: Have them practise with simple designs (e.g., a business card) before moving to
complex ones.
Differentiation Guidance
Level 1 — Supported (for learners who struggle):
Provide a "Rule of Thirds" template for reference.
Pair with a more confident learner for analysis exercises.
Allow extra time for the independent exercise.
Provide additional design examples for practice.
Level 2 — Independent (standard):
Complete all exercises as written.
Answer all knowledge check questions.
Complete the assignment to the standard shown in the rubric.
Level 3 — Challenge (for learners who finish early):
Ask the learner to find and analyse three more designs.
Ask the learner to create a "good version" and a "bad version" of the same design.
Ask the learner to teach the rule of thirds to a partner.
