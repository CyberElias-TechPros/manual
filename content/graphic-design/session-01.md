# Design Foundations Seeing

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define graphic design and explain its purpose in everyday life.
2. Identify the target audience of a design and explain why it matters.
3. Recognise and name the core design principles: hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance and white space.
4. Analyse a real design using correct design vocabulary.
5. Explain why some designs work and others do not.
6. Apply the "less is more" principle to evaluate design choices.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"The 5-Second Test"
Instructor Setup: Prepare 5 design examples — 3 good, 2 bad. Display each one for exactly 5 seconds.
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Welcome to the Graphic Design course. Before we learn any tools or techniques, I
want to train your eyes. I am going to show you five designs. Each one will appear for only 5 seconds. Your job is to decide: does
this design work, or does it not work? You do not need to explain why yet. Just notice your reaction.
- Show each design for 5 seconds.]
Now, which ones worked? [Pause for answers.] Which ones did not? [Pause.]
Here is the interesting thing: you did not need any design training to know which ones worked. Your eyes already know. The
difference between a good designer and a bad designer is that the good designer knows WHY it works — and can make it work
on purpose, every time.
That is what we are going to learn in this course. Not just how to make things look nice, but how to see why they look nice —
and how to fix them when they do not. Let us begin."
Expected Learner Response: Learners will correctly identify which designs work and which do not. This activates visual
intuition and sets the context for the session.

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: What Is Graphic Design? (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Graphic design is the art of organising visual elements — text, images, colours and shapes — to
communicate a message. It is everywhere. The sign on a shop. The flyer at a church. The Instagram post from your favourite
brand. The business card someone hands you. All of that is graphic design.
Why Does It Matter? Good design gets attention. Bad design gets ignored. A well-designed flyer makes people stop and read.
A poorly designed flyer makes people walk past. A professional business card makes people trust you. An unprofessional one
makes them doubt you.
Graphic Design vs Art: Art is about expression. Design is about communication. An artist creates something to express their
feelings. A designer creates something to communicate a message to an audience. In this course, we are learning to be
designers — communicators, not just decorators."
Topic 2: Design Purpose and Target Audience (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Every design has a purpose and an audience. Before you create any design, you must answer two questions:
1. What is the purpose? What do you want the viewer to do? Buy something? Attend an event? Remember a name? Call a
number?
2. Who is the audience? Who will see this design? Young people? Old people? Business owners? Church members? Students?
Why This Matters: A flyer for a nightclub looks very different from a flyer for a church event. A business card for a lawyer looks
different from a business card for a DJ. If you do not know your audience, you cannot make the right design choices.
Example: If you are designing a flyer for a children's birthday party, you would use bright colours, playful fonts and fun images.
If you are designing a flyer for a corporate seminar, you would use professional colours, clean fonts and formal images. Same
tool, different choices — because the audience is different."
Topic 3: Hierarchy — What You See First (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Hierarchy is the arrangement of elements in order of importance. It tells the viewer what to look at first,
second and third. Without hierarchy, everything looks equally important — and nothing stands out.
How to Create Hierarchy:
Size: Bigger elements are seen first. Make the most important text the largest.
Position: Elements at the top are seen first. Put the most important information at the top.
Colour: Bright or contrasting colours stand out. Use them for important information.
Weight: Bold text stands out more than regular text. Use bold for headings.
Example: On a business flyer, the hierarchy might be:
1. Event name (largest, boldest, most colourful)
2. Date and time (medium size, clear)
3. Location (smaller, but still readable)
4. Contact information (smallest, at the bottom)
Common Mistake: Making everything the same size. When everything is big, nothing is big. When everything is bold, nothing is
bold. Hierarchy requires contrast — some things must be bigger or bolder than others."
Topic 4: Contrast — Making Things Stand Out (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Contrast means difference. In design, contrast makes elements stand out from each other and from the
background. Without contrast, everything blends together and nothing is readable.
Types of Contrast:
Size contrast: Large text vs small text.
Colour contrast: Dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark background.
Weight contrast: Bold text vs regular text.
Font contrast: A decorative heading font vs a clean body font.
The Golden Rule of Contrast: If two elements are not the same, make them very different. Do not make them slightly different.
Slight differences look like mistakes. Big differences look intentional.
Example: Bad contrast: Dark grey text on a black background. Hard to read. Good contrast: White text on a black background.
Easy to read.
Bad contrast: Two fonts that look almost the same. Looks like a mistake. Good contrast: A bold serif heading with a clean sans-
serif body. Looks intentional."
Topic 5: Alignment — Everything Has a Place (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Alignment means lining up elements along a common edge or axis. When elements are aligned, the design
looks organised and professional. When elements are randomly placed, the design looks messy and amateur.
Types of Alignment:
Left align: All elements line up on the left edge. Most common for text.
Centre align: All elements line up on the centre axis. Good for titles and short text.
Right align: All elements line up on the right edge. Good for dates and numbers.
The Alignment Rule: Choose one alignment and stick with it. Do not mix left-aligned text with centre-aligned text on the same
design unless you have a specific reason.
Example: On a business card, all text is usually left-aligned or centre-aligned. Mixing alignments makes the card look
unprofessional."
Topic 6: Balance and White Space (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Balance means the visual weight of elements is distributed evenly. A design that is too heavy on one side
looks unstable. A design that is balanced looks calm and professional.
White Space: White space (also called negative space) is the empty space around and between elements. It is not wasted space
— it is essential space. White space gives the viewer's eye a place to rest. It makes the design easier to read and more elegant.
Common Mistake: Filling every inch of the design with text and images. This creates a cluttered, overwhelming design. The fix:
remove unnecessary elements and add more white space.
Example: Look at a professional website like Apple. Notice how much white space there is. The products stand out because
there is nothing competing for attention. That is the power of white space."

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Demonstration 1: Analysing a Good Design (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A well-designed flyer projected on the screen
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to analyse this flyer using the principles we just learned. Watch how I think."
1. Purpose: "The purpose of this flyer is to get people to attend a business seminar. The call to action is 'Register Now.'"
2. Audience: "The audience is business professionals. That is why the design is clean and formal."
3. Hierarchy: "The event name is the largest text — that is what you see first. The date and time are second. The location is
third. The contact information is smallest."
4. Contrast: "The heading is bold and dark blue. The body text is regular and black. The background is white. High contrast
makes it easy to read."
5. Alignment: "All text is left-aligned. Everything lines up on the left edge. This looks organised."
6. Balance and White Space: "The design is balanced — the text is on the left, the image is on the right. There is plenty of
white space around the text. It does not feel cluttered."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor systematically analysing a design using the six principles.
Expected Result: Learners can describe how each principle is applied in the example.
Demonstration 2: Analysing a Bad Design (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A poorly designed flyer projected on the screen
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Now let us look at a design that does not work. What is wrong with it?"
1. Hierarchy: "Everything is the same size. The event name is not bigger than the contact information. You do not know what
to look at first."
2. Contrast: "The text is light grey on a white background. It is hard to read. There is not enough contrast."
3. Alignment: "Some text is left-aligned, some is centre-aligned, some is right-aligned. Nothing lines up. It looks messy."
4. Balance and White Space: "The design is cluttered. There is text and images everywhere. There is no white space. It feels
overwhelming."
5. Purpose: "I cannot tell what the purpose is. Is it an event? A sale? A announcement? The message is unclear."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor identifying specific problems using design vocabulary.
Expected Result: Learners can identify specific problems in a poorly designed piece.
Demonstration 3: Fixing a Bad Design (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
The same poorly designed flyer, with annotations showing fixes
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Now let us fix this design. Here is what I would do:"
1. Fix hierarchy: "Make the event name the largest text. Make the date and time medium. Make the contact information
smallest."
2. Fix contrast: "Change the text colour to dark blue or black. Make the background white or light grey."
3. Fix alignment: "Choose one alignment — left align — and apply it to all text."
4. Fix balance and white space: "Remove unnecessary images. Add more white space around the text."
5. Fix purpose: "Make the call to action clear — 'Register Now' in a bold, colourful button."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor applying fixes based on the principles.
Expected Result: Learners can explain how to fix a poorly designed piece using design principles.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (20 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Exercise: "Design Autopsy"
Practical Objective: The learner will analyse three real designs using the design principles learned in this session.
Starting Materials:
Printed copies of three design examples (one good, one bad, one average)
"Design Analysis Worksheet" (one per learner)
Instructions:
Part 1: Analyse Design A (7 minutes)
1. "Look at Design A. This is a business flyer for a restaurant."
2. "Answer these questions on your worksheet:
What is the purpose of this design?
Who is the target audience?
Is there a clear hierarchy? What do you see first?
Is there enough contrast? Can you read the text easily?
Are the elements aligned? Do they line up?
Is the design balanced? Is there enough white space?
Overall, does this design work? Why or why not?"
Instructor Prompts:
"What is the first thing you see on this flyer?" (Answer: The restaurant name — it is the largest text.)
"Can you read all the text easily?" (Answer: Yes — dark text on a light background.)
"Does anything look out of place?" (Answer: No — everything is aligned and balanced.)
Part 2: Analyse Design B (7 minutes)
3. "Look at Design B. This is a social media post for a church event."
4. "Answer the same questions on your worksheet."
Instructor Prompts:
"What is the first thing you see?" (Answer: Everything is the same size — there is no clear hierarchy.)
"Can you read the text easily?" (Answer: No — some text is light grey on a white background.)
"Does anything look out of place?" (Answer: Yes — the text is not aligned, and there are too many colours.)
Part 3: Analyse Design C (6 minutes)
5. "Look at Design C. This is a business card for a hair salon."
6. "Answer the same questions on your worksheet."
Instructor Prompts:
"What is the first thing you see?" (Answer: The salon name — it is bold and centred.)
"Can you read all the text easily?" (Answer: Mostly yes, but the phone number is very small.)
"Does anything look out of place?" (Answer: The email address is left-aligned while everything else is centre-aligned.)
Expected Result: Every learner has completed a written analysis of three designs using correct design vocabulary.
Troubleshooting Guidance:
If a learner cannot identify hierarchy: "Look at the text sizes. Which one is biggest? That is the first thing you see."
If a learner cannot identify contrast: "Look at the text colour and the background colour. Is there enough difference?"
If a learner cannot identify alignment: "Draw an imaginary line along the left edge of the text. Do all the text blocks line up?"
Completion Checklist:
Design A analysed (purpose, audience, hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance, white space)
Design B analysed (same criteria)
Design C analysed (same criteria)
Overall assessment completed for each design

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Exercise: "Spot the Principle"
Scenario: "You are given six design examples. For each one, identify which design principle is being demonstrated (or violated)."
Task: Look at each design example and write down:
1. Which principle is most clearly demonstrated (or violated)?
2. Why?
Design Examples:
1. A flyer where the headline is huge and the body text is tiny. (Hierarchy)
2. A poster with white text on a white background. (Contrast — violated)
3. A business card where all text is perfectly left-aligned. (Alignment)
4. A social media post that is completely cluttered with no empty space. (White space — violated)
5. A flyer where the left side has lots of text and the right side has one small image. (Balance)
6. A poster with a bold serif heading and a clean sans-serif body. (Contrast)
Requirements:
Identify the principle for each example
Explain why in one sentence
Time Allowance: 10 minutes
Success Criteria:
All 6 examples correctly identified
Explanations are clear and accurate
Hints Available If the Learner Gets Stuck:
Hint 1: "Ask yourself: what is the first thing you see? That is hierarchy."
Hint 2: "Ask yourself: can I read this easily? That is contrast."
Hint 3: "Ask yourself: do the elements line up? That is alignment."
Complete Answer Key for the Instructor:
1. Hierarchy — The headline is much larger than the body text, creating a clear order of importance.
2. Contrast (violated) — White text on a white background has no contrast, making it unreadable.
3. Alignment — All text is left-aligned, creating a clean, organised appearance.
4. White space (violated) — The design is cluttered with no empty space, making it overwhelming.
5. Balance — The text on the left and the image on the right create visual balance.
6. Contrast — The bold serif heading contrasts with the clean sans-serif body, creating visual interest.

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "The Church Flyer That No One Read"
Situation: A church in Port Harcourt needs a flyer for their upcoming revival service. The pastor asks a member who "knows
computers" to design it. The member creates a flyer with 10 different fonts, 5 different colours, text everywhere and no clear
message. They print 500 copies and distribute them. Almost no one attends the revival.
The Problem: The flyer failed because it had no design principles. There was no hierarchy (everything was the same size), no
contrast (light text on a busy background), no alignment (text was randomly placed) and no white space (it was completely
cluttered).
Why It Matters: This happens every day — in churches, schools, businesses and events. People spend money printing designs
that do not work because they do not understand design principles.
Relevant Concept: All six design principles. If the designer had understood hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance and white
space, the flyer would have been effective.
Worked Solution: If the designer had taken this course, here is what they would do:
1. Purpose: Get people to attend the revival.
2. Audience: Church members and the local community.
3. Hierarchy: Make the event name the largest text. Make the date and time second. Make the location third.
4. Contrast: Use dark text on a light background. Use one accent colour for the event name.
5. Alignment: Left-align all text for a clean, organised look.
6. Balance and white space: Use one image (e.g., a cross or a dove) and plenty of white space around the text.
Reasoning: Good design is not about making things look fancy. It is about making things work. A flyer that communicates
clearly and looks professional will always outperform a flyer that is cluttered and confusing.
Possible Mistake: The designer might think that more colours and fonts make the design more attractive. In reality, simplicity is
more attractive and more effective.
Correction: Use a maximum of 2–3 colours and 2 fonts. Keep it simple. Let the message shine.
Transfer Exercise: "Your friend is designing a flyer for a birthday party. She wants to use 8 different fonts and every colour she
can find. What advice would you give her?"
Professional Connection: Every flyer, poster, social media post and business card you create will be judged by these principles.
Whether you are designing for a church, a school, a business or a personal event, the same principles apply.

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Not Knowing the Purpose
Symptoms: The learner creates a design without a clear message. Likely Cause: The learner did not think about the purpose
before starting. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "What is the purpose of this design? What do you want the viewer to do?"
Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to always define the purpose before starting any design. How to Confirm the
Problem Is Resolved: The learner defines the purpose before designing. How to Prevent Recurrence: Make "Define the
purpose" the first step in every design exercise.
Mistake 2: Making Everything the Same Size
Symptoms: The learner creates a design where all text is the same size. Likely Cause: The learner does not understand hierarchy.
How to Investigate: Look at the design. Are there different text sizes? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to make the
most important text the largest and the least important text the smallest. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The
learner creates clear hierarchy. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "If everything is big, nothing is big."
Mistake 3: Using Too Many Colours
Symptoms: The learner uses 5 or more colours in one design. Likely Cause: The learner thinks more colours make the design
more attractive. How to Investigate: Count the colours in the design. Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to use a
maximum of 2–3 colours. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses 2–3 colours. How to Prevent
Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Less is more."
Mistake 4: Not Leaving White Space
Symptoms: The learner fills every inch of the design with text and images. Likely Cause: The learner thinks empty space is
wasted space. How to Investigate: Look at the design. Is there any empty space? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner that
white space is essential for readability and elegance. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner leaves white space.
How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "White space is your friend."
Mistake 5: Mixing Alignments
Symptoms: The learner uses left, centre and right alignment in the same design. Likely Cause: The learner does not understand
the importance of consistent alignment. How to Investigate: Look at the design. Do the text blocks line up? Safe Corrective
Steps: Teach the learner to choose one alignment and stick with it. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses
consistent alignment. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "Choose one alignment and stick with it."

## M. KNOWLEDGE CHECK (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
Questions
1. Multiple Choice: What is graphic design? a) The art of drawing and painting b) The art of organising visual elements to
communicate a message c) The art of writing code d) The art of taking photographs
Answer: b) The art of organising visual elements to communicate a message
2. Short Answer: What are the six core design principles we learned today?
Answer: Hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance, white space, and purpose/audience.
3. Multiple Choice: What is hierarchy in design? a) The arrangement of elements in order of importance b) The use of different
colours c) The size of the design d) The number of images used
Answer: a) The arrangement of elements in order of importance
4. Explain in Your Own Words: Why is white space important in design?
Answer: White space gives the viewer's eye a place to rest. It makes the design easier to read and more elegant. Without white
space, a design looks cluttered and overwhelming.
5. Practical Identification: Look at this design example. Identify the hierarchy — what do you see first, second and third?
Answer: The learner correctly identifies the order of visual importance.
6. Scenario-Based: Your friend creates a flyer where all the text is the same size and all the same colour. What design principle is
being violated, and how would you fix it?
Answer: Hierarchy is being violated. To fix it, make the most important text (e.g., the event name) the largest and boldest. Make
the less important text (e.g., contact information) smaller. Use different colours or weights to create contrast.
7. Error-Detecting: A learner writes in their analysis: "The design is bad because it is ugly." What is wrong with this statement,
and what should they write instead?
Answer: "Ugly" is a subjective opinion, not a design analysis. The learner should identify specific problems using design
principles, such as: "The design has poor hierarchy because all text is the same size. The contrast is low because the text is light
grey on a white background. The alignment is inconsistent."

## N. REFLECTION AND RECAP (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English Summary
Today you learned the foundations of graphic design. You learned what graphic design is, why it matters, and how to analyse
designs using six core principles: hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance, white space and purpose/audience. You also learned
to identify what works and what does not in real designs. These are the skills that separate a designer from someone who just
makes things "look nice."
Five Important Takeaways
1. Graphic design is about communication, not just decoration.
2. Every design has a purpose and a target audience.
3. Hierarchy tells the viewer what to see first.
4. Contrast makes elements stand out.
5. White space is essential — it is not wasted space.
Questions That Help You Recognise What You Can Now Do
Can you define graphic design?
Can you identify the purpose and audience of a design?
Can you recognise hierarchy, contrast, alignment, balance and white space?
Can you analyse a design using correct vocabulary?
Can you explain why a design works or does not work?
Connection to the Next Session
In the next session, you will learn about colour and typography — two of the most powerful tools in a designer's toolkit. You
will learn how to choose colours that work together and fonts that read well. You now know how to see design. Next, you will
learn to choose the right colours and fonts.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes

Observable Signs of Competence
The learner defines graphic design correctly.
The learner identifies purpose and audience.
The learner recognises and names all six design principles.
The learner analyses designs using correct vocabulary.
The learner explains why designs work or do not work.
Common Misconceptions
"Design is just about making things look pretty." Some learners think design is purely aesthetic. Correct this by explaining
that design is about communication.
"More colours and fonts make a better design." Some learners think more is better. Correct this by teaching the "less is
more" principle.
"White space is wasted space." Some learners think empty space should be filled. Correct this by explaining the
importance of white space.
Suggested Remedial Exercises
For learners who cannot identify hierarchy: Show them two designs side by side — one with clear hierarchy and one
without. Ask them to compare.
For learners who cannot identify contrast: Show them a design with good contrast and one with poor contrast. Ask them to
explain the difference.
For learners who cannot analyse designs: Provide a structured analysis template with guiding questions.
Differentiation Guidance
Level 1 — Supported (for learners who struggle):
Provide a "Design Principles Quick Reference" card.
Pair with a more confident learner for analysis exercises.
Allow extra time for the independent exercise.
Provide additional design examples for practice.
Level 2 — Independent (standard):
Complete all exercises as written.
Answer all knowledge check questions.
Complete the assignment to the standard shown in the rubric.
Level 3 — Challenge (for learners who finish early):
Ask the learner to find and analyse three more designs.
Ask the learner to create a "good version" and a "bad version" of the same design.
Ask the learner to teach the six principles to a partner.
