# Assessment, Feedback and Learner Support

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Deliver and Grow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (assessment design and feedback writing — no prior assessment experience required)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 4 completed (or the ability to host a live class). A course idea with outcomes and at least one lesson. A free Google account (for Forms and Classroom). No prior quiz-design or grading experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 you ran a live class. Today you prove that learning happened: you build a quiz, set up an assignment, mark with a rubric, write feedback that helps, and support learners who struggle. You also take the ethics check.

**Preparation before class:** Confirm every learner can access Google Forms and Google Classroom. Prepare a projector with a sample quiz (Section G) and a sample rubric. Print the feedback sentence frames (Section F.5), the rubric template (Section F.4) and the ethics check questions (Section F.8). Have two struggling-learner scenarios ready (Section I). Read the Course Design Document Sections 5 (LO6, LO7) and 15 before class. Instructor note on tone: This session has two halves. The first half (quiz, assignment, rubric, feedback) is practical and concrete — learners build real artefacts. The second half (support, community, ethics) is about care and judgment — learners make decisions about real people. Keep the first half brisk and hands-on; slow down for the second half, where the ethics check and the support scenarios need thought and discussion. The message: assessment is not a weapon — it is a mirror that helps the learner see their progress.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Build an auto-graded quiz in Google Forms with an answer key, matching question types to outcomes.
2. Set up an assignment in Google Classroom and mark it with a simple rubric so grading is fair and quick.
3. Write feedback that is specific, kind and ends in a clear next step — never just "well done."
4. Choose an appropriate support step for two struggling-learner scenarios, respecting dignity and privacy.
5. Pass the ethics check (at least 70%): state the minimum personal data a course needs, how to store and share it safely, and
classify copyright scenarios correctly.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Project two pieces of feedback on the screen. Do not label them yet.
Feedback A: "Good job. Well done. Keep it up."
Feedback B: "Your introduction is clear and your measurements are accurate. Next, add one worked example of how you
calculated the waist measurement — that will make it easier for a beginner to follow."
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. On the screen are two pieces of feedback a teacher might give a learner. Read them both. Now — which
one helps the learner more? … The second one, obviously. Why?"
Let the class call out differences. Then reveal:
"Feedback A is polite but useless — it tells the learner nothing about what they did well or what to do next. Feedback B is
specific (it names what was good), kind (it is encouraging, not harsh) and it ends in a clear next step (add one worked
example). That is the difference between feedback that makes a learner feel good and feedback that makes a learner
better. Today you learn to write feedback that does both."
Then ask the second question:
"Now — how do you know if a learner actually learned? Raise your hand if you have ever taught something, then
wondered later: 'Did they get it?' … That wonder is the problem assessment solves. A quiz, an assignment, a rubric —
these are not punishments. They are mirrors that show the learner — and you — what was learned and what was not.
Today you learn to build those mirrors."
Expected learner response: Learners will say feedback B "tells you what to do," "it is specific," "it is helpful." Accept all answers
and draw out the three parts: specific, kind, next step. For the second question, they will nod — everyone has wondered "did
they get it?"
Why this opener works: It uses a contrast learners can feel (useless vs useful feedback) and a universal experience (wondering if
they learned) to frame the whole session: assessment and feedback are tools for the learner's benefit, not the teacher's power.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a learning outcome? Give one example from your own course."
Expected answer: A sentence saying what the learner will be able to do — e.g., "Take three body measurements and record
them accurately."
Common gap: vague verbs. Clarify: "Check your outcome — action verb? If it says 'understand,' rewrite it."
Question 2: "What participation tools did you use in your micro-teach?"
Expected answer: A poll, a chat prompt, a show of hands, a breakout task, the whiteboard.
Common gap: some learners used only one tool. Clarify: "Today's quiz is another participation tool — but this one marks
the learning."
Question 3: "What is a rubric? Have you ever used one?"
Expected answer: A table that says what good work looks like, with levels and marks.
Common gap: many learners have never seen a rubric. Clarify: "A rubric is a scoring guide — it makes grading fair and fast.
You will build one today."
Question 4: "What is the difference between a quiz and an assignment?"
Expected answer: A quiz is a quick check (often auto-graded); an assignment is a larger task the learner produces and
submits.
Common gap: some learners think they are the same. Clarify: "A quiz checks knowledge quickly; an assignment checks a
bigger piece of work. You build both today."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You taught a class. Now you prove the learning. A quiz checks knowledge fast. An assignment checks a bigger task. A
rubric makes your marking fair. Feedback tells the learner what to do next. And support catches the learner who is falling
behind — before they disappear. Today you build all of it, and you take the ethics check that keeps it all honest."
If more than a third of the class cannot name a learning outcome, spend 5 extra minutes writing one outcome on the board
together before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why assessment matters online
The idea. In a classroom, you can see if learners are confused — you read their faces. Online, you cannot. Assessment is how
you see the learning: a quiz, an assignment, a rubric. Without it, you are teaching into the dark.
Why it matters: For the learner, assessment shows them what they know and what they do not — a mirror, not a weapon. For
you, it shows what worked and what did not — so you can fix your teaching. For the course, it is the evidence that the course
works.

F.3 Assignments in Google Classroom and the simple rubric
The idea. An assignment is a larger task the learner produces and submits — a worksheet, a photo, a short essay, a project.
Google Classroom is where you post it, learners submit it, and you mark it. A rubric is the scoring guide that makes your
marking fair and fast.
Setting up an assignment in Google Classroom (the steps):
1. Open Google Classroom and go to your class.
2. Click "Classwork" → "Create" → "Assignment."
3. Write the title and instructions: "Submit your completed measurement chart."
4. Attach the worksheet (a Google Doc) — learners get their own copy.
5. Set a due date and a points value (e.g., 10 points).
6. Add a rubric (optional but recommended — see below).
7. Click "Assign." Learners see it, complete it and submit.
The simple rubric — what it is and why it matters: A rubric is a table with criteria (what you are marking) and levels (how
well they did). It makes grading fair (everyone is marked against the same standard) and fast (you tick boxes instead of writing
long comments for every learner).
Worked example — Ngozi's rubric for the measurement chart (6 marks):
Criterion Excellent (2) Good (1) Needs work (0)
All three measurements taken Bust, waist and hip all present Two present One or none present
Measurements recorded accurately All in the right range and unit Mostly accurate Several errors
Chart completed and submitted Complete, legible, on time Complete but late or messy Incomplete
Total /6
Say: "Four criteria. Three levels each. A learner can score 0, 1 or 2 on each — total out of 6. When you mark, you tick the level
for each criterion and add the marks. It takes 30 seconds per learner. And the learner sees exactly why they got their mark — no
mystery, no argument."
Why rubrics beat a single grade: A single grade ("7/10") tells the learner nothing. A rubric tells them what was good and what
to improve. It is feedback built into the marking.
F.4 Feedback that helps — specific, kind, with a next step
The idea. Feedback is the most powerful tool in teaching — and the most misused. "Well done" feels nice but changes
nothing. Good feedback is specific (names what was good or what is wrong), kind (encouraging, not harsh) and ends in a clear
next step (what to do next).
The three parts of good feedback:
What it does Weak Strong
Specific Names the exact thing "Good job." "Your introduction is clear and your measurements are
accurate."
Kind Encourages, does not
shame
"This is
wrong."
"You are close — let's fix one thing."
What it does Weak Strong

The feedback sentence frames (use these):
"You did [specific thing] well — I noticed [detail]."
"One thing to improve: [specific issue]."
"Your next step is: [clear action]."
"Compare your work to the rubric — you are strong in [criterion] and developing in [criterion]."
Worked example — weak vs strong feedback:
Weak: "Good effort. Well done."
Strong: "Your measurement chart is complete and your bust measurement is accurate — well done. One thing to improve:
your waist measurement is 5cm smaller than your bust, which is unusual — please re-measure and check you are
wrapping the tape snug, not tight. Your next step: re-measure your waist and resubmit the chart by Friday."
Why the strong version works: It names what is good (complete, bust accurate), names the specific issue (waist 5cm smaller),
explains the likely cause (tape too tight) and gives a clear next step (re-measure and resubmit by Friday). The learner knows
exactly what to do.
The rule: feedback goes to the individual, not the group.
Say this to the class:
"Feedback is a gift, not a verdict. A gift says: 'Here is what I see, here is what is good, and here is what to do next.' A verdict
says: 'You failed.' One helps the learner grow. The other makes them hide. Always choose the gift."
F.5 Certificates — nice, but honest
The idea. A certificate is a nice way to celebrate completion — but its wording must be honest. A certificate that says
"Qualified Online Teacher" when the learner completed a two-week beginner course is a lie, and lies destroy trust.
The honest certificate wording:
Say: "Completed a 6-session online teaching course at Cyber Elias Academy, covering course design, content creation, live
delivery and assessment."
Do NOT say: "Certified Online Teacher," "Qualified Instructor," "Accredited Educator" — these imply a formal qualification
this course does not provide.
Canva certificate (free): Canva has free certificate templates. Use one, add the learner's name, the course title and the honest
wording. It takes five minutes and feels like a real achievement — because it is.
Say this to the class:
"A certificate is a celebration, not a credential. Celebrate what the learner actually did — and say exactly that. An honest
certificate is worth more than a fancy lie."
F.6 Supporting struggling learners — spot them, reach them, keep their dignity
The idea. Online, struggling learners do not raise their hand — they go quiet. They stop submitting, stop attending, stop
replying. Your job is to spot them early and reach out — privately, kindly, with a concrete step.
How to spot a struggling learner: They miss two sessions in a row. They stop submitting assignments. They go quiet in the
chat. Their quiz scores drop.
The support steps (in order):
1. A private message. "Hello [name] — I noticed you missed the last session. Is everything okay? I am here if you need help."
(Private, kind, no pressure.)
2. Extra time. Offer an extension — no penalty, no shame.
3. An alternative task. Offer a simpler version that still meets the outcome.
4. A catch-up path. Send the recording, the slides and a short summary — so they can catch up without humiliation.
5. A referral. If the problem is beyond your skill, refer them to someone who can help.
The dignity rule: Support is private. Never announce in the group: "Some of you are behind." Never compare learners. Support
is a hand up, not a spotlight.
Worked example — two scenarios:
Scenario 1: Adaeze missed two live sessions and has not submitted the measurement chart. You message her privately:
"Hello Adaeze — I missed you at the last two sessions. Is everything okay? If the chart is tricky, I can send you a 5-minute
recording of me doing it. No pressure — just let me know how I can help." She replies: her phone broke and she could not
afford data. You offer: an extension, a downloadable recording, and a catch-up call on WhatsApp audio (cheaper than
video).
Scenario 2: Ibrahim submitted the chart but his measurements are all wrong. You message him privately: "Thank you for
submitting your chart, Ibrahim. I can see you tried hard. One thing to fix: your waist measurement is very tight — the tape
should be snug, not tight. Please re-measure and resubmit by Friday. Here is a 1-minute clip showing the right tension.
You can do this." He resubmits — correct.
Say this to the class:
"Support is not about lowering standards — it is about removing barriers. The standard stays the same; the path to it
gets clearer. And it is always private."
F.7 The learner community — a small, moderated group with norms
The idea. A course is more than lessons — it is a community. A small, moderated group (WhatsApp or Telegram) where
learners ask questions, share progress and support each other. But a community without norms becomes a chaos of spam and
distraction.
The community norms (set them at the start):
1. Purpose: This group is for course questions and support — not for politics, spam or arguments.
2. Respect: We are kind. We do not mock questions — there are no silly questions.
3. Privacy: We do not share anyone's personal information, scores or work outside the group.
4. Response time: The instructor replies within 24 hours; learners help each other sooner.
5. No spam: No forwarded messages, no "good morning" images, no chain posts.
The moderation rule: As the instructor, you are the moderator. You welcome new members, answer questions, gently enforce
the norms, and remove spam. A well-moderated group is a powerful learning tool; an unmoderated one is a distraction.
Say this to the class:
"A community is like a garden. With norms and a gardener, it grows. Without them, it becomes weeds. Set the norms on
day one, and tend the garden every week."
F.8 The ethics check — minimum data, safe storage, consent, minors
The idea. Teaching is a position of trust. People give you their attention, their money, sometimes their children, and often their
personal information. The ethics check makes sure you handle that trust properly. This is not optional — it is a condition of the
course.
1. Collect the minimum data. Ask for only what a course genuinely needs — usually a name and an email. Do not collect
home addresses, bank details, BVN, NIN or unrelated documents for a short course.
2. Store and share data carefully. Keep learner lists, grades and recordings in a controlled place — not a public post or an
open group. Never publish a learner's phone number, email, results, photo or video without explicit consent. Feedback goes
to the individual, not the group.
3. Get consent before recording or publishing. Tell everyone in a live session it will be recorded, offer an alternative to those
who object, and prefer "unlisted" to "public." Never record anyone secretly.
4. Special care with minors. For learners under 18, obtain consent from a parent or guardian before enrolment and before any
recording or image is used. Never message a minor privately one-to-one without a parent or guardian knowing — keep
communication in a group or copied to a guardian. Never request images, never use images of children without written
permission, and never share a child's full name, school or location.
5. Respect copyright. Use only material you made, material free to use, or material you have permission for. Follow Creative
Commons conditions and cite your sources. Never copy another teacher's slides, outline, worksheet, photos, music or
video.
The ethics check questions (for the independent exercise):
1. What is the minimum personal data a short course needs?
2. Where should you keep learner grades and recordings?
3. What must you do before recording a live class?
4. What is the rule for messaging a minor one-to-one?
5. A learner sends you a photo of their work to use in your marketing. What do you need before you can use it?
Say this to the class:
"The rule is simple: collect less, protect more, ask permission, credit the owner, tell the truth."
F.9 Summary of the lecture
Assessment is how you see the learning — a quiz for quick checks, an assignment for bigger tasks.
Google Forms builds auto-graded quizzes — match the question type to the outcome, write a clear answer key.
Google Classroom hosts assignments — post, collect, mark.
A rubric makes marking fair and fast — criteria, levels, ticks.
Feedback is specific, kind and ends in a next step — never just "well done."
Certificates are nice but honest — say exactly what the learner completed.
Support is private and dignified — spot the quiet learner, reach out, remove barriers.
Community needs norms — a moderated group is a garden, not a chaos.
Ethics: collect less, protect more, ask permission, credit the owner, tell the truth.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama J runs a JAMB mathematics class in Port Harcourt. She moved her class online when her students could not
attend in person. She recorded her lessons, set up a WhatsApp group, and told the students: "Watch the videos and do the
exercises." After three weeks, she gave her first online quiz — 10 questions typed into a Word document and posted in the
group. The results were a disaster. Only 3 of 20 students scored above 50%. She was confused: "I taught everything. Why did
they fail?"
Relevant facts.
Mama J is a strong teacher — her in-person students usually score well.
Her quiz was a Word document posted in the group — students typed answers in the chat or sent photos.
The quiz had no answer key, no rubric and no auto-grading — she marked 20 handwritten photos by hand, taking two
evenings.
She had not checked whether the quiz matched her lessons — she tested some things she never taught.
One student, Funmi, scored 20% and stopped attending. Mama J posted in the group: "Some of you need to try harder.
Funmi, you are a disgrace to this class." Funmi left the group.
Decision point. Mama J's online assessment is failing — for her students and for her. What should she change?
Possible approaches.
1. Blame the students — "They are lazy. Online students are not serious."
2. Make the quiz harder — "Maybe they will take it seriously if it is tougher."
3. Redesign the assessment — match the quiz to the lessons, use auto-grading, add a rubric, give private feedback and
support Funmi.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. The students are not lazy — the assessment is broken. A quiz that tests things never
taught is a test of luck, not of the students. A harder quiz would fail more students and still test the wrong things. Every problem
has a simple, free fix.
The redesign (what Mama J did):
Matched the quiz to the lessons: She wrote one quiz question per outcome — only testing what she had taught.
Moved to Google Forms: She built the quiz with auto-grading — multiple choice and short answer for the facts, one
paragraph question for the explanation. The tool marked the easy questions; she marked only the paragraph.
Added a rubric: For the paragraph question, she wrote a simple rubric — correct method (2), clear explanation (2), correct
answer (1). Marking took 10 minutes instead of two evenings.
Gave private feedback: Instead of posting scores in the group, she messaged each student privately: "You scored 6/10. You
are strong in solving equations — well done. One thing to fix: the quadratic formula. Watch the 3-minute clip I am sending
and try the two practice questions. You can do this."
Supported Funmi: She messaged Funmi privately: "Hello Funmi — I noticed you found the last quiz hard. I am sorry for
my message in the group — that was wrong, and I apologise. Would you like a short catch-up call this week? We can go
over the parts that are tricky. No pressure — just help." Funmi accepted, had the call, and her next score was 65%.
The result: The class average rose from 42% to 71% in three weeks. Funmi stayed. Mama J's marking time fell from two
evenings to 30 minutes. And she learned the most important lesson: assessment is a mirror, not a weapon — and feedback is
a gift, not a verdict.
Trade-offs. The redesign took one evening — the investment this session asks of every learner. She had to apologise to Funmi
— and that took courage, but it saved a learner. The first redesigned quiz was not perfect; she adjusted it after seeing the
results. That is normal — assessment improves with use.
Professional connection. This is the pattern behind every fair, effective assessment: match the test to the teaching, use tools
that save time, mark with a rubric, give private feedback, and support the struggling learner with dignity.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 A quiz that tests things never taught
Symptoms: Learners fail despite attending; the teacher is confused. Likely cause: The quiz was written from the subject, not
from the lessons — it tests things that were never taught. How to investigate: For each quiz question, ask: "Which lesson
taught this?" If the answer is "none," the question is unfair. Corrective steps: Rewrite the quiz so every question maps to a
lesson and an outcome. Test only what you taught. How to confirm resolved: Every question traces to a lesson. Prevention:
Build the quiz from the curriculum map — one question per outcome.
K.2 All multiple-choice questions
Symptoms: The quiz can only test facts — not skills or explanations. Likely cause: The learner defaulted to multiple choice
because it is easiest. How to investigate: Look at the outcomes — do any need an explanation or a skill? If so, multiple choice
cannot test them. Corrective steps: Match the type to the outcome — paragraph for explanations, short answer for specific
answers, linear scale for self-assessment. How to confirm resolved: Each question type matches its outcome. Prevention:
Always ask: "What is the outcome? Does this question type test it?"
K.3 No answer key (or a harsh one)
Symptoms: Learners are marked wrong for acceptable answers; marking is slow. Likely cause: The answer key is missing or too
strict — "bust" is correct but "Bust" is marked wrong. How to investigate: Check the answer key — are acceptable
alternatives included? Corrective steps: Write the answer key with acceptable alternatives. For short answers, accept spelling
variations. How to confirm resolved: A learner with a correct-but-differently-worded answer is marked correct. Prevention:
Write the answer key before the quiz, not after.
K.4 Vague feedback ("good job")
Symptoms: Learners do not know what to improve; they repeat the same mistakes. Likely cause: The feedback is polite but
empty. How to investigate: Read the feedback — does it name a specific thing and a next step? If not, it is vague. Corrective
steps: Use the frames: "You did [specific] well… One thing to improve: [issue]… Your next step is: [action]." How to confirm
resolved: The learner can state what to do next. Prevention: Never write feedback without a specific and a next step.
K.5 Public posting of scores or feedback
Symptoms: Learners feel shamed; trust breaks; learners leave the group. Likely cause: The instructor posted scores or critical
feedback in the group. How to investigate: Is any learner's score or feedback visible to the group? If so, it is a breach.
Corrective steps: Remove the post. Apologise. Move all feedback to private messages. How to confirm resolved: No learner's
data is visible to the group. Prevention: Feedback is private — always. The rule: praise publicly, correct privately.
K.6 Ignoring the quiet learner
Symptoms: A learner stops attending and submitting; the instructor notices too late. Likely cause: The instructor did not spot
the early signs — missed sessions, dropped scores, silence. How to investigate: Check attendance and submission records —
who has gone quiet? Corrective steps: Reach out privately and kindly. Offer support. Remove barriers. How to confirm
resolved: The learner responds and re-engages. Prevention: Check your records weekly — spot the quiet learner before they
disappear.
K.7 Over-collecting learner data
Symptoms: The course asks for BVN, NIN, home addresses or bank details. Likely cause: The instructor copied a form from
somewhere without thinking. How to investigate: Look at your enrolment form — what do you actually need? If it is more
than a name and an email, cut it. Corrective steps: Collect the minimum — a name and an email. Delete any unnecessary data
you already collected. How to confirm resolved: Your form asks for only what the course needs. Prevention: The rule: collect
less, protect more.
K.8 Dishonest certificate wording
Symptoms: A certificate says "Certified Online Teacher" for a two-week beginner course. Likely cause: The instructor wanted
the certificate to sound impressive. How to investigate: Read the certificate — does it say exactly what the learner completed?
If it over-claims, it is dishonest. Corrective steps: Rewrite the certificate honestly: "Completed a 6-session online teaching
course at Cyber Elias Academy." How to confirm resolved: The certificate states exactly what was done — no more.
Prevention: An honest certificate is worth more than a fancy lie.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — for example, the learner can
actually point to a quiz question that maps to no lesson, or actually show feedback with no next step. Teach learners to confirm
before concluding.
