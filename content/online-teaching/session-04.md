# Running Live Classes and Engagement

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Deliver and Grow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (hosting and engagement — no prior hosting experience required)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 3 completed (or the ability to share a screen and use chat). A course idea with at least one piece of content (slides or a worksheet). A free Zoom or Google Meet account. No prior hosting experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–3 you designed and built your course. Today you run it — as the host of a live class. You will learn the host's toolkit, the engagement rhythm, classroom management and troubleshooting, and you will deliver a 10-minute micro-teach.

**Preparation before class:** Confirm every learner can host a free Zoom or Google Meet call (test the host controls). Prepare a projector with the host's toolkit diagram (Section F.1). Print the engagement plan template (Section G) and the troubleshooting checklist (Section F.4). Prepare three technical-failure scenario cards (Section I). Set up a sign-up sheet for the 10-minute micro-teaches. Read the Course Design Document Sections 5 (LO5) and 8 before class. Instructor note on tone: This is the "performance" session — the most nervous-making of the course. Many learners have only ever been participants on calls; today they become hosts. Normalise the nerves ("every good host was once a first-time host"), model calm troubleshooting, and make the micro-teach a safe, supported experience. The goal is not a perfect lesson — it is a hosted lesson with at least two moments of real participation.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Host a short live class on Zoom or Google Meet as the host — using screen share, chat, and at least two participation tools
(poll, hand raise, whiteboard or breakout rooms).
2. State three online classroom norms and manage latecomers and disruption calmly and fairly.
3. Design an engagement rhythm with something asked or done at least every eight minutes — a poll, a chat prompt, a
show of hands or a breakout pair task.
4. Diagnose and respond correctly to at least three common technical failures using a checklist (sound → camera → screen
share → connection) and a written fallback plan.
5. Deliver a 10-minute micro-teach using at least two participation tools, with a clear opening, content, interaction and a
closing check for understanding.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner (with the Zoom or Google Meet app installed) or an academy laptop. Either is enough.
Earphones with a microphone (the bundled pair is fine).
Instructor device connected to a projector or large screen.
A stable internet connection — academy Wi-Fi, with a mobile-data fallback plan.
Software (all free)
Zoom (free tier, host account) or Google Meet (free with a Google account) — the live class tool.
Canva or Google Slides — for the slides the learner will share.
Google Docs — for the engagement plan and the fallback plan.
Sample files
Host's toolkit diagram (Section F.1) — projected and printed.
Engagement plan template (Section G) — one per learner, printed.
Troubleshooting checklist (Section F.4) — one per learner, printed.
Three technical-failure scenario cards (Section I) — for the independent exercise.
Micro-teach sign-up sheet — for the 10-minute deliveries.
Worksheets
Worksheet 4.1 — "My Engagement Plan" (time / what happens / participation tool / what the learner does).
Worksheet 4.2 — "My Fallback Plan" (if X fails, I will Y).
Accounts
A free Zoom host account or a Google account for Meet. (Created in Session 1; create in class if needed.)
Internet
Academy Wi-Fi in class. Have a mobile-data fallback ready (a hotspot or a second connection) in case the Wi-Fi fails —
this is also the troubleshooting lesson.
Alternative for learners who lack a facility
Any phone with the Zoom or Meet app works. A learner with only a phone can host, share the screen (slides work on a
phone), use chat and run a poll. Breakout rooms are easier on a laptop but possible on a phone.
Safety note: Recording a live class requires telling everyone and offering an alternative to those who object. Never record
secretly. No learner's personal data, and no images of minors, without written consent. Follow the academy's Ethics rules:
collect less, protect more, ask permission, credit the owner, tell the truth.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Start a mock live class on Zoom or Meet. Admit the learners (or project the screen). Share your screen with a
simple slide. Begin talking — and keep talking for three full minutes. Do not ask a single question. Do not use the chat. Do not
use a poll. Just talk.
After three minutes, stop and ask:
"How many of you are still paying attention? Be honest. … How many of you checked your phone, thought about dinner,
or started reading a message? … That is what I thought. Now — what could I have done in those three minutes to keep
you with me?"
Let the class call out ideas. Then reveal:
"You just lived the number one mistake in online teaching: a host who talks and talks and never asks anyone to do
something. In a classroom, you can see the room — you notice when eyes glaze. Online, silence is invisible. A learner can
be asleep, cooking, or on another app, and you would never know. So you must design the moments where they do
something — a poll, a chat question, a show of hands, a breakout task. Today you learn to design those moments, and
you learn to run them as the host."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "ask us questions," "use a poll," "make it interesting," "shorter." Accept all answers
and draw out the rule: something asked or done at least every eight minutes.
Why this opener works: It makes the learners feel the problem — their own attention drift — before any theory. They will
remember the feeling, and they will remember the fix.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What makes online teaching different from a classroom? Name two things."
Expected answer: Shorter attention; distraction at home; mobile-first learners; screen fatigue; participation must be
designed; data costs.
Common gap: learners recite the list but cannot apply it. Clarify: "Keep those in mind — today you design for them."
Question 2: "What are the five parts of a lesson?"
Expected answer: Opener, input, practice, check for understanding, exit task.
Common gap: none serious; accept all reasonable answers.
Question 3: "What did you build in Session 3? Name your artefacts."
Expected answer: A three-slide deck, a 60–90 second recording, a worksheet, a copyright checklist.
Common gap: some learners did not finish the recording. Clarify: "If your recording is not done, finish it before the micro-
teach — you will teach with your content today."
Question 4: "What is your primary mode, and what free tools did you choose?"
Expected answer: Each learner names their mode and tools.
Common gap: learners who chose live mode but have never hosted. Clarify: "Today you become the host. That is the
point."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have the content. Now you run the class. Today you learn the host's toolkit — screen share, chat, polls, hand raise,
breakout rooms, waiting room, recording. You learn the engagement rhythm — something every eight minutes. You
learn classroom management — norms, latecomers, disruption. You learn troubleshooting — a calm checklist for
when technology fails. And you deliver a 10-minute micro-teach — your first real online lesson. By the end of today, you
will have taught."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five parts of a lesson, spend 5 extra minutes writing them on the board with a
one-line job for each before continuing.

Screen share — the classic mistake: The most common hosting error is sharing the wrong window. You mean to share your
slides, but you share your entire screen — and the learners see your WhatsApp messages, your email, your browser tabs. The
fix: share the specific window or application, not the whole screen — and check what is on your screen before you share.
Close anything you do not want the class to see.
Breakout rooms — the task rule: A breakout room without a task is just a waiting room. Always give a clear task: "In pairs,
discuss: what is the first step in taking your measurements? You have three minutes. Come back with one answer." No task, no
breakout.
Say this to the class:
"The toolkit is not hard — but it is easy to use badly. The golden rules: mute all at the start, share the window not the
screen, never ignore the chat, always give breakout rooms a task, and always tell people before you record. Master those
and you are already a better host than most."
F.2 Engagement — the eight-minute rhythm
The idea. Online attention is short and invisible. If you do not design participation, it will not happen — learners will mute,
vanish and never come back. The rule: something asked or done at least every eight minutes.
Why eight minutes? In a classroom, a teacher can read the room. Online, you cannot. Eight minutes is roughly the attention
ceiling for a passive online learner. After eight minutes of listening, the learner's mind is on their phone, their children, their
dinner. A well-timed poll or chat question resets their attention.
The four types of participation moment:
1. A poll. "Quick check: which of these is a measurement — bust, fabric, or zip?" Instant, anonymous, everyone can answer.
2. A chat prompt. "Type one word in the chat: what is the hardest part of sewing for you?" Low-pressure, everyone can type.
3. A show of hands. "Hands up if you have ever sewn anything." Visual, quick, no typing needed.
4. A breakout pair task. "In pairs, in breakout rooms, discuss: what is the first step? Three minutes. Come back with one
answer." Deeper, but takes longer — use sparingly.

3–11 Input: a short demo Chat prompt: "Type one word: what do you think of when I say

21–26 Check: a quick quiz Poll: "Which is the correct way to hold the tape? A, B or C."

Say: "Something every eight minutes. Five moments in 30 minutes. That is the rhythm. A lesson with one poll at the start and
nothing else is not a lesson — it is a lecture with a pulse check."
Real-life comparison. Engagement is like music. A song with one long note is boring. A song with a rhythm — verse, chorus,
verse, chorus — holds you. Your lesson needs a rhythm too: teach, ask, teach, ask. The "ask" is the chorus. Without it, you are
just holding one long note.
Common misconception. "Engagement means being entertaining." No — it means being participatory. You do not need
jokes and games. You need moments where the learner does something. A poll is not entertainment; it is a design tool that
keeps the learner in the lesson.
F.3 Classroom management — norms, latecomers, disruption
The idea. Online classroom management is not about control — it is about clarity and fairness. When everyone knows the
rules, the class runs itself. When nobody does, the host is constantly firefighting.
The three norms to state at the start:
1. Mute when you are not speaking. "Please mute yourself when you are not talking — it helps everyone hear. Unmute
when you want to speak."
2. How to ask questions. "To ask a question, raise your hand (the hand icon) or type in the chat. I will check the chat every
few minutes."
3. Respect everyone's turn. "We listen when others speak. We do not interrupt. Everyone gets a turn."
Why norms matter: In a classroom, norms are implicit — everyone grew up in classrooms. Online, nothing is implicit. A
learner who does not know they should mute will sit in a noisy room for 30 minutes. A learner who does not know how to ask
will stay confused. Norms are not bureaucracy — they are freedom from confusion.
Managing latecomers — the waiting room:
Turn on the waiting room before the class starts.
Admit everyone at the start time.
A latecomer arrives in the waiting room. Admit them quietly — do not stop the class to explain what they missed.
If they miss something important, catch them up privately in the chat: "Welcome — we are on slide 3, taking
measurements."
Handling disruption — privately, never publicly:
If a learner is disruptive (background noise, talking over you, inappropriate chat), handle it privately. Send a direct message:
"Please mute yourself — I can hear background noise." Never call them out in front of everyone.
If it continues, remove them from the call and follow up privately after. Public confrontation escalates; private correction
resolves.
The rule: praise publicly, correct privately. Everyone is learning, and everyone deserves dignity.
Say this to the class:
"Online, you cannot see the room — so you make the rules visible. Three norms at the start. Latecomers through the
waiting room. Disruption handled privately. And everyone gets a turn. That is management. It is not strict — it is kind
and clear."
F.4 Troubleshooting — the calm checklist and the fallback plan
The idea. Technology will fail. A learner's audio will cut out. Your screen share will freeze. The connection will drop. The
question is not if but when — and your job is to stay calm and follow a checklist, not panic.
The troubleshooting checklist — sound, camera, screen, connection:
Order Check What to do
1 Sound Is the learner muted? Is your microphone selected correctly? Ask: "Can you hear me?" — if not, check
mute and mic settings first.
2 Camera Is the camera on? Is the right camera selected? If the video is black, check the camera toggle.
3 Screen
share
Is the right window shared? Is the screen frozen? Stop sharing and start again.
4 Connection Is the internet stable? If the call freezes, switch to mobile data, or ask learners to turn off video to
save bandwidth.
The fallback plan — write it down:
If the video freezes: Turn off your video and continue with audio only.
If the audio cuts: Switch to a phone call (dial in) or move to a fresh link.
If the screen share fails: Send the slides in the chat or by WhatsApp and teach from your phone.
If the connection drops: Send a fresh link by WhatsApp. Restart the call. If it cannot be fixed, reschedule — and send a
recording of the lesson afterwards.
If a learner cannot join: Send them the slides and a summary by WhatsApp; record the session for them.
The mobile-data fallback: Nigerian Wi-Fi fails. Have a backup: a mobile hotspot, a second SIM, or a plan to switch to audio-
only. Tell the class at the start: "If my video freezes, stay with me on audio — I will fix it."
Say this to the class:
"Troubleshooting is not about being a tech expert. It is about staying calm and following a list. Sound first, then camera,
then screen, then connection. And always have a fallback — a fresh link by WhatsApp, a mobile hotspot, a recording for
those who could not stay. The class that can survive a technical failure is the class that works."
Real-life comparison. Troubleshooting is like driving in Lagos traffic. You do not panic when the traffic stops — you change
route, you take a side street, you stay calm. The destination is the same. Technology failing is not a disaster — it is traffic. Have
a route around it.
F.5 The 10-minute micro-teach — your first real lesson
The idea. Today, each learner delivers a 10-minute micro-teach — a short, live lesson to their classmates, using at least two
participation tools. This is not a test — it is your first real online lesson, in a safe room.
The structure (10 minutes):
Time What you do
0–1 Opening Welcome, state the outcome, set one norm
1–4 Content Teach one thing — share your screen, show your slides
4–7 Interaction A participation moment — a poll, a chat prompt, a show of hands
7–9 Practice/check A second moment — learners do something or answer
9–10 Closing A quick check: "What did we learn?" + a preview of next time
What is assessed (by your peers and the instructor):
You hosted the call (admitted people, managed the room).
You used at least two participation tools (poll, chat, hand raise, breakout, whiteboard).
You stated at least one norm.
You handled the technology calmly.
You kept it to 10 minutes.
Say this to the class:
"Ten minutes. Two participation tools. One norm. That is all. Your micro-teach is not about being perfect — it is about
being a host. If you can run a 10-minute class with two moments where learners do something, you can run a course. The
rest is practice."
F.6 Summary of the lecture
The host's toolkit: mute, screen share (the right window, not the screen), chat, polls, hand raise, whiteboard, breakout
rooms (with a task), waiting room, recording (with consent).
Engagement: something asked or done at least every eight minutes — poll, chat prompt, show of hands, breakout task.
Classroom management: three norms at the start; latecomers through the waiting room; disruption handled privately;
everyone gets a turn.
Troubleshooting: sound → camera → screen → connection; a written fallback plan; mobile-data backup; stay calm.
The micro-teach: 10 minutes, two participation tools, one norm, calm technology.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: The instructor hosts a 10-minute mock live class for Ngozi's Q&A, using the full toolkit and the engagement rhythm,
narrating each step so learners see how a host thinks.
Setup: Host a Zoom or Meet call with the class. Project the host controls. Have Ngozi's three-slide deck ready.
G.1 Step 1 — Before the class: the waiting room and the norms (2 minutes)
Say: "Before anyone joins, I turn on the waiting room. I admit everyone at the start time. As they join, I say good morning and
ask them to mute."
Demonstrate: Turn on the waiting room. Admit a few learners. State the norms:
"Welcome, everyone. Three quick things: please mute yourself when you are not speaking. To ask a question, raise your
hand or type in the chat. And we listen when others speak — everyone gets a turn."
G.2 Step 2 — The opener and the first participation moment (2 minutes)
Say: "I start with a hook and a poll — the first participation moment, in the first two minutes."
Demonstrate: Share the screen with slide 1 (the title and outcome). State the outcome. Launch a poll:
"Quick poll: have you ever sewn anything? Yes or no. … Thank you — 8 yes, 4 no. If you answered no, this course is for
you. If yes, you will still learn something new."
G.3 Step 3 — The input and the second participation moment (4 minutes)
Say: "Now the teaching — a short demo. And I check the chat during it, so learners can ask questions without stopping me."
Demonstrate: Share slide 2 (Bust • Waist • Hip). Demonstrate a measurement on camera. Ask a chat prompt:
"While I talk, type one word in the chat: what do you find hardest about measuring yourself? … I can see 'tight,' 'where to
stand,' 'reading the tape.' Great — those are exactly the things we will fix today."
G.4 Step 4 — The practice and the breakout task (4 minutes)
Say: "Now the practice. I put them in breakout pairs with a clear task — no task, no breakout."
Demonstrate: Create breakout rooms (one per pair). Set the task:
"You are in pairs. In three minutes, discuss: what is the first step in taking your measurements? Come back with one
answer. I will visit each room."
Say: "I join each room for 30 seconds — just to listen and answer questions. Then I bring everyone back."
G.5 Step 5 — The check and the closing (3 minutes)
Say: "Back in the main room, I check understanding with a poll, then close."
Demonstrate: Launch a poll: "Which is the correct way to hold the tape? A — loose, B — snug not tight, C — as tight as
possible." → Results: mostly B. Close:
"Well done — B is correct. Today you learned to take three measurements. Your exit task: take your three measurements
tonight and submit the worksheet. Next time: choosing your fabric. Thank you — see you on Saturday."

complete model to copy for their micro-teach.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner practices the host's toolkit in pairs — hosting, screen share, chat, a poll
and hand raise.
Starting materials: A Zoom or Meet host account, a phone or laptop, a simple slide (or a photo) to share.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow in pairs; instructor walks the room):
1. Pair up. One person is the host, one is the learner. You will swap.
2. The host starts a call and admits their partner. Turn on the waiting room first, then admit.
3. The host shares the screen — share a specific window (a slide or a photo), not the whole screen. The learner confirms
they can see it.
4. The host asks a chat question: "Type one word — what is your favourite food?" The learner types it. The host reads it
aloud.
5. The host launches a poll (yes/no or multiple choice). The learner answers. The host shares the results.
6. The learner raises their hand (the hand icon). The host calls on them.
7. Swap roles. Repeat steps 2–6.
8. Pause and predict: After swapping, ask your partner: "What felt smooth? What felt awkward?" Write one thing you will do
differently in your micro-teach.
Expected result: Every learner has hosted, shared, polled, chatted and used hand raise — at least once as host and once as
learner.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 3: "Who shared their whole screen instead of a window? What could the learners see that they should not?"
After step 7: "What felt awkward? What will you practise more?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Host shares the whole screen → "Share the window, not the screen. Close your WhatsApp first."
Host cannot find the poll button → "Look for 'Polls' in the meeting controls. If you cannot find it, use the chat instead — a
chat question works too."
Host forgets to mute all → "Mute all now. Then unmute to speak."
Hand raise not visible → "Check the 'Participants' panel — the raised hand appears there."
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
I started a call and admitted my partner through the waiting room
I shared a specific window (not the whole screen)
I asked a chat question and read the answer
I launched a poll and shared the results
I used the hand raise and called on my partner
I swapped roles and did all of the above as the learner too
I noted one thing to improve for my micro-teach

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task (new task, done alone): Each learner delivers a 10-minute micro-teach to the class — this is the Simulation — S4 Live
Delivery Drill (15% of the final grade) — using at least two participation tools, then responding to one injected technical
failure.
Scenario. You are the instructor of your own course. You have your content (slides, a worksheet, a recording). Now you teach a
10-minute live lesson to your classmates. They are your learners. You are the host.
Requirements:
1. Host the call (or take a turn in the instructor's call). Admit people, state one norm.
2. Teach one thing from your course — share your screen, show your slides.
3. Use at least two participation tools — a poll, a chat prompt, a show of hands, a breakout task or the whiteboard.
4. Include a closing check for understanding.
5. Keep it to 10 minutes.
6. Technical failure: At some point, the instructor will inject a failure (e.g., "your screen share just froze" or "your audio cut
out"). Respond calmly using the troubleshooting checklist.
Time allowance: 15 minutes (including setup and the failure response).
Success criteria:
Hosted the call and stated a norm.
Used at least two participation tools.
Kept it to 10 minutes.
Responded calmly and correctly to the injected failure.
The three failure cards (instructor chooses one per learner):
1. "Your screen share just froze." → Correct response: stop sharing, check the connection, restart sharing. Do not panic.
2. "Your audio cut out — learners cannot hear you." → Correct response: check mute, check the microphone, switch to
audio-only or a phone call, send a fresh link by WhatsApp if needed.
3. "Half the class just disappeared — the connection dropped." → Correct response: wait, send a fresh link by WhatsApp,
restart the call, record the session for those who could not return.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu is a coding mentor in Port Harcourt. He teaches Python to six students in a small computer shop. He
decided to run his first online class — a live Python session on Zoom for 12 students, including some in Lagos. He prepared his
slides, tested his internet, and felt ready. The class was a disaster. He talked for 20 minutes straight, then shared his screen —
but he shared his entire screen, and the learners saw his WhatsApp messages and his email. Nobody answered his questions.
Three students left after 30 minutes. At the 40-minute mark, Zoom ended the call — he had hit the free-tier limit. He
concluded: "Online teaching is a scam."
Relevant facts.
Chinedu is a good teacher in person — his students love his classes.
He planned a 90-minute live class — far too long for online attention and far beyond the 40-minute free Zoom limit.
He shared his whole screen, not a window — a privacy breach and a distraction.
He used no participation tools — no poll, no chat question, no breakout.
He had no norms, no agenda and no fallback plan.
Decision point. Chinedu wants to run online classes that work. What should he change?
Possible approaches.
1. Give up — online teaching is not for him. Return to in-person only.
2. Keep the same format but buy a paid Zoom plan — so the call does not end at 40 minutes.
3. Redesign the class — shorter sessions, the right screen share, participation tools, norms and a fallback.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Here is the reasoning:
Why not 1? The problem is not online teaching — it is the design. Giving up wastes the reach and flexibility he wanted.
Why not 2? A paid plan solves the 40-minute limit but nothing else. He would still share the wrong screen, still talk for 20
minutes straight, still lose students. Paying cannot fix design.
Why 3? Every problem has a free fix.
The redesign (what Chinedu changed):
Length: Split the 90-minute class into two 30-minute sessions — inside the 40-minute Zoom limit.
Screen share: Share the window (his code editor), not the screen. Close WhatsApp and email before sharing.
Engagement rhythm: A poll in the first two minutes, a chat question during the input, a breakout pair task during practice,
a poll for the check. Something every eight minutes.
Norms: Stated at the start — mute when not speaking, raise hand or chat for questions, everyone gets a turn.
Fallback plan: Written down — if video freezes, continue audio-only; if the connection drops, send a fresh link by
WhatsApp; record each session for absent students.
Cohort: A 6-week Python course — the group starts and finishes together, with a live class every Saturday and a short
recorded lesson during the week.
The result (after four weeks): Attendance rose to 11 of 12. A student in Lagos said: "Your classes are the only ones where I
actually do something." Chinedu had not changed his teaching — he had changed its design. And he paid ₦0 for software.
Trade-offs.
The redesign took Chinedu one evening — writing the engagement plan, practising the screen share, setting up the
fallback.
The first redesigned class was imperfect. The second was better. By the fourth, it was good.
He learned that hosting is a skill — and like all skills, it improves with practice.
Professional connection. This is the pattern behind every well-run online class: short sessions, the right screen share, a
participation rhythm, clear norms and a calm fallback. Master this and you can teach almost anything online. Blame the
technology and you will conclude — wrongly — that "online teaching is a scam."

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Sharing the wrong window (or the whole screen)
Symptoms: Learners see your WhatsApp, email or browser tabs; private information is exposed. Likely cause: You shared the
entire screen instead of the specific window — or you did not close personal apps first. How to investigate: Before sharing,
ask: "What is on my screen right now?" If the answer is anything private, stop. Corrective steps: Share the window (the
application), not the screen. Close WhatsApp, email and personal tabs before sharing. Test with a colleague before the real class.
How to confirm resolved: A learner confirms they see only the intended window. Prevention: Make "share the window, close
WhatsApp" a habit before every class.
K.2 Talking for 20 minutes with no participation
Symptoms: Learners mute and vanish; nobody answers questions; attendance drops. Likely cause: You did not design
participation moments — you lectured. How to investigate: Look at your engagement plan — how many participation
moments are there? If fewer than three in 30 minutes, the design is too passive. Corrective steps: Add a poll in the first two
minutes, a chat question during the input, a show of hands or breakout during practice, a poll for the check. How to confirm
resolved: Something is asked or done at least every eight minutes. Prevention: Plan the rhythm before the class — never
improvise engagement.
K.3 No norms at the start
Symptoms: Background noise, interruptions, learners unsure how to ask questions. Likely cause: You assumed everyone knew
the rules. How to investigate: Ask: "What are the norms?" If you cannot state them, they were not set. Corrective steps: State
three norms at the start of every class — mute when not speaking, how to ask questions, everyone gets a turn. How to confirm
resolved: Learners follow the norms without being reminded. Prevention: Norms are non-negotiable — state them every
time.
K.4 Latecomers barging in mid-lesson
Symptoms: The class stops to admit and brief latecomers; the flow is lost. Likely cause: The waiting room was off, or you
stopped the class to explain what they missed. How to investigate: Is the waiting room on? Are you stopping the class for
latecomers? Corrective steps: Turn on the waiting room. Admit latecomers quietly. Catch them up privately in the chat, not in
front of everyone. How to confirm resolved: Latecomers are admitted without stopping the class. Prevention: Waiting room
on, every class, every time.
K.5 Breakout rooms with no task
Symptoms: Learners enter breakout rooms and sit in silence, or chat about other things. Likely cause: You sent them to
breakout rooms without a clear task. How to investigate: What was the task? If there was none, the breakout failed. Corrective
steps: Always give a clear, timed task: "In pairs, discuss X. Three minutes. Come back with one answer." Visit the rooms to keep
them on task. How to confirm resolved: Learners return with an answer. Prevention: No task, no breakout.
K.6 Panicking at a technical failure
Symptoms: The host freezes, apologises repeatedly, or abandons the class. Likely cause: No fallback plan — the host does not
know what to do when technology fails. How to investigate: Ask: "What is your fallback if the video freezes?" If they cannot
answer, they are unprepared. Corrective steps: Teach the checklist — sound, camera, screen, connection. Teach the fallback
plan: continue audio-only, send a fresh link by WhatsApp, record for absent students. How to confirm resolved: The host
responds calmly and follows the checklist. Prevention: Write the fallback plan down and rehearse it once.
K.7 Recording without telling anyone
Symptoms: Learners discover they were recorded and feel betrayed; trust is broken. Likely cause: The host recorded the class
without warning or consent. How to investigate: Did you tell everyone the class would be recorded? Did you offer an
alternative? Corrective steps: Always announce recording at the start: "I am recording this session for those who could not
attend — please tell me if you prefer not to be on camera." Offer an alternative (audio only, or a summary). How to confirm
resolved: Everyone knows and has consented or opted out. Prevention: Recording without consent is an ethics breach —
never do it.
K.8 Ignoring the chat
Symptoms: Learners type questions and get no response; they feel invisible. Likely cause: The host is focused on teaching and
forgets the chat. How to investigate: After the class, are there unanswered questions in the chat? Corrective steps: Check the
chat every few minutes. Ask a chat partner to monitor it if the class is large. Answer questions aloud or in the chat. How to
confirm resolved: Every chat question is acknowledged. Prevention: Build "check the chat" into your engagement plan.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — for example, the learner can
actually show an engagement plan with fewer than three participation moments, or actually describe their fallback plan. Teach
learners to confirm before concluding.

Q1. Short answer. What is the engagement rhythm rule, and why does it matter online?

Q4. Practical. A learner joins your class 10 minutes late. What do you do — and what do you NOT do?
Q5. Matching. Match each participation tool to its best use: poll, chat prompt, show of hands, breakout rooms. a) A quick
anonymous check of understanding. b) A small-group discussion with a task. c) A low-pressure way for everyone to share a
thought. d) A fast visual check of who has done something.
Q6. Scenario. Your screen share freezes in the middle of your lesson. Describe, step by step, what you do.
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. The rule: something asked or done at least every eight minutes. It matters because online attention is short and invisible —
learners can mute and vanish without you knowing. A participation moment resets their attention and keeps them in the lesson.
A2. (b) Sharing the whole screen instead of the specific window — and exposing private apps. (The fix: share the window, close
personal apps first.)
A3. Because public confrontation embarrasses the learner and escalates the situation; private correction resolves it while
preserving the learner's dignity. The rule: praise publicly, correct privately.
A4. You admit them quietly through the waiting room without stopping the class, and you catch them up privately in the chat.
You do NOT stop the whole class to explain what they missed, and you do NOT scold them in front of everyone.
A5. poll = (a); breakout rooms = (b); chat prompt = (c); show of hands = (d).
A6. Step by step: (1) Stay calm — do not panic or apologise repeatedly. (2) Say "I will restart my screen share — one moment."
(3) Stop sharing. (4) Wait a few seconds. (5) Start sharing again — the correct window this time. (6) Continue the lesson. If it
freezes again, turn off video to save bandwidth and teach audio-only. (Accept any calm, correct sequence.)

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner hosts the call, admits through the waiting room and states a norm without being prompted.
Learner's engagement plan has something every eight minutes — and they actually use the tools in the micro-teach.
Learner shares the correct window, not the whole screen.
Learner checks the chat and responds to questions.
Learner responds to the injected failure calmly and correctly.
Learner keeps the micro-teach to 10 minutes.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Engagement means being entertaining." → It means being participatory — moments where learners do something.
"I will buy a paid Zoom plan and that fixes everything." → Paying cannot fix design; the problems are length, screen share,
participation and norms.
"The waiting room is unnecessary." → It is how you admit people calmly and manage latecomers.
"Breakout rooms are for discussion." → They are for a task — no task, no breakout.
"Technology failing is a disaster." → It is traffic; have a route around it.
"Recording is automatic." → Recording requires telling everyone and offering an alternative.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Tool drill: give a list of five hosting actions (mute all, share a window, launch a poll, check the chat, admit from the waiting
room); the learner performs each one and explains when to use it.
2. Engagement plan fill-in: give a 30-minute lesson plan with the participation moments missing; the learner adds them.
3. Failure rehearsal: inject a failure (e.g., "your audio just cut out") and watch the learner respond — calmly and correctly, or
with panic to coach.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide an engagement plan already structured; the learner adds the participation tools and delivers a
5-minute micro-teach with one tool.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full engagement plan and a 10-minute micro-teach with two tools.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to design an engagement plan for a large class (30+ learners) and explain how it differs
from a small class — what tools work at scale, and what breaks.
Capstone links. The engagement plan and micro-teach completed today become the engagement plan and delivery skill
behind the capstone in Session 6. A learner who hosts well today has the delivery skill the capstone assesses. Remind learners to
keep every artefact in their working folder ( CEA-OT-FirstnameLastname ).
Timing guidance. The priority is learners hosting and delivering their micro-teach (Sections H and I). If the room is slow,
shorten the classroom-management lecture (F.3) to a 5-minute summary and protect the micro-teach time. A learner who
leaves without having hosted has not met LO5.
