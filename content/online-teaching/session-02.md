# Designing a Course and Planning a Lesson

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Design and Set Up

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (writing, structure and planning — no software required for the core skills)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 completed (or the ability to name the four online teaching modes). A course idea — real or fictional — that the learner genuinely knows how to teach. Basic writing ability. No prior lesson-planning experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 you decided how your course will run — your mode and your free tools, on a decision card. Today you decide what you will teach and how each lesson will run: measurable outcomes, a curriculum map and a timed lesson plan.

**Preparation before class:** Print the verb bank cards (Section F.2), the curriculum map template (Section F.5) and the timed lesson plan template (Section F.6) — one per learner. Prepare a projector slide with the "spot the fake outcome" examples (Section D). Have Ngozi's five outcomes and curriculum map (Sections G and I) ready as the model. Read the Course Design Document Sections 5 (LO2, LO3) and 11 before class. Instructor note on tone: This session is the "design backbone" of the course. It is thinking work, not software work, and some learners will find it abstract at first. Keep it concrete by anchoring everything to Ngozi's skirt course and to the learners' own course ideas. Celebrate the moment a learner turns "understand sewing" into a real outcome — that is the skill that separates a course from a folder of videos.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what a learning outcome is and is not, and why vague outcomes like "understand sewing" fail.
2. Write at least five measurable learning outcomes for their own course, each starting with an observable action verb (list,
identify, demonstrate, build, produce) and containing no "understand," "know about" or "appreciate."
3. Organise their outcomes into modules and lessons and produce a curriculum map — a table proving that every outcome
is taught by at least one lesson and every lesson has a purpose.
4. Plan one complete timed lesson with an opener, input, practice, a check for understanding and an exit task, with timings
that suit online reality.
5. Justify their timings and explain two techniques for keeping an online session on schedule (a visible agenda, a parking lot
for side questions, a technical buffer, or short blocks).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Project five "outcomes" on the screen. Do not label them yet.
1. "Understand sewing."
2. "Know about skirts."
3. "Cut and sew a basic A-line skirt using three measurements you took yourself."
4. "Appreciate the art of tailoring."
5. "Take three body measurements and record them accurately on a measurement chart."
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. On the screen are five sentences. They all claim to be 'learning outcomes' — the things a learner will be
able to do at the end of a course. But only some of them are real. Your job: vote for the ones that are real outcomes. Hold
up one finger for outcome one, two for outcome two… which ones are real?"
Let the class vote with their fingers. Then reveal:
"Outcomes 3 and 5 are real. Outcomes 1, 2 and 4 are fakes. The difference? A real outcome starts with an action verb —
something you can see or do. 'Cut,' 'sew,' 'take,' 'record.' A fake outcome uses a vague verb — 'understand,' 'know
about,' 'appreciate' — something that happens inside the learner's head and cannot be seen. If I cannot see it, I cannot
test it. If I cannot test it, I do not know if my teaching worked."
Expected learner response: Learners will vote, some hesitating on outcome 4 ("appreciate" sounds fancy). Accept all votes and
use the reveal to draw out the rule: action verb = real; vague verb = fake.
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"This is the single most important skill in course design. A course built on 'understand' and 'know about' is a course that
cannot be tested — and a course that cannot be tested is a course where neither the teacher nor the learner knows if
anything was learned. Today you learn to write outcomes that are so clear you could test them with a camera. By the
end of this session, you will have written five real outcomes for your own course, built a curriculum map, and planned one
complete timed lesson."
Why this opener works: It creates instant debate, it uses the familiar Ngozi example, and it teaches the core rule (action verbs)
before any definition — learners discover it by voting, which makes it memorable.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four modes of online teaching?"
Expected answer: Live, recorded, cohort, one-to-one.
Common gap: learners mix up cohort and recorded. Clarify: "Recorded is when they watch (anytime). Cohort is how long
the group runs (a start and end date together)."
Question 2: "What did you put on your decision card in Session 1? What is your primary mode?"
Expected answer: Each learner names their primary mode and one reason.
Common gap: some learners completed the card in a rush and cannot recall it. Clarify: "If you cannot remember your
mode, look at your card now — you will build on it today."
Question 3: "What is a free-tier limit? Give one example."
Expected answer: Zoom free limits group calls to 40 minutes; YouTube unlisted is not public.
Common gap: learners forgot. Clarify: "Limits are design constraints, not problems. We design around them."
Question 4: "What makes online teaching different from a classroom? Name two things."
Expected answer: Shorter attention; distraction at home; mobile-first learners; screen fatigue; participation must be
designed; data costs.
Common gap: none serious; accept any two.
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 1 you decided how your course runs. Today you decide what it teaches and how each lesson runs. A course is not
a pile of videos — it is a structure: outcomes, modules, lessons and activities, all mapped together. A lesson is not a talk
— it is a timed plan with a beginning, a middle and an end. By the end of today, you will have the design backbone of
your course."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the four modes, spend 5 extra minutes re-sorting the four mode cards on the wall
before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a learning outcome is — and is not
The idea. A learning outcome is a single sentence that says exactly what the learner will be able to do at the end of a lesson or
course — something you can observe, test or see.
Plain-English definition. A learning outcome is a promise you can keep and check: "By the end of this lesson, you will be able to
do this specific thing."
Why it exists. Without outcomes, a course is just a collection of videos and slides with no direction. With outcomes, every
lesson has a purpose and every activity has a reason. Outcomes are the rudder of the course — they decide what you teach,
what you practise and what you test.
The two kinds of outcome verb:
Vague verbs (do NOT use) Action verbs (USE these)
Understand List, name, identify
Know about Describe, explain, compare
Be aware of Demonstrate, show, teach
Appreciate Build, make, create, produce
Vague verbs (do NOT use) Action verbs (USE these)
Learn Measure, cut, sew, write, calculate
Familiarise Choose, decide, design
Why vague verbs fail. "Understand sewing" happens inside the learner's head. You cannot see it, film it or test it directly. "Cut
and sew a basic A-line skirt" is something you can watch — you can see if the learner did it. A vague outcome cannot be
assessed; an action outcome can.
Real-life comparison. Think of a job description. "The candidate will understand customer service" is vague — it tells nobody
what the person will actually do. "The candidate will answer customer calls and resolve complaints within 24 hours" is an
outcome — it says exactly what the person will do, and you can check if they did it. A learning outcome is a job description for
the learner.
The rule to write on the board: If you cannot see it, film it or test it, it is not an outcome.
Worked example — turning vague into verbal:
Vague (fake) outcome Real outcome (action verb)
"Understand sewing." "Cut and sew a basic A-line skirt using three measurements you took yourself."
"Know about body measurements." "Take three body measurements (bust, waist, hip) and record them accurately on a
measurement chart."
"Appreciate fabric types." "Identify three common fabrics (cotton, ankara, polyester) by sight and touch, and state
one use for each."
"Learn about zips." "Insert a basic zip into a skirt opening and sew it securely."
Common misconception. "More outcomes = a better course." No. A short course needs five to seven outcomes — enough to
cover the skill, few enough to actually teach and test. Twenty outcomes in a six-lesson course is a course nobody can finish.

How to use it: Pick the column that matches what the learner must do. If they must remember, use "list" or "name." If they

Nigerian examples across subjects:

JAMB maths: "Solve five quadratic equations correctly using the factorisation method."

Church teaching: "Retell the story of David and Goliath in your own words and list three lessons from it."
Say this to the class:
"The verb bank is your friend. When you are stuck on an outcome, do not stare at a blank page — open the bank, pick the
column that matches your skill, and start with one of those verbs. Then add the specific thing. 'Demonstrate…' —
demonstrate what? 'Demonstrate how to take three body measurements.' Done."
F.3 Course structure — course, modules, lessons, activities
The idea. A course is not a flat pile of lessons. It has a structure — four levels, each one smaller and more specific than the
last.
The four levels:

Course The whole learning journey — the big promise. "Sewing Basics: Cut and Sew Your First A-Line Skirt."
Module A chapter of the course — a group of related
lessons.
Module 1: "Getting Started" (measurements, tools, fabric).
Lesson One session — one focused piece of learning with

Lesson 1.2: "Taking Your Body Measurements."

"Measure your bust, waist and hip, and write them on the

Why structure matters. Structure is what makes a course feel organised instead of overwhelming. A learner who sees "Module
1, Lesson 1.2" knows where they are, what comes next, and how far they have come. Structure also helps you — the instructor
— because it stops you from teaching randomly.
Real-life comparison. Think of a building. The course is the whole building. A module is a floor. A lesson is a room. An activity
is the furniture in the room. You would not build a building by piling furniture in a field — you build floor by floor, room by
room. A course is the same.
How many lessons? For a short online course like Ngozi's, aim for five to seven lessons, each with one clear outcome. More
than ten lessons in a short course is usually a sign that the course is trying to do too much.
Common misconception. "A lesson and a video are the same thing." No. A lesson is a learning experience — it may contain a
video, but it also contains an activity, a practice and a check. A video is just one piece of content inside a lesson. (You will build
the video in Session 3.)
F.4 The curriculum map — proving every outcome is taught
The idea. A curriculum map is a table that proves two things: (1) every outcome is taught by at least one lesson, and (2) every
lesson teaches at least one outcome. It is the quality guarantee of your course.
Plain-English definition. A curriculum map is a grid: lessons down the side, outcomes across the top. You put a mark (a tick, a
lesson number) where a lesson teaches an outcome. If an outcome has no tick, it is not taught — you must add a lesson. If a
lesson has no tick, it has no purpose — you must remove it or give it an outcome.
Worked example — Ngozi's five-lesson skirt course:
Outcome 1: Take 3
measurements
Outcome 2:
Identify fabrics
Outcome 3: Cut
pattern
Outcome 4:
Sew skirt
Outcome 5:
Insert zip
Lesson 1: Measurements ✔
Lesson 2: Fabric & tools ✔
Lesson 3: Cutting the pattern ✔
Lesson 4: Sewing the skirt ✔
Lesson 5: The zip & finishing ✔
How to read it: Every outcome has at least one tick (it is taught). Every lesson has at least one tick (it has a purpose). The map is
complete and consistent — no orphans, no freeloaders.
Why this matters. Without a map, learners build courses with gaps ("I never taught the zip, but I tested it on the zip") and waste
("I made a whole lesson on something I never test"). The map catches both before they become problems. It is also the
document that proves your course is well-designed — you will submit it in the capstone.
Common misconception. "A curriculum map is extra paperwork." It is not — it is the proof that your course works. A builder
does not skip the blueprint to save time; they draw it so the house does not fall down.

Why five parts? The opener earns attention. The input delivers the new learning. The practice makes them do it. The check tells

Real-life comparison. Think of a good sermon or talk. It opens with a story that grabs you (opener), teaches the message
(input), asks you to discuss or pray (practice), asks "do you understand?" (check), and sends you out with one thing to do this

middle.

F.6 Online time management — keeping the session on schedule
The idea. Online time slips away faster than classroom time. A side question, a technical problem, a slow connection — and
you have lost 10 minutes. You need techniques to protect the schedule.
Why it is harder online: In a classroom, you can see the clock, read the room and steer back. Online, side questions arrive in the
chat and derail you; technical problems eat time; learners arrive late and you repeat yourself. Without a plan, a 30-minute lesson
becomes 45 minutes of talking.
The four techniques:
1. Short blocks online. Attention online is shorter than in a room. Plan 5–10 minute blocks, not 30-minute stretches.
Change the activity every block: watch, then do, then answer, then discuss. (You learned this in Session 1 — now you apply
it to the lesson plan.)
2. A visible agenda. Put the agenda on the screen at the start: "Today: 3 min opener, 8 min demo, 10 min practice, 5 min
check, 4 min exit task." When a side question comes, you can point to the agenda: "Great question — that is for the
practice block, write it in the parking lot and we will come back." The agenda is your shield against derailment.
3. A parking lot. A "parking lot" is a visible list (on a slide or in the chat) where you park side questions that are good but off-
topic. "That is a great question about fabric cost — I have parked it. We will answer it in the live Q&A." This honours the
question without losing the lesson.
4. A technical buffer. Plan your lesson for 25 minutes inside a 30-minute slot. The extra 5 minutes is your buffer for
latecomers, a frozen screen, a learner who cannot find the link. If everything runs perfectly, you end early — a happy
problem. If something breaks, the buffer saves you.
Worked example — how the buffer saves a lesson:
A learner joins 4 minutes late because the link did not work. Without a buffer, you either repeat the opener (boring for the
others) or skip it (confusing for the latecomer). With a 5-minute buffer, you let them in, they watch the 30-second opener
recording you prepared, and you start the input on time. The buffer absorbed the problem.
Say this to the class:
"Online, time is like data — it runs out faster than you think. The agenda, the parking lot and the buffer are not
bureaucracy. They are the difference between a lesson that ends on time and one that ends in chaos. Plan for 25, deliver in
30."
F.7 Summary of the lecture
A learning outcome is a sentence saying what the learner will be able to do — observable, testable.
Use action verbs (list, demonstrate, build, produce) — never "understand," "know about" or "appreciate."
A course has a structure: course → modules → lessons → activities.
A curriculum map proves every outcome is taught and every lesson has a purpose.
A lesson has five parts: opener, input, practice, check for understanding, exit task.
Online time management: short blocks, a visible agenda, a parking lot, a technical buffer.
Plan for 25 minutes, deliver in 30.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Walk the class through writing five outcomes, building a curriculum map and planning one timed lesson for Ngozi's skirt
course — thinking aloud so learners see the reasoning, not just the result.
Setup: Project the blank curriculum map template and the blank lesson plan template. Have the verb bank visible.
G.1 Step 1 — Write five outcomes (4 minutes)
Say: "Ngozi's course promise is: 'Cut and sew your first A-line skirt.' But that is too big for one lesson. I need to break it into five
outcomes — the five things a learner must be able to do to keep that promise."
Write them on the board, narrating each choice:
1. "Take three body measurements (bust, waist, hip) and record them accurately on a measurement chart."
2. "Identify three common fabrics (cotton, ankara, polyester) by sight and touch, and state one use for each."
3. "Cut a basic A-line skirt pattern from the learner's own measurements."
4. "Sew the skirt panels together and finish the seams."
5. "Insert a basic zip into the skirt opening and sew it securely."
Say: "Check every one: does it start with an action verb? Take, identify, cut, sew, insert. Yes. Could I film the learner doing it? Yes.
Could I test it? Yes. These are real outcomes."
G.2 Step 2 — Build the curriculum map (4 minutes)
Say: "Now I prove every outcome is taught. I make a grid — lessons down the side, outcomes across the top."
Draw the map (as in Section F.4) and place the ticks one by one:
"Lesson 1 teaches outcome 1 — measurements. Lesson 2 teaches outcome 2 — fabric. Lesson 3 teaches outcome 3 —
cutting. Lesson 4 teaches outcome 4 — sewing. Lesson 5 teaches outcome 5 — the zip."
Say: "Now I check for orphans. Does every outcome have a tick? Yes. Does every lesson have a tick? Yes. The map is complete. If
I had a lesson with no tick — say, a lesson on 'the history of fashion' — I would either remove it or give it an outcome. No
freeloaders."
G.3 Step 3 — Plan one timed lesson (5 minutes)
Say: "Now I plan Lesson 1 in detail — the measurements lesson. Thirty minutes online. Five parts."
Fill the lesson plan template on the board (as in Section F.5), narrating the timings:
"Opener 3 minutes — I will not start teaching cold. I show a 30-second clip of me measuring a learner and state the
outcome. Input 8 minutes — three short demos, one per measurement. Practice 10 minutes — they measure themselves, I
stay in the chat. Check 5 minutes — a poll: 'type your waist measurement.' Exit task 4 minutes — photograph the chart,
submit it, preview next lesson. Total: 30 minutes. I planned for 25, so I have a 5-minute buffer."
Say: "Notice the rhythm: they do something in every block. Watch, then do, then answer, then submit. That is the design. A
lesson where they only watch for 30 minutes is not a lesson — it is a film."
G.4 The finished artefacts (project them)
Say: "Here is what you should have after today: five outcomes, one curriculum map, one timed lesson plan. These are not
homework — they are the design backbone of your course, and they become part of your capstone in Session 6."
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why five outcomes and not fifteen?" (Fifteen is too many to teach and test in a short course. Five is focused.)
"Why is the practice block the longest?" (Because that is where the learning happens — they do it, not just watch.)
"What is the parking lot for?" (Side questions that are good but off-topic — park them, do not lose them.)
"Why plan for 25 minutes in a 30-minute slot?" (The 5-minute buffer absorbs latecomers and technical problems.)
Expected result: Learners see that outcomes, a map and a lesson plan are not paperwork — they are the argument that a
course works. They now have a complete model to copy.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes five measurable outcomes for their own course and starts a
curriculum map.
Starting materials: The verb bank (projected), Worksheet 2.1 (From Vague to Verbal), Worksheet 2.2 (curriculum map template),
a pen or notes app.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your course promise in one sentence: "By the end of this course, my learner will be able to ______."
2. Look at the verb bank. Pick the column that matches your skill (remembering, doing, creating…).
3. Write five outcomes for your course. Each must start with an action verb from the bank and name the specific thing the
learner will do.
4. Pause and predict: For each outcome, ask: "Could I film a learner doing this?" If no, rewrite it with a clearer verb.
5. Now open your curriculum map. Write your five lessons down the side and your five outcomes across the top.
6. Place a tick where each lesson teaches an outcome.
7. Check for orphans: Does every outcome have a tick? Does every lesson have a tick? If not, fix it.
8. Share your five outcomes with the person beside you. Ask them: "Which is your favourite, and why?"
Expected result: Five outcomes and a draft curriculum map for their own course.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 3: "Who used a vague verb by accident? Which word?" (Common: "understand," "learn about." Catch them now.)
After step 7: "Who found an orphan — an outcome with no tick?" (This is the map doing its job.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner writes "understand" → Point to the verb bank: "Pick a verb from the 'doing' or 'applying' column instead."
Learner writes too many outcomes → "Choose your five most important. What must they be able to do to keep your course
promise?"
Learner's map has a lesson with no tick → "What outcome does this lesson teach? If none, should this lesson be here?"
Learner is stuck on the course promise → "What is the one thing a learner cannot do now, but will be able to do after your
course? That is your promise."
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Course promise written in one sentence
Five outcomes, each starting with an action verb

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Pastor Bello runs a Sunday school class of 25 teenagers in a church in Port Harcourt. When the church decided to
"move classes online," he simply started teaching the same way on Zoom — 45 minutes of talking, a Bible verse on the screen,
and a "any questions?" at the end that nobody answered. After three weeks, only 8 of the 25 teenagers were still attending. He
concluded: "Online teaching does not work for teenagers."
Relevant facts.
Pastor Bello is a good teacher in person — his teenagers love his class.
His "outcomes" were vague: "The learners will know the Bible" and "understand the story of David."
His lessons were 45 minutes of input with no practice, no check and no exit task.
The teenagers were on phones, on mobile data, at home with distractions.
He had never heard of a curriculum map, a check for understanding or a parking lot.
Decision point. Pastor Bello wants his Sunday school class back. What should he change?
Possible approaches.
1. Give up and return to in-person only — accept that online does not work.
2. Keep teaching exactly as before, but longer — maybe 60 minutes will hold their attention.
3. Redesign the class — write real outcomes, structure the lessons, and design participation.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Here is the reasoning:
Why not 1? The problem is not online teaching — it is the design. Abandoning online gives up the reach and flexibility the
church wanted.
Why not 2? Longer input is the opposite of what online needs. A 60-minute talk will lose the remaining 8 teenagers too.
Why 3? The class needs the design backbone from this session: real outcomes, a structured lesson and designed
participation.
The redesign (what Pastor Bello changed):
Vague outcome: "The learners will know the Bible." → Real outcome: "Retell the story of David and Goliath in your own
words and list three lessons from it."
Structure: A six-week cohort — the group starts and finishes together, with a live class every Sunday and a short recorded
story during the week.
Lesson plan (30 minutes): 3-min opener (a dramatic question: "Have you ever faced a giant?"), 8-min input (the story, told
well), 10-min practice (in breakout pairs, each pair retells one part of the story), 5-min check (each pair shares one sentence
in the main room), 4-min exit task (write one lesson from the story in the chat).
Engagement: A poll in the first minute, a chat question in the input, a breakout task in the practice, a share in the check.
Something every 8 minutes.
Time management: A visible agenda on screen, a parking lot for side questions, a 5-minute buffer.
The result (after four weeks): Attendance rose back to 22 of 25. The teenagers started arriving early to talk in the chat. One
parent messaged: "My daughter now retells us Bible stories at dinner." Pastor Bello had not changed his teaching — he had
changed its design.
Trade-offs.
The redesign took Pastor Bello one evening of work — writing outcomes, building the map, planning the lesson. That is
the investment this session asks of every learner.
The first redesigned lesson was imperfect. That is normal. The second was better. By the fourth, it was good.
The skills are transferable: the same design works for a JAMB class, a tailoring course, a phone-repair workshop.
Professional connection. This is the pattern behind every well-run online class in Nigeria and beyond: a real outcome, a
structured lesson, designed participation and honest time management. Master this and you can teach almost anything
online. Forget it and you will conclude — wrongly — that "online teaching does not work."

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Using vague verbs ("understand," "know about," "appreciate")
Symptoms: Outcomes cannot be tested; the learner does not know if they achieved them. Likely cause: The learner wrote what
they want the learner to think, not what they will do. How to investigate: Read the outcome and ask: "Could I film a learner
doing this?" If no, it is vague. Corrective steps: Replace the verb with an action verb from the bank; add the specific thing. How
to confirm resolved: The outcome starts with an action verb and names something observable. Prevention: Always run
outcomes through the verb bank before accepting them.
K.2 Writing too many outcomes
Symptoms: A 12-outcome course that cannot be taught in six lessons. Likely cause: The learner tried to include everything they
know. How to investigate: Ask: "How many lessons do you have? Can you teach and test 12 outcomes in that time?"
Corrective steps: Cut to the five to seven outcomes that matter most. Save the rest for a future course. How to confirm
resolved: The number of outcomes matches the number of lessons (roughly one per lesson). Prevention: Rule of thumb —
one outcome per lesson.
K.3 Lessons with no outcome (orphans on the map)
Symptoms: A lesson that teaches something nobody tests — a "fun" lesson with no purpose. Likely cause: The learner added
content because it seemed interesting, not because it served an outcome. How to investigate: Look at the map — is there a
lesson row with no tick? Corrective steps: Either give the lesson an outcome (and test it) or remove the lesson. How to confirm
resolved: Every lesson has a tick. Prevention: Build the map after the outcomes, not before.
K.4 Outcomes with no lesson (gaps on the map)
Symptoms: An outcome that is tested but never taught — the learner is set up to fail. Likely cause: The learner wrote
outcomes from the course promise but planned lessons from habit. How to investigate: Look at the map — is there an
outcome column with no tick? Corrective steps: Add a lesson that teaches that outcome, or remove the outcome. How to
confirm resolved: Every outcome has a tick. Prevention: The map exists to catch this — always complete it.
K.5 A lesson that is all input, no practice
Symptoms: A 30-minute lesson where the learner watches for 25 minutes and does nothing. Likely cause: The learner planned
what they will teach, not what the learner will do. How to investigate: Look at the lesson plan — how many minutes have the
learner doing something? Corrective steps: Add a practice block (10 minutes) and a check (5 minutes); shorten the input. How
to confirm resolved: The learner does something in every block. Prevention: Plan the practice first, then the input that
prepares for it.
K.6 Timings copied from face-to-face
Symptoms: A 45-minute input block "because that is how long it takes in the classroom." Likely cause: The learner did not
adjust for online attention spans. How to investigate: Ask: "What will the learners be doing in minute 30?" If the answer is
"listening," it is too long. Corrective steps: Break the input into 5–10 minute chunks with a change of activity between them.
How to confirm resolved: No single block exceeds 10–12 minutes without the learner doing something. Prevention:
Remember: online attention is shorter. Plan for 25, deliver in 30.
K.7 No exit task
Symptoms: The lesson just… stops. No check, no task, no pointer to next time. Likely cause: The learner ran out of planning
time. How to investigate: Ask: "How does the learner know they succeeded? How do they know what is next?" Corrective
steps: Add a 4-minute exit task — a submission, a chat answer, a preview. How to confirm resolved: The lesson ends with a
task and a preview. Prevention: The exit task is the proof of learning — never skip it.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — for example, the learner can
actually point to the vague verb in their outcome, or actually show a lesson row with no tick. Teach learners to confirm before
concluding.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned the design backbone of a course. You learned that a learning outcome is a sentence
saying what the learner will be able to do — and that vague verbs like "understand" and "know about" are not outcomes
because they cannot be seen or tested. You learned the verb bank — action verbs like list, demonstrate, build, produce — and
how to use them. You learned the four-level structure of a course: course → modules → lessons → activities. You learned the
curriculum map — the table that proves every outcome is taught and every lesson has a purpose. You learned the five parts of
a lesson: opener, input, practice, check for understanding, exit task — with timings that add to 30 minutes. And you learned
the four online time-management techniques: short blocks, a visible agenda, a parking lot and a technical buffer.
Five important takeaways:
1. Outcomes are promises you can test — action verbs, specific, observable.
2. Structure is course → modules → lessons → activities — each level smaller and more specific.
3. The curriculum map proves the course works — no orphan outcomes, no purposeless lessons.
4. A lesson has five parts — opener, input, practice, check, exit task — and the learner must do something in every part.
5. Online time needs design — short blocks, a visible agenda, a parking lot and a buffer.

Learner's curriculum map has no orphans — they check it themselves.
Learner's lesson plan has all five parts and the learner does something in every block.

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Understand is fine as a verb — it means learning." → It is vague; you cannot test it. Use an action verb.
"More outcomes = a more serious course." → Five to seven is right; more is a course nobody finishes.
"A lesson is a video." → A video is one piece of content; a lesson is a five-part learning experience.
"The curriculum map is bureaucracy." → It is the proof the course works — it catches gaps before they become failures.
"I will talk for 45 minutes because that is how I teach." → Online attention is shorter; plan for 25, deliver in 30.
"The exit task is optional." → It is the proof of learning; never skip it.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Verb swap drill: give five vague outcomes ("understand cooking," "know about phones"); the learner rewrites each with an
action verb and a specific thing.
2. Map check drill: give a small curriculum map with one orphan lesson and one orphan outcome; the learner finds both and
fixes them.
3. Lesson plan fill-in: give a lesson plan with the practice and exit task blocks missing; the learner writes them.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide outcomes already written for the learner's course; the learner checks them against the verb
bank and completes the map.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — five outcomes, a map and a timed lesson plan.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to plan a second version of their lesson for a different mode (e.g., if their course is
recorded, plan the same lesson as a live class) and explain in a short paragraph what changed and why.
