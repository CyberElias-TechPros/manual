# Online Teaching Foundations

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Design and Set Up

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with online teaching design)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** A smartphone or laptop and basic ability to use it (open an app, type, take a photo, browse). A free email account (Gmail preferred). Working microphone and speakers/earphones (the bundled pair is fine). Something to teach — a real or fictional subject the learner genuinely knows. No prior teaching qualification, no prior Zoom/Classroom/Canva/OBS experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. There is no previous session. It connects directly to everyday Nigerian life: nearly every learner has joined a WhatsApp tuition group, watched a church class online, or been asked to "take the class online" at short notice.

**Preparation before class:** Print the four mode cards (Section F.2) and the scenario cards (Section D) — one set per learner or projected. Prepare a projector with the mode comparison table ready. Have a sample completed decision card (Ngozi's, Section I) printed or projected. Confirm the academy Wi-Fi works and that learners can create free Gmail/Zoom accounts in class if needed. Prepare a short "what makes online teaching different" slide. Read the Course Design Document Section 15 (Ethics) before class. Instructor note on tone: This session must feel possible, not overwhelming. Many learners arrive believing online teaching is "for tech people" or "needs expensive equipment." Your job in the first 105 minutes is to prove, with ordinary Nigerian examples, that if they can teach in a room, they can learn to teach online — with a phone and free tools. Keep the mood practical, warm and confident.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain the four online teaching modes — live, recorded, cohort and one-to-one — in their own words, and give one
advantage and one limitation of each.
2. Name at least four free tools (Zoom, Google Meet, Google Classroom, Google Docs/Slides, Google Forms, Canva, OBS,
YouTube unlisted) and match each to the teaching job it does, including stating at least one free-tier limit (for example,
Zoom's 40-minute group call ceiling).
3. Select an appropriate mode and platform stack for a stated Nigerian teaching need, giving clear reasons for the choice.
4. Explain what makes online teaching different from face-to-face teaching — shorter attention, distraction at home,
mobile-first learners, screen fatigue, and participation that must be designed rather than assumed.
5. Complete a mode + platform decision card for their own course idea, recording the mode, the tools, the reasons, and the
known limits.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner (with a notes app) or an academy laptop. Either is enough; both is ideal.
Earphones with a microphone (the bundled pair is the baseline and is genuinely good enough for a first course).
Instructor device connected to a projector or large screen.
A quiet corner of the room for any short recording practice later in the course.

Free Gmail account (opens Classroom, Docs, Forms, Meet).
Free Zoom account or Google Meet (free with a Google account).
Free Canva account (free elements only).
OBS Studio or the device's built-in recorder (set up in S3).
Free video hosting (YouTube unlisted) — set up in S3.
Sample files
Four mode cards (Section F.2) — one per mode, printed or projected.
Four scenario cards (Section D) — short teaching situations for the opening activity.
Mode comparison table (Section F.2) — projected and printed.
Decision card template (Section I) — one per learner, printed.
Ngozi's completed decision card (Section I answer key) — instructor only.
Worksheets

None required to start. Learners create free Gmail/Zoom/Canva accounts during this session and S3 if they do not already

Academy Wi-Fi in class. The lesson works fully offline with printed mode cards and scenarios if the connection fails — the

Any phone with a notes app works. A learner with only a phone and no laptop can complete every activity in this session. A

we collect less, protect more, ask permission, credit the owner, and tell the truth.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Before class, place four large signs on four walls (or four corners of the room): LIVE, RECORDED, COHORT,
ONE-TO-ONE. Do not explain them yet.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning, and welcome to Online Teaching at Cyber Elias Academy. Before we open any app or talk about any tool,
we are going to do something physical. On the four walls of this room are four words. They are four different ways to teach
online. I will read out four short teaching situations. For each one, I want you to stand up and walk to the wall that you
think is the best fit. There are no wrong answers yet — we are learning. Ready?"
Read the four scenarios one at a time. After each, give learners 20 seconds to move, then ask one or two people why
they chose that wall.
1. "A JAMB tutor in Port Harcourt wants to teach maths to 40 students in Lagos, Abuja and Kano — all at the same time, with
him talking and them asking questions." (Best fit: LIVE.)
2. "A tailor wants to show learners how to take body measurements. She wants them to watch it whenever they are free, pause
it, and rewatch it at midnight." (Best fit: RECORDED.)
3. "A church wants to run a six-week study class. Everyone starts together, everyone finishes together, and the group supports
each other week by week." (Best fit: COHORT.)
4. "A coding mentor wants to help one student build a website, watching her code and giving personal feedback every week."
(Best fit: ONE-TO-ONE.)
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"You just sorted four real teaching situations into the four modes of online teaching. That is the whole idea of today: there
is no single 'online teaching' — there are four modes, and most good courses mix two of them. By the end of this
session, you will be able to look at any teaching situation and say: 'This is the mode I need, these are the tools I will use,
and here is why.'"
Expected learner response: Learners will move, laugh, and debate ("but a recorded video can also be watched by 40 people!").
Accept all answers warmly and use the debate: "Yes — and that is exactly why we compare modes properly today. A recorded
video can reach 40 people, but they cannot ask you questions live. The mode you pick depends on what you need the learners
to do."
Why this opener works: It gets learners moving, it uses familiar Nigerian contexts (JAMB, tailoring, church, coding), it
introduces the four modes physically before any definition, and it frames the session as decision-making rather than software
training.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What apps do you use every day on your phone? Name three."
Expected answer: WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Gmail, Google Maps, a banking app.
Common gap: some learners say "the internet" or "Google." Clarify: "Google is a search engine — a place you visit.
WhatsApp is an app — a tool you open. Both are tools."
Question 2: "Have you ever joined a video call on WhatsApp, Zoom or Google Meet? What was it for?"
Expected answer: family call, church service, a class, a work meeting.
Common gap: some learners have only been participants, never hosts. Clarify: "Today we learn to be the host — the
person running the class — not just a participant."
Question 3: "What is an email address for? Why do tools ask you to sign up with one?"
Expected answer: it is your identity online; it lets the tool know who you are and save your work.
Common gap: some learners think email is only for formal letters. Clarify: "A Gmail account is a key — it opens Classroom,
Docs, Forms, Meet and more. One free account, many tools."
Question 4: "What does 'online' mean? Say it in your own words."
Expected answer: on the internet; using the internet to do something.
Common gap: none serious; accept all reasonable answers.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already use the tools — WhatsApp, YouTube, a browser, maybe email. Today we do not add new tools. We add a
new way of thinking about teaching with the tools you already have. We ask four questions: What are the four modes?
What free tools do they need? What makes online teaching different from a classroom? And — most importantly —
which mode fits your teaching?"
If more than a third of the class cannot name three apps they use, spend 5 extra minutes letting learners open their phones and
show each other their home screens before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why online teaching, why now — the Nigerian reality
The idea. Online teaching means teaching learners who are not in the same room as you, using the internet. It is not new, but in
Nigeria it has moved from "something fancy schools do" to "something almost every teacher and trainer is expected to do" —
often with no training.
Why it exists. Three gaps make online teaching necessary in Nigeria right now:
1. Geography still limits teachers. A good instructor in Port Harcourt can only reach people who can physically get to their
shop, church or classroom. Online teaching removes that boundary — the same instructor can reach learners in Lagos,
Abuja, Kano and the diaspora without paying rent for a second venue or buying a bus ticket.
2. Skills training is still mostly in-person. Tailoring, hairdressing, makeup, gele tying, cooking, phone repair and exam prep
are taught face-to-face in tiny numbers — often one master and six apprentices. Online, an instructor can reach more
learners at once, and the learners keep the material and can replay it at midnight.
3. Free tools have quietly become good enough. Zoom, Google Meet, Google Classroom, Docs, Forms, Canva and OBS are
free and run on an ordinary Android phone or an old laptop. What is missing is not equipment — it is the habit of
designing for them.
The honest promise. This course does not promise six figures a month. It promises a real, demonstrable skill: by the end, you
will have built a small online course and taught part of it live or on recording. If you can teach it in a room, you can learn to
teach it online — and you do not need to pay for a single subscription to do it.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market woman in Port Harcourt. She has two ways to sell: sit in her stall and wait for people
to walk past, or take her goods to where the people are — a church, an office, a WhatsApp group. Online teaching is taking
your teaching to where the people are. The learners are already online — on WhatsApp, on YouTube, on Instagram. The
question is whether your teaching is there too.
Important limitation / misconception. "Online teaching does not work" is something people say after a bad experience —
usually a 20-minute video of a bored voice, or a live class where everyone muted and vanished. Online teaching does work; it
simply has to be designed. A classroom has walls, a blackboard and body language to help you. Online, you must design the
attention, the interaction and the structure on purpose. That is what this course teaches.

Most courses mix two modes. This is the key insight. A pure live course is hard to schedule across time zones and busy lives. A
pure recorded course can feel lonely and learners quit. So most good courses mix: for example, recorded lessons + a weekly
live Q&A (this is what Ngozi chooses), or a cohort with recorded lessons + live group sessions. The mix is called a blended
approach.
Real-life comparison. Think of a restaurant. A live class is like eating in the restaurant — fresh, interactive, but you must be
there at opening time. A recorded class is like a packaged meal you heat at home — convenient, but no waiter to ask. A cohort
class is like a supper club — a group that meets weekly for a month. A one-to-one class is like a personal chef — exactly what
you want, but expensive per person. A good restaurant offers more than one of these. So should you.
Ngozi's mix (the running example). Ngozi Okpara runs Style with Ngozi, a tailoring business in Port Harcourt. She teaches six
apprentices in her shop and wants to build a small online course — "Sewing Basics: Cut and Sew Your First A-Line Skirt" —
with five short lessons, to reach learners in Lagos, Abuja and a cousin in Toronto. She owns a smartphone and an older laptop,
her home data is slow, and she has never taught on Zoom. She plans to charge ₦4,500 per learner. Her mix: five recorded
lessons (learners watch anytime, rewatch the hard parts) + one live Q&A session (learners ask questions, she demonstrates
answers). This is a cohort-flavoured blend — recorded for flexibility, live for connection.
Common misconception. "I must pick ONE mode." No — pick the primary mode and add a second for support. A recorded
course with no live element loses learners; a live course with no recording excludes people who cannot attend. Design the mix
around your learners' lives.

Real-life comparison. Teaching online is like radio, not like a classroom. On radio, you cannot see your listeners. You must
imagine them, speak to them, and ask them to do things — "call in now," "text this number." If you just talk for 30 minutes,
your listeners switch stations. Online teaching is the same: you must design the moments where the learner does something, or
they will switch off — and switching off online is silent and invisible.
The most important sentence of this session: Silence online is usually a design failure, not a learner failure. If nobody is
answering, the problem is rarely "the learners are lazy." The problem is almost always "I did not design a moment for them to
respond." You will learn to design those moments in Sessions 2 and 4.
F.5 Opportunities for Nigerian teachers and trainers
The idea. Online teaching is not just a way to survive — it is a way to grow. Here are the real opportunities open to Nigerian
teachers and trainers, with the honest limits.
1. Reach beyond your city. A JAMB tutor in Port Harcourt can teach a student in Kano. A hairdresser in Benin can sell a
recorded class to a learner in London. The internet removes the wall around your classroom.
2. Paid exam-prep and skill classes. JAMB, WAEC, NECO, IELTS and entrance candidates pay for good preparation. A well-run
online class — recorded lessons + live revision — can earn real money. Charge honestly, deliver well, and your reputation
grows.
3. Serve a church, mosque or NGO across locations. A church with branches in three states can train all its teachers from one
place. An NGO can train community workers without flying them to Lagos.
4. Build a diaspora audience. Nigerians abroad want their children to learn languages, culture and skills. A recorded class
serves them in their time zone — they watch at 6pm Canadian time, you sleep.
5. Create once, serve many. A recorded lesson you make once can serve 10 learners or 10,000. Your time is the only limit —
and even that is reduced by good design.
The honest limit. Online teaching does not guarantee income. It gives you a skill and a reach. What you build with them —
your quality, your honesty, your marketing — determines the income. This course teaches the skill. It does not promise
earnings, and anyone who says a two-week course guarantees money is not being told the truth.
Say this to the class:
"The scammer is not smarter than you. The scammer is patient. In teaching, the same is true: the instructor who succeeds is
not always the most talented — it is the one who designs the best. Today you start learning to design."
F.6 Summary of the lecture
Online teaching means teaching learners who are not in the same room, using the internet — and it is now a normal skill
for Nigerian teachers and trainers.
There are four modes: live, recorded, cohort, one-to-one — each with an advantage and a limitation.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Walk the class through building a mode + platform decision card for Ngozi's sewing course, thinking aloud so learners
see how a trained mind makes the decision. The finished card is the model for their own work.
Setup: Project the blank decision card template (Section I). Have the four mode cards visible. Have Ngozi's situation on screen:
Ngozi Okpara runs Style with Ngozi, a tailoring business in Port Harcourt. She teaches six apprentices in her shop. She
wants to build a small online course — "Sewing Basics: Cut and Sew Your First A-Line Skirt" — with five short lessons, to
reach learners in Lagos, Abuja and a cousin in Toronto. She owns a smartphone and an older laptop, her home data is
slow, and she has never taught on Zoom. She plans to charge ₦4,500 per learner.
G.1 Step 1 — Define the need (3 minutes)
Say: "Before we pick a mode or a tool, we define the need. What must the learners be able to do at the end?"
Write on the board:
Who: Adult beginners who want to sew their first skirt — in Lagos, Abuja, Toronto.
What they must do: Cut and sew a basic A-line skirt using their own body measurements.
Constraints: Ngozi has a phone and an old laptop; slow home data; never taught online; learners are busy adults.
Say: "The need drives everything. A mode that needs fast internet is wrong for slow data. A mode that needs everyone online at
once is wrong for busy adults in different time zones."
G.2 Step 2 — Compare the modes against the need (4 minutes)
Say: "Now we test each mode against the need. Live? Learners are in three time zones and busy — a fixed live time is hard.
Recorded? Yes — they can watch anytime, pause, rewatch the measuring part. Cohort? Yes — a group starting together keeps
them moving. One-to-one? No — Ngozi wants to reach many learners, not one."
Fill the comparison on the board:
Mode Fits? Why
Live Partly Good for Q&A, but hard to schedule across time zones.
Recorded Yes Learners watch anytime; rewatch hard parts; suits busy adults.
Cohort Yes A group finishing together keeps motivation.
One-to-one No Does not scale; Ngozi wants many learners.
G.3 Step 3 — Choose the mix (3 minutes)
Say: "So Ngozi's mix is: five recorded lessons + one live Q&A session, run as a small cohort over two weeks. The recorded
lessons do the teaching; the live Q&A does the connection. This is a blended cohort model."
Write the decision:
Primary mode: Recorded (five short lessons).
Secondary mode: Live (one Q&A session, optional but encouraged).
Cohort flavour: The group starts and finishes together over two weeks.
G.4 Step 4 — Pick the free tools (3 minutes)
Say: "Now the tools — one job at a time, all free."
Job Free tool Why
Host live Q&A Zoom (free) Screen share, chat, polls; 40-minute limit is fine for a Q&A.
Record lessons OBS or phone's built-in recorder Free; records screen + voice.
Host recordings YouTube (unlisted) Free; only people with the link can watch; low-data friendly.
Slides for lessons Canva (free) or Google Slides Free; phone-readable slides.
Worksheets Google Docs Free; learners fill in and submit.
Reminders & community WhatsApp group Free; where the learners already are.
Say: "Notice: every tool is free. Ngozi pays ₦0 for software. Her only costs are her time and her data."
G.5 Step 5 — Note the limits and the plan (2 minutes)
Say: "Finally, we write down the honest limits and the plan to handle them."
Zoom 40-minute limit: The Q&A is 30 minutes — inside the limit. If it runs long, restart the call.
Slow data: Keep recordings short (5–8 minutes), compress them, host unlisted so they buffer less.
Never taught online: Ngozi will practise with one learner first (Session 4).

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner fills in the Four Modes Comparison Table (Worksheet 1.1) for their own
course idea, then begins their own decision card.
Starting materials: Worksheet 1.1 (printed), a pen, and the learner's own course idea (from the opening activity or their own
life). The instructor projects the blank table.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your name and your course idea at the top of the worksheet — one sentence: "I want to teach ______ to ______."
2. Fill in the "What it is" column for all four modes, in your own words. (If you get stuck, look at the mode cards on the wall.)
3. Fill in one advantage and one limitation for each mode. Be honest — there are no perfect modes.
4. Now ask: "Which mode fits my learners' lives?" Write one sentence: "My learners are ______, so ______ mode fits because
______."
5. Choose your primary mode and (if you want) a secondary mode. Circle them.
6. Pause and predict: Cover your paper and ask yourself: "If I taught this mode for real, what would go wrong?" Write one risk.
7. Share your primary mode with the person beside you. If you chose different modes, explain why.
Expected result: A completed comparison table and a first draft of a mode choice for their own course.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 4: "Who chose recorded? Who chose live? Why?" (Hear at least three different reasons.)
After step 6: "What is the risk you wrote down?" (Common answers: "learners won't show up," "my internet will fail," "I
don't know how to record." Say: "Good — naming the risk is the first step to fixing it. We fix these in Sessions 3, 4 and 5.")
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner leaves the advantage/limitation columns blank → Point to the mode cards on the wall and ask: "What is one good
thing about live? What is one bad thing?"
Learner chooses a mode they cannot explain → Ask: "Why that mode and not the others?" If they cannot answer, they are
guessing — send them back to step 4.
Learner says "all of them" → Clarify: "You can mix two, but you must pick one primary — the one that does most of the
teaching."
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Course idea written in one sentence
All four modes described in my own words
One advantage and one limitation for each mode
Primary mode chosen with a reason
One risk named
Shared my choice with a partner

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Emeka is a JAMB mathematics tutor in Port Harcourt. He teaches 12 students in a rented classroom twice a week and
charges ₦15,000 per student per term. His classroom is full, and he has a waiting list — but he cannot fit more students into
the room. A former student now in Abuja messages him: "Sir, I wish I could still attend your class." Emeka thinks: what if I could
teach the Abuja students too?
Relevant facts.
Emeka is a strong teacher — his students' JAMB scores are good, and parents trust him.
He has a laptop and a smartphone, and he uses WhatsApp daily.
His students are secondary school leavers, 16–19 years old, most of them on mobile data.
He has never recorded a lesson or hosted a live online class.
He worries: "Will online students take me seriously? Will they pay the same ₦15,000?"
Decision point. Emeka wants to grow beyond his 12-seat classroom. What mode and tools should he choose?
Possible approaches.
1. Record his classroom lessons and sell the videos. He films his existing classes and sells access to the recordings.
2. Add a live online class. He runs a second, live class on Zoom for students in Abuja and beyond, at the same time as his
physical class.
3. Do nothing different — just tell the Abuja students to come to Port Harcourt.
4. Build a blended cohort: recorded lessons for the teaching + a weekly live revision class on Zoom.
Recommended solution. Approach 4 — a blended cohort. Here is the reasoning:
Why not 1 (recorded only)? Recorded lessons alone are hard to sell at ₦15,000 — learners expect interaction at that price,
and without it they drop out. Also, recording a live classroom is noisy and hard to hear.
Why not 2 (live only)? A live class at a fixed time excludes students in other time zones and those with bad internet at that
moment. It also doubles Emeka's teaching hours without doubling his reach efficiently.
Why not 3? It is the safe option, but it caps Emeka at 12 students and ignores the waiting list and the Abuja demand.
Why 4? Recorded lessons let Emeka teach once and serve many — the Abuja students, the waiting list, and future terms. A
weekly live revision class on Zoom gives the interaction that justifies the fee and keeps learners accountable. The cohort
structure (a term with a start and end date) creates deadlines and community.
Emeka's decision card (the fix):
Primary mode: Recorded — one lesson per week, 10–15 minutes, uploaded to YouTube unlisted.
Secondary mode: Live — one 45-minute revision class on Zoom every Saturday.
Cohort: A 10-week JAMB term; students start and finish together.
Tools: OBS/phone recorder, YouTube unlisted, Zoom, WhatsApp group for reminders.
Price: ₦15,000 for the term — same fee, more students, no bigger room.
Trade-offs.
Approach 4 requires Emeka to learn two new skills: recording and hosting. That is exactly what this course teaches — and
he can learn them in six sessions.
The first term will be imperfect. That is normal. The goal is a teachable first version, not a perfect one.
Recording once and serving many means his income can grow without his hours growing — the core advantage of online
teaching.
Professional connection. This is the pattern behind most successful small online courses in Nigeria: a recorded core + a live
connection + a cohort for accountability. Master this pattern and you can teach almost any subject online — tailoring, JAMB,
phone repair, cooking, Excel, anything you genuinely know.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Thinking "online teaching" means only live video calls
Symptoms: Learner plans a course that is only live classes, then struggles to schedule them. Likely cause: They have only ever
seen live classes (church streams, Zoom meetings) and assume that is the whole of online teaching. How to investigate: Ask:
"What are the four modes?" If they can only name "live," they have missed the point. Corrective steps: Re-teach the four
modes with the mode cards. Show that recorded, cohort and one-to-one are equally valid. How to confirm resolved: Learner
can name all four modes and give one advantage of each. Prevention: Always start with the four modes before any tool.
K.2 Choosing tools before choosing a mode
Symptoms: Learner says "I will use Zoom" before saying what they are teaching or to whom. Likely cause: Tools are exciting;
modes are abstract. It feels easier to pick an app than to think about learners. How to investigate: Ask: "What mode is your
course?" If they cannot answer, the tool choice is a guess. Corrective steps: Send them back to the decision card: need →
modes → mix → tools. Tools come last. How to confirm resolved: Learner can explain why they chose each tool for a specific
job. Prevention: Never let a learner name a tool before they have named their learners and their primary mode.
K.3 Ignoring free-tier limits
Symptoms: Learner plans a 90-minute live class on free Zoom, or a public YouTube channel for private lessons. Likely cause:
They have not read the free-tier limits. How to investigate: Ask: "How long is a free Zoom group call?" If they do not know,
they have not checked. Corrective steps: Teach the limits: Zoom free = 40 minutes for group calls; YouTube unlisted ≠
public. Design around them. How to confirm resolved: Learner states the limit of each tool they chose and a plan for it.
Prevention: Put the limits table (Section F.3) on the wall and refer to it every session.
K.4 Assuming learners have laptops and fast internet
Symptoms: Learner designs a course that needs a laptop, a big screen and unlimited data. Likely cause: They designed for
themselves, not for their learners. How to investigate: Ask: "What device and data do your learners actually have?" If they do
not know, they have not asked. Corrective steps: Teach mobile-first design: phones, expensive data, uneven connections.
Design for the weakest learner. How to confirm resolved: Learner can state their learners' devices and data reality and has
designed for it. Prevention: Ask "who are your learners?" before any design decision.
K.5 Copying classroom habits exactly
Symptoms: Learner plans a 45-minute online lecture with no interaction. Likely cause: They are teaching the way they were
taught, not the way online works. How to investigate: Ask: "What will the learners do in minute 20?" If the answer is "listen," the
design is wrong. Corrective steps: Teach the differences (Section F.4): shorter blocks, designed participation, mobile-first. How
to confirm resolved: Learner's plan has a change of activity at least every 8–10 minutes. Prevention: Repeat: "Silence online
is a design failure."
K.6 Believing online teaching guarantees income
Symptoms: Learner expects to get rich quickly and is discouraged when the first class is small. Likely cause: They have heard
"make money online" promises and not the honest limits. How to investigate: Ask: "What does this course promise?" If they
say "money," correct them. Corrective steps: Restate the honest promise: this course teaches a skill; income depends on
quality, honesty and effort over time. How to confirm resolved: Learner can state what the certificate does and does not mean.
Prevention: Repeat the honest statement in every session.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — for example, the learner can
actually name the four modes, or actually state their learners' devices. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that online teaching is not one thing — it is four modes: live, recorded, cohort and
one-to-one. Each mode has an advantage and a limitation, and most good courses mix two — like Ngozi's recorded lessons
plus a live Q&A. You learned the free tool stack — Zoom, Meet, Classroom, Docs, Forms, Canva, OBS, YouTube unlisted —
and the honest rule that no paid subscription is ever required. You learned what makes online teaching different: shorter
attention, home distraction, mobile-first learners, screen fatigue, and participation that must be designed. And you learned the
opportunities — reach beyond your city, paid exam-prep, church and NGO training, the diaspora — with the honest limit that
income is not guaranteed. You finished by completing your own mode + platform decision card.
Five important takeaways:
1. Four modes: live, recorded, cohort, one-to-one — know them, compare them, mix them.
2. Most courses blend two modes — recorded for flexibility, live for connection.
3. Free tools are enough — the stack covers everything, and ₦0 is a valid software budget.
4. Online is different — design for short attention, phones and distraction; silence is a design failure.
5. The decision comes first — need → modes → mix → tools → limits. Never tools first.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes

Learner names all four modes correctly and gives a reason for each choice, not just a label.
Learner chooses a mode that fits the learners' lives (schedule, device, data), not the instructor's preference.
Learner names at least four free tools and matches each to a job.

Learner explains what makes online teaching different in their own words.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Online teaching = live video calls." → There are four modes; live is only one.
"I must pick one mode." → Most courses mix two; pick a primary and a secondary.
"Free tools are not professional." → Professionalism comes from design, not price.
"I should choose the tool I know best." → Choose the mode first, then the tool that fits the job.
"My learners have laptops and fast internet." → Design for the weakest learner: a phone and expensive data.
"Online teaching will make me rich." → It teaches a skill; income depends on quality and effort over time.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Mode sorting drill: give five short scenarios on cards; the learner sorts them into the four modes and explains one reason
each.
2. Tool matching drill: give six tools and six jobs; the learner matches them and says why.
3. Limit check: give three tools; the learner states the free-tier limit of each and one plan.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed decision card (modes and tools already listed); the learner chooses and
gives reasons. Allow the learner to work with a partner.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full decision card with reasons, limits and plans.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to design two different mode/tool options for the same course and compare them in
a short paragraph, recommending one with reasons. (This builds real decision-making skill.)
Capstone links. The decision card completed today becomes the "delivery mode and tools" section of the capstone mini-
course outline in Session 6. A learner who completes it well already owns one piece of the final submission. Remind learners to
keep every artefact in their working folder ( CEA-OT-FirstnameLastname ).
Timing guidance. The priority is learners understanding the four modes and completing a decision card (Sections F.2 and I).
If the room is slow, shorten the opportunities discussion (F.5) and the case study (J) to a 5-minute mention. Protect the decision
card — a learner who leaves without one has no foundation for Session 2.
