# Creating Learning Content

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Design and Set Up

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (hands-on building in free tools — no prior design or recording experience required)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 2 completed (or the ability to write a learning outcome and a lesson plan). A course idea with at least one lesson planned. A smartphone or laptop with a free Canva account and a free Google account. No prior Canva, OBS or recording experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 2 you designed the backbone: outcomes, a curriculum map and a timed lesson plan. Today you build the material for that lesson — slides, a short recorded video, a worksheet — and you learn the rules that keep your content honest, accessible and phone-friendly.

**Preparation before class:** Install OBS Studio on academy laptops (or confirm the built-in screen recorder works on phones). Confirm every learner can sign up for free Canva and Google accounts. Prepare a projector with a sample "bad" slide deck and a "good" slide deck (Section D). Print the copyright checklist (Section F.6) and the content pack checklist (Section H). Set up a quiet corner with natural light for recording practice. Have a sample 60–90 second recording ready to play. Read the Course Design Document Sections 6 and 15 before class. Instructor note on tone: This is the "making" session — the most hands-on of Week 1. Learners will produce real artefacts today: a slide deck, a recording, a worksheet. Some will be nervous about recording themselves; normalise it ("your first recording will be imperfect — that is normal and expected"). Keep the focus on clear and honest, not polished. A learner who leaves with three usable artefacts has succeeded.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Design a short slide deck (at least three slides) that works on a phone screen — one idea per slide, large readable type,
high contrast — using Canva (free) or Google Slides.
2. Record a 60–90 second clean video lesson with clear audio, using OBS or the device's built-in recorder, following a
simple script and a quiet, well-lit setup.
3. Build a worksheet in Google Docs that makes the learner do something — fill in, measure, answer or produce — not just
read.
4. Apply accessibility basics to their content: captions or a transcript, readable font size, plain language and good contrast.
5. Apply mobile-friendly, low-data design: small file sizes, downloadable alternatives and unlisted hosting.
6. Classify five copyright scenarios correctly (usable / unusable without permission / must be cited) and use only their own,
free-to-use or permitted material, with attribution.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner (with a camera and a notes app) or an academy laptop. Either is enough; both is ideal.
Earphones with a microphone (the bundled pair is the baseline).
Instructor device connected to a projector or large screen.
A quiet corner with natural light (near a window) for recording practice — free and the cheapest production improvement
there is.
Software (all free or built-in)
Canva (free tier) or Google Slides — for slides.
Google Docs — for worksheets.
OBS Studio (free, open source) or the device's built-in screen/voice recorder — for recording.
YouTube (unlisted) — for hosting recordings (set up in class or at home).
A notes app — for scripts.
Sample files
"Bad" slide deck (Section D) — tiny text, five ideas per slide, low contrast.
"Good" slide deck (Section D) — one idea per slide, large type, high contrast.
Sample 60–90 second recording — a clear, well-lit intro (the instructor's own, or Ngozi's model).
Copyright checklist (Section F.6) — one per learner, printed.
Content pack checklist (Section H) — one per learner, printed.
Worksheets
Worksheet 3.1 — "Slide by Slide" (a planning table: slide number / one idea / what the learner does).
Worksheet 3.2 — "My Recording Script" (a simple template: hook / value / CTA, with timings).

Accounts
Free Canva account and free Google account (created in Session 1; create in class if needed). Free YouTube channel for

Academy Wi-Fi in class for Canva, Google Docs and account creation. The lesson works offline for planning and scripting;

Any phone with a camera and a notes app works. A learner with only a phone can complete every activity: Canva and

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Project two versions of the same slide — a "bad" slide and a "good" slide. Do not label them yet.
Bad slide: A slide titled "Sewing" with six bullet points in small font, a busy background, low contrast, and a paragraph of
text at the bottom.
Good slide: A slide with one big idea — "Take three measurements" — in large, high-contrast type, with one simple
image and nothing else.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Look at these two slides. They are about the same topic. I want you to imagine you are a learner, sitting on
a bus in Port Harcourt, holding your phone, trying to read this slide on a small screen with one hand. Which slide can you
actually read? … The second one, obviously. Now — why? What is different?"
Let the class call out differences. Then reveal:
"The bad slide has too many ideas, tiny text, low contrast and a busy background. The good slide has one idea, large type,
high contrast and one image. That is the whole rule of online slides: one idea per slide, big enough to read on a phone,
clear enough to see in bad light. A slide is not a document — it is a signpost. If a learner cannot read it in two seconds
on a phone, it is not a slide — it is a wall."
Then play two short audio clips (or describe them):
"Now listen. Clip one: a person recording in a noisy room, far from the phone, with traffic in the background. Clip two: the
same person, near a window, phone close, quiet room. Which audio is clearer? … The second. Clear audio matters more
than fancy video. A learner will forgive a simple video; they will not forgive audio they cannot hear. Today you learn to
make slides a phone can read and audio a learner can hear."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at the good slide and say "it is simple," "big words," "one thing." Accept all
answers and draw out the rules: one idea, large type, high contrast, one image. For audio, they will say "the second is clearer"
— draw out: quiet room, near a window, phone close.
Why this opener works: It uses the phone — the device learners actually use — as the test. It teaches the two core rules (slide
design and audio) through contrast before any definition, and it sets the standard: if it does not work on a phone, it does not
work.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a learning outcome? Give one example from your own course."
Expected answer: A sentence saying what the learner will be able to do — e.g., "Take three body measurements and record
them accurately."
Common gap: learners still write vague verbs. Clarify: "Check your outcome — does it start with an action verb? If it says
'understand,' rewrite it now."
Question 2: "What are the five parts of a lesson?"
Expected answer: Opener, input, practice, check for understanding, exit task.
Common gap: learners mix up the order. Clarify: "Opener first, exit task last. The practice is where the learning happens."
Question 3: "What is a curriculum map for?"
Expected answer: To prove every outcome is taught and every lesson has a purpose.
Common gap: some learners think it is optional paperwork. Clarify: "It is the proof your course works — it catches gaps
before they become failures."
Question 4: "What mode did you choose in Session 1, and what free tools did you pick?"
Expected answer: Each learner names their mode and tools.
Common gap: some learners chose tools they have never used. Clarify: "Good — today you will actually use them. That is
the point of this session."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have the plan — outcomes, a map, a lesson. Now you build the material. A lesson is not a plan; it is things a learner
can use: slides they can read on a phone, a video they can hear, a worksheet that makes them do something. Today you
build all three — and you learn the rules that keep your content accessible, phone-friendly, low-data and honest."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five parts of a lesson, spend 5 extra minutes writing the five parts on the board
with a one-line job for each before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Slides that work on a phone
The idea. A slide is not a document. It is a signpost — one idea, big enough to read on a phone, clear enough to see in bad
light. If a learner cannot read it in two seconds on a phone, it is not a slide — it is a wall.
The five rules of phone-friendly slides:
1. One idea per slide. One slide, one job. If you have five points, use five slides. A slide with five ideas is a document, and
nobody reads a document on a phone.
2. Large type. Body text at least 24pt (bigger is better); titles much bigger. If it fits comfortably on a phone screen without
zooming, it is big enough. Test it: hold the phone at arm's length — can you read it?
3. High contrast. Dark text on a light background, or light text on a dark background. Never grey on grey, never yellow on
white. Contrast is what makes text readable in sunlight on a bus.
4. Few words. Aim for six words per slide as a target, never more than a short sentence. If you need more words, say them
— do not write them. Slides support your teaching; they are not your teaching.
5. One clear image. One image that supports the idea — not a decoration. If the image does not help the learner understand,
remove it.
Canva vs Google Slides — which to use?
Canva (free): Beautiful templates, drag-and-drop, free elements. Best for learners who want their slides to look professional
fast. Use free elements only — never pay.
Google Slides: Simple, free, works in any browser, easy to share. Best for learners who want speed and simplicity.
Either is fine. The rules above matter more than the tool.
Worked example — Ngozi's three-slide deck for "Taking three body measurements":
Slide One idea What is on it
1 Title + outcome "Taking Your Body Measurements" + "By the end, you will take three measurements and
record them."
2 The three
measurements
"Bust • Waist • Hip" — three words, one image of a tape measure.
3 How to do it "Stand straight. Wrap the tape snug, not tight. Read the number. Write it down."
Say this to the class:
"Three slides. Three ideas. Could you read each one on a phone in two seconds? Yes. That is the test. Now imagine Ngozi
put her whole script on the slides — every word she plans to say. That is the mistake. The slides are the signpost; Ngozi is
the teacher. The learner watches the slides and listens to Ngozi — the two work together."
Common misconception. "More slides = more content." No. More slides with more words = a course nobody reads. Ten slides
with one idea each beat three slides with five ideas each.
F.2 Recording a short video lesson
The idea. A recorded lesson is you, teaching, captured on video. The goal is not a Hollywood film — it is clear audio, clear
picture, clear teaching. A learner will forgive a simple video; they will not forgive audio they cannot hear or a lesson that
rambles.
The five steps to a clean recording:
1. Script first. Write what you will say — a hook (grab attention), a value (the teaching), a CTA (what to do next). A script
stops rambling. You do not have to read it word for word, but you must have it. (Use the template in Section H.)
2. Set up the space. Quiet room, near a window (natural light is free and flattering), phone close to you (arm's length),
background tidy. Warn the household: "I am recording for 10 minutes — please do not disturb."
3. Record in short takes. Do not record 10 minutes in one go. Record the hook (30 seconds), then the first point (1 minute),
then the second point. Short takes are easier to re-record when you make a mistake — and you will make mistakes.
4. Keep the phone close. The closer the phone, the clearer the audio. A phone on a table across the room sounds distant. A
phone at arm's length sounds like a teacher.
5. Cut the silences. After recording, trim the long pauses, the "ums" and the false starts. Most phones and OBS let you trim
simply. A tight 90 seconds beats a rambling 3 minutes.
OBS vs the built-in recorder:
OBS Studio (free): Records your screen and/or camera. Best for lessons that show software, slides or a screen demo. Slightly
more setup — but you only set it up once.
Built-in recorder (free, already on the device): Records the camera and voice. Best for talking-head lessons — a skill
demonstration, an introduction, a story. The simplest option.
Rule of thumb: If the lesson is mostly you talking and demonstrating, use the built-in recorder. If the lesson is mostly a
screen, use OBS.
Worked example — Ngozi's 60–90 second introduction:
Hook (10 sec): "Have you ever wanted to sew your own clothes but did not know where to start? I am Ngozi, and in this
course you will cut and sew your first A-line skirt."
Value (50 sec): "In five short lessons, I will show you how to take your measurements, choose your fabric, cut your pattern,
sew your skirt and insert your zip. Each lesson is under 10 minutes, and you can watch them anytime, on your phone."
CTA (15 sec): "Your first step: watch Lesson 1 and take your three measurements. I will see you there."
Say this to the class:
"That is 75 seconds. Clear, warm, and it tells the learner exactly what will happen and what to do. You do not need a
studio. You need a quiet corner, a window, a phone and a script. Your first recording will be imperfect — record it again.
By the third take, you will be fine. That is normal."
Common misconception. "I need a good camera." No. A 2018 smartphone camera is enough. Learners care about clarity —
can they see and hear you — not about cinematic quality. Spend your effort on the script and the audio, not the camera.
F.3 Worksheets that make the learner do something
The idea. A worksheet is not a page of text — it is a task. The learner must do something: fill in, measure, answer, produce. A
worksheet that only asks them to read is not a worksheet; it is a handout.
The four parts of a good worksheet:
1. Title and outcome. "Taking Your Body Measurements — by the end, you will have recorded three measurements." The
learner knows why they are doing it.
2. Clear instructions. Short sentences, numbered steps, plain language. "1. Stand straight. 2. Wrap the tape around your bust.
3. Read the number. 4. Write it below."
3. Space to do the task. A table to fill in, a box to write in, a question to answer. The worksheet is where the learning happens.
4. A submission instruction. "Photograph your completed worksheet and submit it in Google Classroom." The learner knows
what to do with it when they finish.
Worked example — Ngozi's measurement chart (Google Docs):
My Body Measurements
1. Stand straight, in front of a mirror.
2. Measure your bust, waist and hip. Write each number below.
Measurement My number (cm)
Bust
Waist
Hip
3. Photograph this page and submit it in Google Classroom.
Why Google Docs? It is free, works on any device, and the learner can type directly into the table. When they submit it, you can
see their answers and give feedback. (You will set up the submission in Google Classroom in Session 5.)
Common misconception. "A worksheet is a PDF to print." No — a printable worksheet excludes learners without printers. A
Google Doc worksheet works on a phone, needs no printer, and can be submitted digitally. Design for the phone and the printer
becomes irrelevant.
F.4 Accessibility as standard
The idea. Accessibility means making content usable by everyone — including learners with poor eyesight, hearing difficulties,
or who are watching in a noisy place. It is not an extra; it is standard practice, and it makes content better for everyone.
The four accessibility habits:
1. Captions or a transcript. Add captions to your video, or provide a transcript (a text version of what you said). Captions help
learners who are deaf, learners watching in a noisy bus, and learners whose first language is not English. Many tools add
captions automatically; you can also write a simple transcript in Google Docs.
2. Readable font size. At least 24pt for body text on slides; 14pt or larger in documents. If a learner needs to zoom, the font is
too small.
3. Plain language. Short sentences, common words, no jargon without explanation. "Wrap the tape snug, not tight" beats
"Ensure the measuring tape is applied with appropriate tension."
4. Good contrast. Dark text on light background (or the reverse). Never low-contrast combinations. Contrast helps learners
with poor eyesight and everyone reading in sunlight.
Real-life comparison. Accessibility is like a ramp at a building entrance. It is built for wheelchair users, but it helps everyone
— a parent with a pram, a delivery man with a trolley, a learner carrying books. Captions are built for deaf learners, but they
help everyone watching in a noisy place. Design for the edges and you help the middle too.
Say this to the class:
"Accessibility is not 'extra work for special people.' It is the difference between content that works for everyone and content
that works for some. A learner watching your video on a bus with noise around them — captions are not optional for that
learner. They are the lesson."
F.5 Mobile-friendly, low-data design
The idea. Nigerian data is expensive and connections are uneven. A 500MB video is not a lesson — it is a barrier. Design
content that works on a phone, on slow data, in the real world.
The five low-data habits:
1. Short videos. 5–8 minutes per lesson, never 30. A short video downloads faster, buffers less, and fits a learner's attention
span.
2. Small files. Compress images and videos before uploading. A 10MB video is kinder than a 100MB video. Free tools
compress automatically; check the file size before uploading.
3. Downloadable alternatives. Offer a downloadable version (a PDF of the slides, an audio-only version) so learners can
download once on Wi-Fi and watch offline later.
4. Unlisted hosting. Host recordings on YouTube as unlisted — only people with the link can watch. Unlisted videos buffer
well and are not public.
5. Test on a phone, on mobile data. Before you publish, watch your own video on a phone, on mobile data, in a moving bus
if you can. If it buffers or the audio drops, fix it before your learners find the problem.
Real-life comparison. Low-data design is like packaging food for a journey. You would not send a learner a whole goat to
cook on the bus — you send a wrapped portion they can manage. A 500MB video is the whole goat. A 10MB video is the
wrapped portion. Package for the journey.
Common misconception. "Learners can just use Wi-Fi." Many learners are on mobile data, at home, at night, on a bus. Design
for the learner with the least — and you serve everyone.
F.6 Copyright — use what is yours, free or permitted
The idea. Copyright means the person who made something owns it. Using someone else's material without permission is theft
— and it is also a fast way to lose trust, get reported, or face legal trouble. The rule is simple: use what you made, what is free
to use, or what you have permission for — and credit the owner.
The three categories:
Category What it means Examples
Usable — your own You made it; you own it. Your photos, your slides, your script, your
recordings.
Usable — free, with attribution Free to use under a licence (like
Creative Commons), but you must
credit the owner.
Free images with a CC licence, free music with
attribution, free Canva elements.
Unusable without permission Someone else owns it; you need their
permission.
Another teacher's slides, a paid course's
material, copyrighted music, film clips, most
images from Google.
The five copyright scenarios (for the sort exercise):
1. A photo you took of your own sewing machine. → Usable — your own.
2. A free Canva element (marked "free") used in your slide. → Usable — free (Canva's free elements are free to use; no
attribution needed for free elements, but credit is good practice).
3. A background song from a popular Nigerian artist, added to your video. → Unusable without permission (copyrighted
music).
4. A diagram from a textbook, scanned into your worksheet. → Unusable without permission (someone else's work).
5. A free image from a site that says "Creative Commons — attribution required." → Usable — free, with attribution (you
must credit the owner).
Creative Commons (CC) in plain English: A Creative Commons licence is the owner saying, "You may use my work if you follow
these rules." The rules vary — some require attribution, some forbid commercial use, some forbid changes. Read the licence
and follow it. When in doubt, credit the owner.
Say this to the class:
"The rule is simple: collect less, protect more, ask permission, credit the owner, tell the truth. If you did not make it,
and it is not free, and you do not have permission — do not use it. A course built on other people's material is a course
built on sand. A course built on your own knowledge and free tools is a course that lasts."
Common misconception. "If it is on the internet, it is free to use." No. The internet is a library, not a free-for-all. A photo on
Google Images is usually someone's property. Use free sources (your own photos, free Canva elements, sites that explicitly offer
free use) and credit them.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Slides: one idea per slide, large type (24pt+), high contrast, few words, one clear image.
Recording: script first, quiet space, near a window, phone close, record in short takes, cut the silences. Clear audio beats
fancy video.
Worksheets: a task, not a page of text — title, instructions, space to do it, submission step. Google Docs, phone-friendly.
Accessibility: captions or a transcript, readable font, plain language, good contrast — standard, not optional.
Low-data design: short videos, small files, downloadable alternatives, unlisted hosting, test on a phone.
Copyright: use what you made, what is free (with attribution), or what you have permission for. Never copy another
teacher's material.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze runs a small hairdressing academy in Port Harcourt. She teaches braiding and weaving to eight apprentices in
her salon. She decided to create an online course — "Braiding Basics: Three Styles for Beginners" — and she worked hard on it.
She recorded a 12-minute video of herself braiding, added a popular Nigerian song as background music, used beautiful images
she found on Google for her slides, and scanned a page from a hairdressing textbook into her worksheet. She uploaded the
video to YouTube as public and shared the link in a WhatsApp group. Within a week, three learners told her they could not
watch the video — it kept buffering on their phones. A fourth learner messaged: "Adaeze, this song is nice, but is it yours?"
Relevant facts.
Adaeze's braiding demonstration is genuinely good — clear, skilled, well-lit.
The video is 12 minutes long and 180MB — too big for mobile data.
The background song is a copyrighted popular artist's track.
The slide images are from Google Images — not confirmed free.
The scanned textbook page is someone else's work.
The video is public on YouTube — anyone can find it.
Decision point. Adaeze's course is failing. What should she change?
Possible approaches.
1. Do nothing — the learners' phones are the problem. Blame the learners and their data.
2. Fix only the video length — split the 12-minute video into three shorter ones, but keep everything else.
3. Fix everything — re-record shorter, remove the copyrighted music, replace the Google images, remove the scanned page,
and make the video unlisted.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Here is the reasoning:
Why not 1? The learners' phones are not the problem — the design is. A 180MB video on mobile data is a barrier, not a
lesson.
Why not 2? Splitting the video helps, but the copyright problems remain. The song, the images and the scanned page are
all someone else's work. Using them without permission is theft — and it risks Adaeze's reputation and legal trouble.
Why 3? Every problem has a simple, free fix.
The fixes (what Adaeze did):
Video length: Split the 12-minute video into three 4-minute lessons — one per style. Each under 20MB after compression.
Copyright — music: Removed the popular song. Used no music (or a free, royalty-free track with attribution).
Copyright — images: Replaced the Google Images with her own photos of her work and free Canva elements.
Copyright — textbook: Removed the scanned page. Wrote her own instructions in her own words.
Hosting: Changed the video from public to unlisted — only learners with the link can watch.
Accessibility: Added a simple transcript in Google Docs for each lesson.
Low-data: Compressed the videos and offered a downloadable PDF of the slides.
The result: The three learners who could not watch the video now completed all three lessons. The fourth learner said: "Now it
feels like your course — your voice, your work, your rules." Adaeze's course was not more talented after the fix — it was more
honest, accessible and legal. That is what made it work.
Trade-offs.
The fixes took Adaeze one evening. That is the investment this session asks of every learner.
Her course is simpler now — shorter videos, fewer images, no music. Simplicity is not a loss; it is the design.
She could have paid for a music licence and a stock photo subscription. She did not need to — free alternatives were
enough.
Professional connection. This is the pattern: content that works is content that is clear, legal, accessible and kind to data. A
course that ignores any of these will fail — not because the teaching is bad, but because the design is. Master these rules and
your content will outlast the competition.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Slides with too many ideas and tiny text
Symptoms: Learners cannot read the slides on a phone; the slide looks like a document. Likely cause: The learner put their
script on the slides instead of their teaching points. How to investigate: Hold the slide at arm's length on a phone. Can you
read it in two seconds? If not, it fails. Corrective steps: One idea per slide; cut to six words; increase type to 28pt; add more
slides if needed. How to confirm resolved: Each slide has one idea, readable at arm's length. Prevention: Plan slides with the
slide-by-slide planner before opening Canva.
K.2 Recording in a noisy room, far from the phone
Symptoms: Audio is unclear; learners cannot hear the lesson. Likely cause: The learner recorded in a busy room with the phone
on a table across the room. How to investigate: Listen to the recording with earphones. Can you hear every word? If not, the
setup failed. Corrective steps: Quiet room, near a window, phone at arm's length, warn the household. Re-record. How to
confirm resolved: Every word is clear on earphones. Prevention: Always do a 10-second test recording and listen before the
full take.
K.3 Recording one long take and stumbling
Symptoms: A 10-minute recording with mistakes, "ums" and dead air. Likely cause: The learner tried to record the whole lesson
in one go without a script. How to investigate: Listen — are there long pauses and false starts? Corrective steps: Use a script;
record in short takes (hook, then each point); cut the silences after. How to confirm resolved: The final video is tight, with no
dead air. Prevention: Script first, short takes always.
K.4 A worksheet that only asks the learner to read
Symptoms: A page of text with no task; the learner reads but does nothing. Likely cause: The learner designed a handout, not a
worksheet. How to investigate: Ask: "What does the learner do?" If the answer is "read," it is not a worksheet. Corrective steps:
Add a task — a table to fill, a question to answer, a measurement to take. How to confirm resolved: The learner produces
something (a filled table, an answer, a photo). Prevention: Every worksheet must have a task and a submission step.
K.5 Using copyrighted music, images or text
Symptoms: A popular song in the video; Google Images on slides; a scanned textbook page. Likely cause: The learner assumed
"on the internet = free to use." How to investigate: For each item, ask: "Did I make it? Is it free? Do I have permission?" If none,
it is a problem. Corrective steps: Replace with own work, free elements (with attribution), or get permission. How to confirm
resolved: Every item is accounted for on the copyright checklist. Prevention: Run the copyright checklist before publishing
anything.
K.6 Heavy files that buffer on mobile data
Symptoms: A 180MB video that learners cannot download. Likely cause: The learner uploaded an uncompressed video. How to
investigate: Check the file size. Over 30MB for a short lesson is too big. Corrective steps: Compress the video; split long
lessons into short ones; offer a downloadable alternative. How to confirm resolved: The video is under 20MB and plays on
mobile data. Prevention: Compress before uploading; test on a phone on mobile data.
K.7 No captions or transcript
Symptoms: A video with no text support; learners in noisy places cannot follow. Likely cause: The learner did not think about
accessibility. How to investigate: Watch the video in a noisy place. Can you follow without sound? If not, captions are needed.
Corrective steps: Add captions (automatic or manual) or provide a transcript in Google Docs. How to confirm resolved: The
lesson works with the sound off. Prevention: Treat captions as standard, not optional.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — for example, the learner can
actually show a slide that fails the arm's-length test, or actually point to the copyrighted item. Teach learners to confirm before
concluding.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner's slides pass the arm's-length test without being told to check.
Learner's recording has clear audio — quiet room, phone close, no background noise.
Learner's worksheet has a task and a submission step, not just text.
Learner's copyright checklist is complete and honest.
Learner can explain why captions and small files matter, not just that they "should" do them.
Learner catches a copyrighted item in a partner's work during the practical.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Slides are documents." → They are signposts — one idea per slide.
"I need a good camera." → You need clear audio and a script; the camera is secondary.
"A worksheet is a handout." → It is a task — the learner must do something.
"Captions are for deaf learners only." → They help everyone, especially learners in noisy places.
"If it is on the internet, it is free." → Use only what is yours, free, or permitted.
"A big video is more valuable." → A small video that plays is more valuable than a big video that buffers.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Slide fix drill: give a "bad" slide (tiny text, five ideas); the learner rewrites it as three phone-friendly slides.
2. Audio test: the learner records 30 seconds in a noisy room, then 30 seconds in a quiet room near a window — and
compares.
3. Copyright sort drill: give ten mixed items (own photos, free elements, popular songs, textbook pages); the learner sorts
them into the three categories.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a slide template with the type size and contrast already set; the learner adds one idea per slide.
Provide a worksheet template with the task already designed; the learner writes the instructions.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — three slides, a recording, a worksheet, a copyright checklist.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to record a second version of their introduction with captions added, and to write a
short paragraph comparing the two versions — which is more accessible, and why.
