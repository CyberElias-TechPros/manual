# Capstone: Design and Deliver a Mini-Lesson

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Deliver and Grow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (assembly, delivery and spoken defence — no new software taught)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5 completed. Learners must bring their full working folder: the Session 1 mode/platform decision card, the Session 2 outcomes, curriculum map and timed lesson plan, the Session 3 content pack (slides, short recording, worksheet, copyright checklist), the Session 4 engagement plan and micro-teach notes, and the Session 5 quiz, rubric, feedback examples and ethics note. Learners should have decided whether their mini-lesson will be recorded or live.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 5 you built the assessment and support system — the quiz, rubric, feedback and support plan. Today you stop building separate pieces and assemble everything into the certificate evidence: a complete mini-course outline plus a 5–10 minute mini-lesson, which you present and defend. Nothing today is new work — it is the final, honest assembly of everything you have made across the course.

**Preparation before class:** Confirm every learner can access their working folder ( CEA-OT-FirstnameLastname ). Prepare a projector showing the finished exemplar capstone (Section G): Ngozi's one-page outline, her 7-minute recorded mini-lesson (or a detailed walkthrough of it) and her defence answers. Print the outline template (Worksheet 6.1), the mini-lesson plan template (Worksheet 6.2) and the defence prep card (Worksheet 6.3). Have a timer visible. Draft the viva slot schedule for days after Session 6 (5-minute slots; assign each learner their slot at the end of Session 5 — see §L). Read the Course Design Document Sections 5 (LO8), 12 (Capstone), 13 (Certification) and 15 (Ethics) before class. Instructor note on tone: This is the final session, and it carries two jobs. The first is practical: every learner leaves with a complete, assembled capstone — the outline filled in, the mini-lesson planned or recorded, and three defence answers written down. The second is personal: this is the session where a learner who has quietly struggled all course finally sees that they own something real. Move briskly through the assembly (it is familiar material), but slow down for the defence and the close. A learner who leaves thinking "I built this" has learned the course; a learner who leaves thinking "he talked for two hours" has not. End the session by telling the truth about what they can now do — no more, and no less.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a complete one-page mini-course outline from their existing artefacts — course title, target learner, delivery
mode and reasons, five lessons each with a measurable outcome, the free tools used, a schedule, an assessment plan and
an ethics note — with every outcome taught and checked. (Design doc LO8, LO2.)
2. Produce a 5–10 minute mini-lesson, recorded or live, that has a clear opening tied to a stated outcome, at least one
piece of learning content, a moment of learner interaction or practice, and a closing check for understanding. (Design doc
LO8, LO4, LO5.)
3. Choose and justify recorded or live delivery for their mini-lesson, applying the free-tool, low-data and time-management
lessons from Sessions 1–4. (Design doc LO8, LO1.)
4. Present and defend their design choices orally, answering at least three viva questions accurately by pointing to concrete
evidence from their artefacts. (Design doc LO8.)
5. Verify their outline against the ethics rules — minimum data, safe storage, consent, minors, and copyright — and state
what their CEA certificate honestly certifies. (Design doc LO7, LO8.)

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner (with a browser and storage space for a recording) or an academy laptop. Either is enough.
Earphones with a microphone (the bundled pair is fine — but remind learners that earphones massively improve recorded
audio).
Instructor device connected to a projector or large screen.
A visible timer (a phone timer is fine).
Software (all free)
Google Docs — for the one-page outline, the mini-lesson plan and the written defence.
Canva (free) or Google Slides — for the mini-lesson's slides, if slides are used.
OBS Studio or the device's built-in recorder — for a recorded mini-lesson.
YouTube (unlisted) — for hosting a recorded mini-lesson.
Zoom or Google Meet — for a live mini-lesson and for the viva.
Google Forms / Google Classroom — for the quiz, worksheet and assignment the mini-lesson connects to.
WhatsApp — for the learner community and the practice prompt during a recorded lesson.
Sample files
The finished exemplar capstone (Section G) — Ngozi's outline, her recorded mini-lesson (or a timestamped walkthrough
of it) and her defence answers, projected.
A thin "wrong" outline (Section D) — a vague one-page course sketch, projected.
Worksheet 6.1 — "My Mini-Course Outline" template (printed, one per learner).
Worksheet 6.2 — "My Mini-Lesson Plan" template (printed, one per learner).
Worksheet 6.3 — "My Defence Prep" card (printed, one per learner).
Worksheets
Worksheet 6.1 — the outline template with the eight parts as labelled boxes: title / target learner / mode + reasons / five
lessons with outcomes / tools + schedule / assessment plan / ethics note.
Worksheet 6.2 — the mini-lesson plan: outcome, opening (0:00–), input, practice moment, check for understanding,
close, plus a "total minutes" box.
Worksheet 6.3 — three defence questions with five lines each for spoken answers, plus an "evidence I will point to"
column.
Accounts
A free Google account (opens Docs, Slides, Forms, Classroom, YouTube, Meet).
A free Canva account and a free Zoom account (created in Sessions 1–3; create in class if needed).
Internet
Academy Wi-Fi in class for Google tools and YouTube. The outline assembly and defence writing work fully offline;
recording and hosting need internet.
Alternative for learners who lack a facility
Any phone with a browser works. A learner who cannot type quickly may dictate their outline and defence answers to a
classmate or write them by hand — the skill is the assembly and the thinking, not the typing. A learner who cannot record
at home may book the academy corner for 20 minutes before or after class.
Safety note: The capstone includes real decisions about real learners — a recorded lesson, a class group, consent. Rehearse the
ethics rules one final time: collect the minimum data, store it carefully, get consent before recording or publishing, use
"unlisted" not "public", give special care to minors, and use only your own or free-to-use material. No learner's personal data,
and no images of minors, without written consent. A mini-lesson is the learner's own work — never present someone else's
material as yours.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Project two outlines side by side. Do not label them yet.
Outline A: "Course: Sewing. Lessons: 1) measurements, 2) fabric, 3) cutting, 4) sewing, 5) finishing. Quiz at the end. Use
Zoom."
Outline B: Ngozi's complete one-page outline (abbreviated on screen — title, target learner, mode with one-line reason,
five lessons with one-line outcomes, tools and schedule, assessment plan, ethics note). The full version is in Section F.2.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning — and welcome to the final session. On the screen are two course outlines. Read them both. Now — be
honest — if a stranger messaged you one of these and said 'run my class for me,' which one could they actually run? …
The second one, obviously. Why?"
Let the class call out differences. Then reveal:
"Outline A is a wish list. Outline B is a plan. A has no learner, no outcomes, no reasons, no schedule, no assessment and no
ethics. B has eight parts, and each part answers a question a real stranger would ask: Who is this for? Why this mode?
What will they learn? How will I know they learned it? What data will I collect, and is that fair? Today you take five sessions
of work and assemble it into something like Outline B — and then you stand up and defend it. That is the capstone. That
is what earns the certificate."
Then ask the second question:
"Raise your hand if, at some point in this course, you thought: 'I will never get all this together.' … Good — most of you.
Here is the truth: you already have. Every artefact you need exists. Your decision card is in your folder. Your outcomes are in
your folder. Your slides, your recording, your quiz, your rubric — all in your folder. Today is not new work. Today is
assembly, delivery and defence. By the end of this session, your capstone will be whole."
Expected learner response: Learners will say Outline B "tells you who it is for," "it has outcomes," "it has reasons," "it has a
plan." Accept all answers and draw out the eight parts. For the second question, nearly every hand goes up — name it honestly,
it builds trust.
Why this opener works: It shows the gap between "I have ideas" and "I have a plan" in thirty seconds, it names the eight parts
before any teaching, and it lowers the fear of the capstone by proving the material already exists. The message: the capstone is
not a mountain — it is a folder, closed.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. Go learner to learner; do not answer for them.
Question 1: "Point to your folder. What did you produce in Session 1 — and what one decision does it record?"
Expected answer: The mode/platform decision card — the mode chosen (live, recorded, cohort or one-to-one) and the
free platform stack, with reasons.
Common gap: some learners left the decision vague. Clarify: "If your card just says 'use Zoom,' add one line of why. One
line is enough — but it must be there. You will need that line in the defence."
Question 2: "What did Session 2 give you — the two documents?"
Expected answer: Five measurable outcomes plus a curriculum map, and one timed lesson plan.
Common gap: outcomes with vague verbs. Clarify: "Check your outcomes one more time — action verb? If any says
'understand' or 'know about,' rewrite it now with a verb like list, identify, demonstrate, build, produce. You will defend these
today."
Question 3: "What is in your Session 3 content pack — name the four items."
Expected answer: A short slide deck, a 60–90 second recording, a worksheet that makes the learner do something, and a
copyright checklist.
Common gap: a missing copyright checklist. Clarify: "The copyright checklist goes in your outline's ethics note today. If
yours is empty, write one line: 'All material is my own or free to use; I credit any free material.'"
Question 4: "What did you run in Session 4 — and what is the magic number for engagement?"
Expected answer: A 10-minute micro-teach using at least two participation tools (poll, chat prompt, hand raise, breakout,
whiteboard). The magic number is eight — something asked or done at least every eight minutes.
Common gap: learners who talked for ten straight minutes. Clarify: "Online silence is usually a design failure. Your mini-
lesson needs at least one planned moment where the learner does something — answers, pauses, tries, submits."
Question 5: "What did Session 5 give you — the four pieces?"
Expected answer: A Google Forms quiz with an answer key, an assignment with a rubric, feedback examples (specific, kind,
next step), and a support plan — plus the ethics check.
Common gap: an ethics note that was never written down. Clarify: "Your ethics note is one paragraph. You wrote the
answers on the ethics check — today you shape them into a paragraph for the outline."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Five sessions, five artefacts, one folder. Today the folder closes. You assemble the outline, you prepare your 5–10 minute
mini-lesson — recorded or live — and you learn to defend every choice you made. By the end of this course you will hold
something real: a designed course, a taught lesson, and the ability to explain both. That is what a certificate is for."
If more than a third of the class cannot name their own five artefacts, spend 5 extra minutes in pairs: each learner opens their
folder and shows their neighbour the five pieces, naming each one. Do not continue until everyone can point at something real.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why the capstone exists — assembly, not new work
The idea. For five sessions you have been building a small course piece by piece. A builder does not buy a new house the day
before handing over the keys — they do the last job: they walk through every room, check every door closes, and fix what is
loose. That walk-through is the capstone. It asks one honest question: can you assemble everything you have learned into a
course that a stranger could run, teach one lesson from it, and explain every decision you made?
What the capstone is. Three things, together:
1. A one-page mini-course outline — your course on a single page: who it is for, how it runs, what the learner learns, how
you know they learned it, and how you keep it ethical.
2. A 5–10 minute mini-lesson — one real lesson from that course, either recorded (OBS or your phone's built-in recorder,
hosted free as an unlisted video) or delivered live, with an opening tied to an outcome, real learning content, a moment
where the learner does something, and a closing check.
3. A defence — you present your work and answer questions about it, aloud, pointing to your artefacts as evidence.
Why it is assembled, not invented. A learner who completed Sessions 1–5 already owns almost the entire capstone. The
decision card becomes the "mode and tools" section. The outcomes and map become the five-lesson structure. The slides,
recording and worksheet become the mini-lesson's content. The engagement plan becomes the interaction moment. The quiz,
rubric, feedback and support plan become the assessment and support sections. Today is the day the pieces stop being separate
and start being one thing.
What the capstone proves. Not that you are a qualified teacher — the course has never claimed that. It proves the honest,
limited, real thing the design document promises: that you can sit down with a phone and a free account and build something
a learner can actually use. That is employable. That is verifiable. That is what the CEA Certificate in Online Teaching certifies —
and nothing more.
Say this to the class:
"The capstone is not a test of how much you remember. It is a test of whether your folder is whole — whether every
promise you made in Session 1 is kept by Session 5. If your outline says five lessons, five lessons appear. If your outcome
says 'demonstrate,' something in your course asks the learner to demonstrate. If your ethics note says 'minimum data,' your
quiz and enrolment form collect the minimum. Wholeness is the whole assignment."

The two tests of a good outline. Read your finished outline twice:
The coverage test: every outcome has a lesson that teaches it, and every lesson has an outcome. If a lesson has no
outcome, cut it or give it one. If an outcome has no lesson, you have promised something you do not teach.
The stranger test: hand your outline to someone who knows nothing about your course. Can they tell you who it is for,
how it runs, what the learner learns, and how you know they learned it? If they cannot, the outline still has holes.
Worked example — Ngozi's complete one-page outline:
MINI-COURSE OUTLINE — NGOZI OKPARA, STYLE WITH NGOZI
1. Title: Sewing Basics: Cut and Sew Your First A-Line Skirt.
2. Target learner: A complete beginner — usually a young woman in her twenties or thirties — who owns or can borrow
a sewing machine and has never cut a pattern. She may be in Port Harcourt, Lagos, Abuja or abroad (my cousin in Toronto
is my sanity check). She learns mainly from her phone, sometimes on mobile data, so lessons must be short and files small.
3. Delivery mode and why: A cohort course — five recorded lessons released across two weeks, plus one live Q&A on
Zoom in Week 2. Recorded lessons let learners in different cities and time zones learn at their own pace and replay tricky
parts; the live Q&A fixes the one thing recordings cannot, which is answering a learner's specific question in front of
everyone.
4. Five lessons and outcomes:
Lesson 1 — Take and record your three body measurements. Outcome: The learner takes her bust, waist and hip
measurements and records them accurately in a measurement chart.
Lesson 2 — Choose and prepare your fabric. Outcome: The learner selects the right fabric and thread for an A-line
skirt and prepares it for cutting.
Lesson 3 — Cut your pattern pieces. Outcome: The learner cuts the front and back skirt pieces from her fabric using
her own measurements.
Lesson 4 — Sew the seams, insert the zip and hem. Outcome: The learner sews the side seams, inserts the zip and
hems the skirt using the demonstrated stitches.
Lesson 5 — Fit, press and review your finished skirt. Outcome: The learner fits the skirt, presses it and reviews it
against a simple quality checklist.
5. Free tools used: Canva (free) for slides; OBS Studio for recording; YouTube (unlisted) for hosting; Google Forms for the
quiz; Google Classroom for the worksheet and assignment; WhatsApp for the learner group; Zoom (free) for the live Q&A.
No paid subscription is used at any point.
6. Schedule and length: Week 1 — Lessons 1, 2 and 3 (one every two or three days, each 8–12 minutes). Week 2 —
Lessons 4 and 5, then the live Q&A (30 minutes). Learners get a WhatsApp reminder before each release.
7. Assessment plan: A 5-question auto-graded Google Forms quiz after Lesson 3 (questions mapped to the outcomes,
answer key with acceptable alternatives). An assignment in Google Classroom: "Photograph your finished skirt and submit
it with your measurement chart," marked with the 4-criterion rubric from Session 5. Feedback is given privately using the
specific–kind–next-step frames. The Session 5 support plan (private message, extra time, alternative task, catch-up
path) applies to any learner who goes quiet.
8. Ethics note: I collect only a learner's name and email address — the minimum needed to run the class. I store the class
list, grades and recordings in a private Google Drive folder, never in a public post. I tell every learner before the live Q&A
that it will be recorded, and I offer an alternative to anyone who objects; the recording is unlisted, not public. All course
material is my own or free to use; I credit any free material in the lesson description. For learners under 18, I obtain consent
from a parent or guardian before enrolment and before any recording or image is used, and I never message a minor
privately one-to-one without a guardian knowing.
Say this to the class:
"One page. Eight parts. Notice what Ngozi did — she did not write eight essays. She wrote eight specific answers. 'Cohort'
is a mode; 'cohort because my learners are in different cities and on different data budgets' is a defence. 'A quiz' is an
idea; 'a 5-question auto-graded quiz after Lesson 3, mapped to outcomes, with an answer key that accepts spelling
variations' is a plan. Vague answers are what you wrote in Week 1. Specific answers are what you write today."
Common misconception. "The outline is just paperwork I must finish." No — the outline is the map of your defence. Every
viva question — Why this mode? How do you keep people engaged? How do you know they learned? What about ethics? —
is answered by pointing at a section of this page. The learner who fills the outline carefully is 80% prepared for the viva already.
F.3 Preparing the 5–10 minute mini-lesson — recorded or live
The idea. A mini-lesson is one real lesson from your course, delivered start to finish in 5–10 minutes. It is not a trailer, not a
summary of the whole course, and not a lecture about teaching — it is a real lesson a real learner could actually learn from. You
choose one lesson — usually the one you know best — and you deliver it properly.
Recorded or live — the decision. Both are accepted. Choose on purpose, not by accident:
Choose recorded if… Choose live if…
Your subject benefits from close-ups, demos and editing (sewing, cooking, phone repair, makeup). Your subject benefits from
real-time questions and
discussion (exam prep,
Bible study, coaching).
Your home internet is slow or unstable — you can record in short takes. You want to prove live
facilitation skill — polls,
chat, hand raise, handling
latecomers.
Your learners are in different time zones (Ngozi has a cousin in Toronto). Your learners are all in the
same time zone and can
attend one slot.
You are nervous — recording lets you stop, breathe and retake. You are confident on
camera and enjoy
thinking on your feet.
Whichever you choose, the lesson needs four things:
1. A clear opening tied to an outcome. The first 30–45 seconds must do three jobs: get attention (a question, a problem, a
promise — "By the end of this lesson, you will have taken your three measurements and written them down"), state the
outcome in plain words, and say why the learner should care ("because every skirt you ever sew starts with these three
numbers").
2. At least one piece of learning content. Real teaching — a demonstration, an explanation, a worked example, a story with
a point. For a recorded lesson, this is where your slides, your hands, your whiteboard or your screen share do the work.
3. A moment of learner interaction or practice. The learner must do something, not just watch. In a recorded lesson this is a
planned pause: "Pause the video now and take your own three measurements. Write them in the worksheet. I will wait." In a
live lesson this is a chat prompt, a poll, a show of hands or a quick pair task. Put the pause in the plan — a moment you
did not plan will not happen.
4. A closing check for understanding. The last 60–90 seconds must check that the outcome was met: "Check your chart —
do you have three numbers, in centimetres, with the right ranges? If your waist is smaller than your bust, something went
wrong — re-measure. Post your chart in the WhatsApp group or submit it in Classroom. Next lesson: choosing your
fabric."
The timed plan — the mini-lesson's skeleton. Every strong mini-lesson has timings written down before delivery. Here is
Ngozi's 7-minute plan for Lesson 1:
Time Segment What happens
0:00–0:45 Opening Greets the learner, states the outcome ("By the end of this lesson you will have taken
and recorded your three body measurements"), shows the finished skirt for thirty
seconds as the reason to care.
0:45–2:30 Input — the three
measurements
Explains bust, waist and hip with the tape measure and the dress form; names the
common mistakes (tape too tight, measuring over thick clothes, writing numbers down
wrong).
2:30–4:30 Demonstration Measures the dress form on camera, slowly, narrating each number and writing it in
the chart; then shows a filled chart and what a good one looks like.
4:30–5:45 Practice moment "Pause the video now. Take your own three measurements and write them in the
worksheet I sent you. It takes about ten minutes. I will wait — pause the video." (In
the recording, a quiet 30-second hold with on-screen text, then:) "Welcome back."
5:45–6:30 Check for
understanding
Reads three model numbers and asks the learner to compare: "Is your waist smaller
than your bust? Re-measure. Do you have all three numbers in centimetres? If not, fix
them now."
6:30–7:00 Close Recaps the outcome, previews Lesson 2 (fabric), gives the task: "Submit your chart in
Classroom by Friday." Total: 7 minutes.
Scripted, semi-scripted, or cue cards? For a recorded lesson, script the opening and the outcome word for word — they
carry the most weight and cannot be improvised well under nerves. Script the demonstration as bullet points (the steps in
order), not full sentences — you will sound like a person, not a reader. For a live lesson, use cue cards: the timings, the opening
sentence, the three key points, the practice prompt, the closing check. Never read a script on camera; the learner can feel it.
The recording checklist (for a recorded mini-lesson):
Quiet corner, household warned, phone on silent, door closed.
Natural light from a window in front of you, not behind you (light on your face, not your silhouette).
Phone charged, storage cleared, landscape (sideways) for anything with slides or demos.
Earphones with the mic close to your mouth — this one free upgrade does more than any other.
Record a 30-second test, watch it with sound, check audio first, then light, then framing.
Record in short takes (opening take, demo take, close take) — you will edit out the dead silences and the "umms" later, or
simply leave a natural pause.
Keep the file small: export at the lowest acceptable quality, compress if needed, and remember the learner may be on
mobile data. Offer the slides as a PDF alongside the video for learners who cannot stream.
Host as unlisted on YouTube (or share the file directly in Classroom) — never public.
The live rehearsal checklist (for a live mini-lesson):
Test screen share, audio and camera before the session; have the fallback plan from Session 4 ready (fresh link by
WhatsApp, phone dial-in).
Open the room early, state three norms, state the outcome on a slide.
Run the timed plan: opening, input, practice prompt (poll or chat), close. Keep one eye on the clock and one on the chat.
Leave a 2-minute technical buffer — if the demo fails, the plan survives.
Say this to the class:
"Seven minutes is not short. In seven minutes you can state an outcome, teach one thing, make the learner do one thing,
and check that it landed. Most online lessons fail not because seven minutes is too little, but because forty minutes is spent
on everything except those four jobs. Plan the four jobs. Time them. Then record or deliver. That is the whole craft."
F.4 Presenting and defending your design — the viva
The idea. A design you cannot explain is a design you do not own. The defence (the viva) is a short spoken conversation — five
to ten minutes — where you present your outline and mini-lesson and answer questions about the choices you made. It is not
an interrogation; it is the proof that the thinking is yours.
The five standard viva questions (these are the ones to prepare):
1. Why this mode and these outcomes? — Point to Part 3 and Part 4 of your outline. Name the mode, then name the reason
that connects to your real learner: "Cohort, because my learners are in different cities and on different data budgets —
recorded lessons let them learn anywhere, and the live Q&A fixes what recordings cannot."
2. How does the lesson keep learners engaged? — Point to your mini-lesson plan. Name the practice moment and the
rhythm: "The lesson states the outcome in the first 45 seconds, demonstrates once, then makes the learner pause and take
their own measurements — something asked or done well inside the eight-minute rule. In the live Q&A I add a poll and a
breakout task."
3. How would you know learning happened? — Point to Part 7 of your outline: "The 5-question quiz maps to the outcomes
and auto-grades; the assignment is marked with the rubric; the closing check in the lesson catches the learner whose
measurements are wrong before they cut fabric."
4. What did you do about accessibility, low data, copyright and privacy? — Point to your content choices and Part 8: "Slides
use large type and high contrast; the video is compressed and the slides are offered as a PDF for learners who cannot
stream; all material is my own or free to use and credited; I collect only a name and an email, and the recording is unlisted
with consent."
5. What would you change after your first real run? — The honest answer every examiner loves: "I would watch where
learners pause and rewatch, read the quiz responses for questions they all got wrong, and adjust those two things first —
assessment tells me what to fix."
The defence technique — point to evidence. Every answer follows one shape: claim → evidence → reason. "I chose a
cohort mode (claim) because my outline's target learner learns from her phone, possibly on data (evidence), and recorded
lessons plus one live session fit that life best (reason)." A vague answer floats; an answer with evidence lands. Practise saying
each answer out loud at least twice — spoken answers that were only ever thought sound different from spoken answers that
were spoken.
The peer feedback — one strength, one next step. After each presentation, the class gives two things: one strength ("your
practice moment was clear and well-timed") and one next step ("state the outcome on a slide as well as speaking it, so
latecomers catch it"). This is not marking — it is the habit of a professional: every piece of work gets one thing praised and one
thing improved.
Saying "I don't know." If a question genuinely stumps you, the honest move is: "I have not thought about that — my current
plan would be X, and I would test it." Examiners mark honesty and reasoning higher than a confident guess. The ethics rule
applies here too: do not invent learner numbers, results or testimonials to impress — ever.
Say this to the class:
"The viva feels like a test, but it is a conversation about work you already did. You are not defending a stranger's choices
— you are explaining your own. Point to the artefact. Name the reason. That is the whole skill. And it is the skill employers
actually ask for: 'Walk me through how you built this and why.'"
F.5 The certificate, honestly — and life after this course
The idea. At the end of this session you have earned something real — and real things deserve honest words. The CEA
Certificate in Online Teaching certifies exactly this: that you can design and deliver a short, structured online lesson using
free tools. It means you understand modes and platforms, can write measurable outcomes and a curriculum map, can build
slides, short videos, worksheets and quizzes in free tools, can run a live online session with genuine participation, can assess and
give useful feedback, and do all of it with privacy, safeguarding and copyright in mind.
What it does not mean. It does not make you a government-recognised teacher, a qualified teacher of record, a licensed
lecturer or an accredited institution. It does not replace an NCE, a B.Ed or a PGDE for those who need one. It does not certify
deep knowledge of the subject you teach — only of the online delivery craft. And it does not guarantee income, students or
business success. Anyone who sells a two-week course with a earnings promise is not being honest. What we will say — and
mean — is this: a graduate can sit down today with a phone and a free account and build something a learner can actually use.
That is a genuine, verifiable, employable starting skill.
Verification. Certificates can be verified at cea.ng/certificates/verify. Graduates should quote their certificate reference
whenever they share it, so employers, schools and clients can check it.
Where to go from here. The capstone is the first item in a teaching portfolio. From here: teach the course for real (even free, to
five friends) and collect honest feedback; iterate on the two things the data shows; then consider advanced CEA work — longer
course design, video production, facilitation, running a paid cohort. Each taught course makes the next one better.
Say this to the class:
"You came in with a subject you know. You are leaving with a craft you can prove. That is what this academy promises,
and it is what your certificate will say — no more, and no less."
F.6 Summary of the lecture
The capstone is assembly — your five artefacts become one outline, one lesson, one defence.
The outline has eight parts — title, target learner, mode and why, five lessons with outcomes, tools and schedule,
assessment plan, ethics note — and passes two tests: coverage and the stranger test.
The mini-lesson has four jobs — opening tied to outcome, real content, a practice moment, a closing check — planned
with timings, delivered recorded or live.
The defence is evidence — claim, evidence, reason — for five standard questions, with honest "I don't know"s.
The certificate is honest — it certifies the craft, not a qualification, and it can be verified.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Two weeks after enrolling, Ngozi Okpara sits at her kitchen table in Port Harcourt with a folder full of work and a
deadline: her capstone mini-lesson, to be presented and defended in the final session. She has everything — five lessons of
content, a quiz, a rubric, a worksheet. But three problems are staring her down. Her first attempt at recording Lesson 1 came out
at fourteen minutes of rambling. The video file is 700 megabytes, and she knows her learners in Lagos are on mobile data. And
when she tries to say her design choices out loud — "why cohort, why these outcomes" — her mind goes blank. She is one
session away from the certificate, and she is seriously considering giving up and saying the course "was not for her."
Relevant facts.
Ngozi is a strong teacher in her shop — her six apprentices learn well from her. The subject is not the problem.
Her first recording was one continuous take: no plan, no timings, no pause for the learner to practise. Fourteen minutes of
her talking.
The 700MB file came from recording at full quality on her laptop's built-in recorder. She does not yet know about
compressing, or about offering PDF slides as a low-data alternative.
She has never once said her design choices out loud. She knows them — she has been making them for five sessions —
but knowing and explaining are different skills.
Her cousin in Toronto is waiting to watch Lesson 1 tonight.
Decision point. Ngozi's capstone is at risk — from length, from file size, and from silence. What should she do?
Possible approaches.
1. Give up — "Online teaching is not for me. I am a shop teacher, not a computer person."
2. Submit the fourteen-minute video anyway — "It covers everything. Longer must be better."
3. Fix all three problems with the tools she already has — re-record against a timed plan, compress the file and offer PDFs,
and practise the defence aloud.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. The problems are not in Ngozi — they are in the preparation, and every one of them has
a free, twenty-minute fix. "Online teaching is not for me" is the conclusion she would draw from one bad recording, and it
would be false. She has taught six apprentices for years; she simply has not yet designed for a camera. The course has given her
everything she needs — she only needs to use it properly.
The fix (what Ngozi did, in one evening):
Re-recorded against the timed plan. She wrote the four jobs with times — opening 45 seconds, input 2 minutes,
demonstration 2 minutes, practice pause, closing check, close — and recorded in three short takes. The result: 7 minutes,
20 seconds. Cutting seven minutes of rambling was not a loss; it was the lesson finally being designed.
Shrunk the file and planned for weak data. She exported at 720p instead of full quality, compressed the file to under
150MB, and uploaded the slides as a PDF in Classroom with a note: "If your data is weak, read the PDF and watch the video
on Wi-Fi." Low-data design is not an afterthought — it is respect for the learner's reality.
Practised the defence out loud — badly, then less badly. She answered the five viva questions into her phone's voice
notes, listening back and wincing. The first attempt was two minutes of "um." The second was 90 seconds of
claim–evidence–reason. The third, to her cousin on WhatsApp audio, was the one. Her cousin asked "why not just live?"
— and Ngozi answered without notes: "Because you are in Toronto and my Lagos learners are on data — recorded lets
everyone learn, and live fixes what recordings cannot."
The result: Ngozi presented her mini-lesson in the final session, answered four viva questions cleanly, and received her CEA
Certificate in Online Teaching. Her cousin in Toronto watched the lesson that week. Her first real cohort — twelve learners in
four cities, paying ₦4,500 each — started the following month. And she kept the folder. Nine files, every one a record of a
decision she could explain.
Trade-offs. The re-record cost her one evening — the same evening the course asks every learner to invest. She could have
submitted the fourteen-minute version and scraped a pass; she chose the version her learners could actually use. She also faced
a humbling truth: explaining your work out loud is a separate skill from doing the work, and it only improves by doing it badly
first. Every professional knows this. Beginners rarely get the chance to learn it safely — that is what the capstone's defence is
for.
Professional connection. This is the pattern behind every real course launch: plan with timings, design for the learner's
constraints, and be able to explain every choice. The instructors who thrive online are not the ones with the best cameras —
they are the ones whose decisions are deliberate and whose evidence is a folder they can open. Ngozi's folder is now her
portfolio. Yours will be too, by the end of today.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. Name the eight parts of the one-page mini-course outline, in order.
Q2. Multiple choice. Which mini-lesson opening best ties to a stated outcome? a) "Hello everyone, welcome back to my
channel." b) "Today I will talk about sewing." c) "By the end of this lesson, you will have taken and recorded your three body
measurements." d) "Sewing is a very useful skill that many people want."
Q3. True or false. A recorded mini-lesson must be exactly 10 minutes long.
Q4. Explain. Your outline promises five lessons and six outcomes. What does the coverage test tell you to do?
Q5. Scenario. In the viva you are asked, "How would you know learning happened?" Which artefacts do you point to, and what
do you say about each? (Name at least two.)
Q6. Practical. Your recorded mini-lesson is 14 minutes long. List the three things you will do to bring it inside 5–10 minutes.
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. (1) Course title, (2) target learner, (3) delivery mode and why, (4) five lessons each with a measurable outcome, (5) free tools
used, (6) schedule and session length, (7) assessment plan, (8) ethics note. (Accept any order that covers all eight; the order
above matches the template.)
A2. (c) "By the end of this lesson, you will have taken and recorded your three body measurements." (It states the outcome in
plain words and tells the learner exactly what they will be able to do. A is a greeting, B is a topic, D is a vague claim — none
states an outcome.)
A3. False. The mini-lesson must be 5–10 minutes — within that range, not at one exact length. A 7-minute lesson that does
the four jobs well beats a padded 10-minute one; an 11-minute lesson fails the time limit.
A4. The coverage test says: every outcome must have a lesson that teaches it, and every lesson must have an outcome. Five
lessons cannot honestly carry six outcomes — so either cut the outcome (it was a wish, not a promise) or restructure the course
so each outcome is taught. A course that promises six outcomes in five lessons is not internally consistent, and the viva will find
it.
A5. Name at least two, e.g.: (1) The quiz — "my 5-question auto-graded Google Forms quiz maps one question to each
outcome, so I see instantly which outcome is weak"; (2) The assignment and rubric — "the 'photograph your finished skirt'
assignment is marked with the 4-criterion rubric, so the final product is checked against a standard"; (3) The lesson's closing
check — "the lesson ends with a self-check that catches wrong measurements before the learner cuts fabric." (Accept any two
with a correct evidence link; the answer must point at real artefacts, not float.)
A6. Three things, e.g.: (1) Plan with timings first — write the four jobs (opening, content, practice, close) with times, then
record to the plan instead of talking freely; (2) Record in short takes — opening, demo, close — and cut the rambling and
dead silences; (3) Cut content to one outcome — a mini-lesson teaches one thing well; everything else moves to another
lesson or the worksheet. (Accept any three of: timed plan, short takes/editing, cutting content, re-recording.)

Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner's outline has all eight parts filled with specific, artefact-backed content — no vague boxes, no "a quiz"

The learner runs the coverage test without being asked and fixes any lesson-withno-outcome or outcome-with-no-lesson

which proves they understand that engagement is designed, not hoped for.
The learner chose recorded or live on purpose and can state one honest reason.
The learner's defence answers use claim–evidence–reason and point at real artefacts ("my Session 5 quiz maps to the
outcomes") — not floating generalities.
The learner answers "I don't know" honestly and offers a plan, rather than inventing.
The learner's ethics note covers all five rules, and their material is their own or free-to-use with credit.
The learner's folder is complete: nine files, named, dated, every session represented.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"The capstone is new work I haven't done." → It is assembly — the artefacts already exist; today's job is to put them on
one page and defend them.
"Longer is better." → A 14-minute ramble fails; a 7-minute planned lesson passes. Tight beats long.
"The practice moment will happen naturally." → It will not. It must be planned with a time, written on the sheet, and
spoken (or prompted) deliberately.
"The defence is about sounding clever." → It is about evidence. Point at the artefact; name the reason.
"Ethics is a box to tick." → It is a design decision: what data you collect shapes what your course asks of real people.
"The certificate makes me a qualified teacher." → It certifies the online delivery craft — honestly stated and verifiable.
Never let a graduate over-claim it; correct them kindly and quickly.
"I can copy the Ngozi model exactly." → They may copy the structure, never the content. A defence answer about a
sewing course from a JAMB tutor is instantly exposed.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Coverage test drill: give the learner an outline with two mismatches (a lesson with no outcome; an outcome with no
lesson) and ask them to find and fix both.
2. Viva aloud drill: the learner answers one standard question out loud in 90 seconds while a partner checks for claim,
evidence and reason. Repeat until the shape is automatic.
3. Timing trim drill: the learner takes a 12-minute lesson plan and re-times it to 8 minutes by cutting content — learning that
cutting is a design skill.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide the outline with Parts 1–3 and 5–6 already filled from the learner's artefacts; the learner
completes only the lessons/outcomes, assessment plan and ethics note. For the mini-lesson, allow the learner to deliver live
with the instructor as co-host, or to submit a 5-minute version. For the defence, allow two questions instead of three.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full outline, full mini-lesson, three defence answers.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to design a second delivery mode for the same course (e.g., if recorded, plan the live
version) and explain in a short paragraph what changes in the engagement plan, the schedule and the assessment —
showing they understand mode as a design decision, not a default. Additionally, ask them to write a one-paragraph "first
iteration plan": what they would change after their first real run of the course.
Capstone links. This session is the capstone: the outline assembled in Section H becomes the capstone submission, the mini-
lesson planned in Section I becomes the recorded or live delivery, and the defence answers become the viva. Remind learners
that the marking rubric for the full capstone (30%) lives in Part6_Capstone_Project/01_Capstone_Assessment_Rubric.md , and that the
viva adds 5%. A learner who completes today's work well is not "preparing for" the capstone — they are finishing it.
Timing guidance. The priority is every learner leaving with a complete outline and a timed mini-lesson plan (Sections H and
I). If the room is slow, shorten the case study (Section J) to a 5-minute read-aloud and cap the demonstration walkthrough (G.2)
at the four jobs without pausing the video. Protect the assembly time — a learner who leaves with a blank outline has no
capstone, no matter how good the lecture was. Reserve the last 10–12 minutes of the session for the course wrap-up and the
honest close; it matters more than it looks.
