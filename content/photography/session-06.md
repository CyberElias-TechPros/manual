# Capstone: Photo Shoot and Portfolio

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: "Process and Deliver"

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (practical, capstone assessment)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5 — the learner should have a foundations folder, an edited set, a portfolio page, a price list and a client workflow sheet. All shooting, editing, composition, lighting and business skills are assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the final session and the capstone assessment. Everything learned in Sessions 1–5 comes together: the learner plans, shoots, edits, assembles and presents a themed photo set for a fictional Port Harcourt client. This is the deliverable that earns the CEA Certificate in Photography.

**Preparation before class:** The two fictional client briefs (Track A — Product: Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices; Track B — Portrait: Ms. Amaka) printed per learner; academy computers with Canva and internet access; the instructor's computer connected to the projector; printed capstone assessment rubrics; a timer for the presentation segment; consent forms for any person photographed; a simple product set (soup containers, spice jars, food items) or a portrait setup (a chair, a window, a plain background) depending on the learners' chosen tracks. Instructor note on tone: This session is a celebration and an assessment. The learners have worked hard for two weeks — today they prove what they can do. Create an atmosphere of pride and professionalism. The presentations should feel like a mini graduation — each learner stands, presents their work, and receives structured feedback. Be warm, be specific, be honest. The goal is for every learner to leave feeling like a photographer.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Take a brief and translate it into a one-page brief and shot plan (client need, concept, shot list, light plan, location).
2. Shoot a themed set of at least 5 finished images (up to 8), applying all shooting skills: sharpness, composition, light,
moment and consent.
3. Select the strongest frames honestly and edit the set consistently using the editing workflow.
4. Assemble a mini portfolio in Canva with a title page, the edited set, and a contact sheet of selects.
5. Obtain and document consent — signed consent for every identifiable person in the final set (parent/guardian for under-
18s), or a note that no identifiable third parties appear.
6. Present and defend the work in a short talk: the brief, light, compositions, edits, consent obtained and what they would
do differently.

be an adult, or if under 18, a parent/guardian must sign the consent form before the shutter is pressed.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a single photograph — a strong, professional product shot or portrait. Do not explain
it. Just show it.
What the instructor says:
"Look at this photo. In a moment, I am going to ask you to tell me everything you notice. Not just 'it is nice' — I want
details. Where is the light coming from? How is the subject composed? Is it sharp? What mood does it create? What would
you do differently? You have two minutes to study it. Then we will discuss."
After two minutes, ask the class to call out what they notice. Write their observations on the board. They should identify:
light direction, composition (rule of thirds, framing, leading lines), sharpness, exposure, colour, mood, background, moment.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"Everything you just said — light, composition, sharpness, mood — that is what you have learned in five sessions. Today
you prove it. This is your capstone: a themed photo shoot for a fictional Port Harcourt client. You will plan it, shoot it, edit it,
assemble it and present it. This is not an exam — it is a demonstration of everything you can do. By the end of this
session, you will have a mini portfolio and a presentation. That is your proof of skill — the deliverable that earns your CEA
Certificate in Photography."
Why this opener works: It activates everything the learners have studied — light, composition, sharpness, mood — and
frames the capstone as a demonstration of skill, not a test. It builds confidence before the work begins.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five quality words for a good photograph?"
Expected answer: Clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose.
Common gap: Learners may forget one or two. Clarify: "Clarity = sharp. Light = correctly exposed. Composition =
intentional arrangement. Moment = right instant. Purpose = does the job."
Question 2: "What is the editing workflow? Name the steps in order."
Expected answer: Crop and straighten, correct exposure, adjust colour, retouch, sharpen.
Common gap: Learners may forget the order. Clarify: "Crop first, then exposure, then colour, then retouch, then sharpen."
Question 3: "What are the six steps of the client workflow?"
Expected answer: Brief, quote, shoot, edit, deliver, follow-up.
Common gap: Learners may forget follow-up. Clarify: "Brief, quote, shoot, edit, deliver, follow-up. Follow-up is the most
forgotten — but it is how you build relationships."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have all the skills. Today you use them all. The capstone is not about learning something new — it is about proving
what you already know. Plan carefully, shoot deliberately, edit honestly, present confidently. This is your moment."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five quality words or the editing workflow, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing
them on the board before continuing.

She needs a clean, appetising, consistent set of product images for her WhatsApp Business catalogue and Instagram.
The products: pepper soup base, dried fish, spice mix, palm oil, and a bowl of finished soup.
The style: clean, natural light, consistent look, honest editing.
The deliverable: 5–8 edited product photos, assembled as a mini portfolio.

Why the brief and shot plan matters:
It forces you to think before you shoot. A plan prevents wasted time and missed shots.

It gives you a checklist — you can tick off each shot as you complete it.
It is the first component of the capstone — without it, the rest is incomplete.
Real-life comparison. The brief and shot plan is like a building blueprint. A builder does not start laying bricks without a plan
— the plan ensures the building is sound, the rooms are the right size, and nothing is missing. The brief and shot plan does the
same for your photo shoot — it ensures the set is complete, the shots are deliberate, and nothing is forgotten.
Common mistake. Skipping the plan and just shooting. This leads to missed shots, inconsistent style and a set that does not
hang together. Always plan first.
F.3 Shooting the capstone set — applying everything
The idea. The capstone shoot is where everything comes together. You apply all the skills you have learned: sharpness,
composition, light, moment, consent. The goal is a set of 5–8 photos that are sharp, well-lit, well-composed, honestly edited
and consistent.
The shooting checklist (use this for every shot):
Consent obtained — signed form for every person photographed
Light checked — direction, softness, colour
Background checked — clean, uncluttered, appropriate
Composition planned — rule of thirds, framing, leading lines, symmetry
Sharpness routine — steady, focus, exposure, background, frames
Several frames taken — at least 3 per shot
Focus checked — zoom in to confirm sharpness
Moment captured — the right expression, the right action
Shooting tips for the capstone:
1. Start with the plan. Review your shot list. Know what you are going to shoot before you start.
2. Check the light first. Before you position the subject or product, check the light. Is it soft? Is it flattering? Is it coming from
the right direction?
3. Build the scene. For products: clean surface, plain background, product positioned at its best angle. For portraits: clean
background, subject positioned in good light.
4. Shoot the plan. Work through your shot list systematically. Do not skip shots.
5. Take more than you need. If your plan says 5 shots, take 8. You can always edit down.
6. Review as you go. After each shot, check the photo. Is it sharp? Is it well-lit? Is the composition right? If not, adjust and
retake.
7. Stay honest. Do not use filters. Do not fake anything. Shoot what is real.
Real-life comparison. Shooting the capstone is like performing a piece of music you have practised. You know the notes, the
rhythm, the dynamics. Now you perform it — not perfectly, but with skill and feeling. The performance is the proof of practice.
Common mistake. Rushing through the shoot to get it over with. The capstone is not a race. Take your time. Check each shot.
The quality of the shoot determines the quality of the portfolio.
F.4 Selecting and editing the set
The idea. After the shoot, you have a folder of raw photos. Your job is to select the strongest ones and edit them consistently.
This is where the editing workflow and batch editing skills come in.
The selection process:
1. Review all the raw photos from the shoot.
2. Rate each photo: Strong, Acceptable or Weak.
3. Select 5–8 Strong photos for the final set.
4. Create a contact sheet — a grid of the selected photos before editing. This shows your selection process.
5. Edit the selected photos using the workflow: crop, exposure, colour, retouch, sharpen.
6. Batch-edit for consistency — copy the edits from the first photo, paste to the others, review each one individually.
7. Export the final set as JPEG files.
The editing rules for the capstone:
Edit honestly. Do not change the product's appearance. Do not fake a background. Do not reshape a body.
Edit consistently. All photos in the set should look like they belong together.
Edit to enhance, not to transform. The goal is a better version of reality, not a different reality.
Real-life comparison. Selecting and editing is like editing a film. The director shoots hours of footage, then selects the best
scenes and edits them into a coherent story. You do the same — you select the best frames and edit them into a cohesive set.
Common mistake. Including a photo because you like the memory, not because it is strong. The capstone set should contain
only Strong photos — no exceptions.
F.5 Assembling the mini portfolio in Canva
The idea. The mini portfolio is the final presentation of the capstone — a professional document that shows the client (and the
instructor) what you can do. It is assembled in Canva and exported as a PDF.
The mini portfolio structure:
Page What it contains
Title page Your name, course name, date, client name
The set 5–8 edited photos, sequenced for flow, with brief descriptions
Contact sheet A grid of the raw selects before editing
Consent note A statement that consent was obtained for all identifiable people, or that no identifiable third parties
appear
How to assemble the mini portfolio in Canva:
1. Open Canva. Choose a clean, simple portfolio template.
2. Title page: Add your name, "CEA Photography — Capstone," the date, and the client name.
3. The set: Add your 5–8 edited photos in the sequenced order. Add a brief description under each photo.
4. Contact sheet: Add a grid of your raw selects (before editing). Label it "Contact Sheet — Selects."
5. Consent note: Add a page with a statement: "Consent was obtained for all identifiable people in this set. Signed consent
forms are available on request." Or: "No identifiable third parties appear in this set."
6. Review and export: Check the layout, spacing and alignment. Export as a PDF.
Real-life comparison. The mini portfolio is like a book. The title page is the cover. The set is the chapters. The contact sheet is
the appendix. The consent note is the legal page. A good book is well-organised, easy to read and professionally presented. Your
mini portfolio should be the same.
Common mistake. Rushing the portfolio assembly. The portfolio is the client's first impression of your work — take the time to
make it clean and professional.
F.6 The presentation — defending your work
The idea. The presentation is the final component of the capstone — a short talk where you explain your work and defend your
choices. This is where you show that you are not just a person who takes photos, but a professional who understands what they
did and why.
The presentation structure (3–5 minutes per learner):
Section What to say
1. The brief
Who is the client? What did they need? What was the purpose?
2. The plan
What was your concept? What shots did you plan? What light did you use?
3. The shoot
Where did you shoot? What challenges did you face? How did you handle them?
4. The edits
What did you change? Why? How did you keep it honest?
5. The consent
Who did you photograph? How did you obtain consent?
6. What I would do differently
What did you learn? What would you improve next time?
The presentation rules:
Be honest. If something did not go well, say so. Honesty is a sign of professionalism.
Be specific. Do not say "I took some photos." Say "I positioned the product near a window for soft, directional light and
used the rule of thirds to place it on a grid intersection."
Be confident. You have worked hard for two weeks. Own your work.
Be open to feedback. The audience will give feedback — receive it gracefully.
Real-life comparison. The presentation is like a job interview. You are not just showing your work — you are explaining your
thinking, your process and your professionalism. The interviewer (the client, the instructor) is judging not just the photos, but
the photographer — can you think, can you plan, can you explain, can you learn?
Common mistake. Being vague. "I took some photos and edited them" is not a presentation. Be specific — explain every
choice, every challenge, every decision.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
The capstone is a themed photo shoot for a fictional Port Harcourt client, planned, shot, edited and presented as a mini
portfolio.
Two tracks: Product (Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices) or Portrait (Ms. Amaka).

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)

G.1 What the instructor prepares
A completed brief and shot plan (for Track A or B).
A set of raw photos from a sample shoot.
A completed mini portfolio in Canva.
A sample presentation.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the brief and shot plan (3 minutes)
Say: "This is my brief and shot plan for Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices. Watch how I think before I shoot."
Show the completed brief and shot plan. Walk through each section:
Client need: "Mama Nkechi needs product photos for her WhatsApp Business catalogue and Instagram."
Concept: "Clean, appetising, natural light, consistent look."
Shot list: "Eight shots — hero shot, detail shots, context shots, variety shots."
Light plan: "Window light, morning, reflector for fill."
Location: "Her kitchen, near the window."
Consent: "No people in the final set — products only."
Say: "A plan is not paperwork — it is thinking. It forces you to make decisions before you shoot, so you do not waste time and
miss shots."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — selecting and editing (5 minutes)
Say: "I have 20 raw photos from the shoot. Now I select the strongest and edit them consistently."
Step 1: Show all 20 photos quickly. Rate each one. "This one is Strong — sharp, well-lit. This one is Weak — blurry. This one is
Acceptable — slightly dark."
Step 2: Select the strongest 6. "These are my six. Quality over quantity."

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu has just completed the CEA Photography course. He has his capstone portfolio — a set of product photos
for Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices. He is ready to find his first paying client. He posts his portfolio on Instagram with the
caption "Photographer available for bookings. DM for prices." After one week, he has had 15 likes but no enquiries.
Relevant facts.
Chinedu's portfolio is strong — sharp, well-lit, consistent, professional.
He has a price list — three packages in naira.
He has a client workflow — brief, quote, shoot, edit, deliver, follow-up.
He has consent forms ready.
But he does not know how to reach potential clients.
Decision point. What should Chinedu do to find his first paying client?
Possible approaches.
1. Post more often on Instagram — consistency on social media is the key.
2. Reach out directly to small businesses in Port Harcourt — offer his services in person or by phone.
3. Lower his prices significantly — charge less than the competition to attract clients.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Chinedu's portfolio is strong — his problem is visibility, not quality. Posting more on
Instagram may help eventually, but the fastest way to find a first client is to reach out directly. Small businesses in Port Harcourt
— food vendors, tailors, salons, shops — need product photos. Chinedu can visit them, show his portfolio on his phone, and
offer his services. A face-to-face conversation with a strong portfolio is more powerful than an Instagram post.
Specific steps for Chinedu:
1. Identify 10 small businesses in his area that need product photos.
2. Visit each one — introduce himself, show his portfolio on his phone, explain his packages.
3. Offer a fair price — his Basic package (5 photos for ₦15,000) is an easy entry point.
4. Follow up — if the business is not ready now, check back in a week.
5. Deliver excellently — his first client is his most important marketing tool. A happy client refers others.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (post more) is passive — it may work eventually, but it is slow and uncertain.
Approach 2 (reach out directly) is active — it requires courage, but it is the fastest way to find a first client.
Approach 3 (lower prices) is unnecessary — Chinedu's prices are already fair. Lowering them further devalues his work and
sets an expectation that his skill is cheap.
Professional connection. This case study illustrates the transition from student to professional. Chinedu has the skill — now he
needs the client. The transition requires courage (reaching out), professionalism (a strong portfolio and clear pricing) and
persistence (following up). The first client is the hardest — but once Chinedu delivers excellent work, the referrals will come.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Skipping the brief and shot plan
Symptoms: The learner starts shooting without a plan — no clear concept, no shot list, no light plan. Likely cause: The learner
is eager to shoot and does not see the value of planning. How to investigate: Ask the learner: "What is your concept? What
shots are you going to take? What light will you use?" Corrective steps: Stop the shoot. Complete the brief and shot plan first.
A plan prevents wasted time and missed shots. How to confirm resolved: The learner has a completed brief and shot plan.
How to prevent recurrence: Make the brief and shot plan the first step of every shoot. No plan, no shoot.
K.2 Inconsistent set — photos do not belong together
Symptoms: The set of photos looks like they came from different shoots — different light, different background, different
style. Likely cause: The learner did not use the same light and background for all photos, or did not batch-edit for consistency.
How to investigate: Compare the photos side by side. Are the colours, brightness and style consistent? Corrective steps: Re-
shoot using the same light and background. Batch-edit for consistency. Review each photo individually. How to confirm
resolved: All photos in the set look like they belong together. How to prevent recurrence: For a themed set, use the same
light, background and editing style throughout.
K.3 Dishonest edits — changing reality
Symptoms: The edited photos show something that was not in the original — a replaced sky, a removed person, a reshaped
body, a changed product. Likely cause: The learner used editing to deceive — to make the photo look like something it is not.
How to investigate: Compare the edited photos to the originals. Are there elements that were not in the original? Corrective
steps: Undo the dishonest edits. Edit honestly — enhance, do not deceive. How to confirm resolved: The edited photos are
better versions of the originals, not different images. How to prevent recurrence: Follow the honest editing rules. If an edit
would mislead the viewer, do not do it.
K.4 Missing consent
Symptoms: The learner has photographed people without obtaining consent, or has not documented consent. Likely cause:
The learner does not understand the consent requirement, or feels awkward asking. How to investigate: Ask the learner: "Did
you ask permission before photographing that person? Do you have a signed consent form?" Corrective steps: Stop. Obtain
consent. If the photo has already been taken, ask the subject now. If they say no, delete the photo. How to confirm resolved:
Consent is obtained and documented for every person photographed. How to prevent recurrence: Make consent the first step
of every portrait or event shoot. No consent, no photo.
K.5 Incomplete portfolio — missing components
Symptoms: The mini portfolio is missing one or more required components — no title page, no contact sheet, no consent
note. Likely cause: The learner ran out of time or did not understand the requirements. How to investigate: Check the portfolio
against the six required components. Which are missing? Corrective steps: Add the missing components. Prioritise: title page,
set, consent note — the minimum. How to confirm resolved: All six components are present. How to prevent recurrence:
Use the portfolio checklist. Review before submitting.
K.6 Vague presentation — cannot explain choices
Symptoms: The learner says "I took some photos and edited them" — no specifics, no explanation of choices. Likely cause:
The learner did not prepare the presentation or does not understand the importance of explaining choices. How to investigate:
Ask the learner: "Why did you choose that light? Why did you crop that way? What did you refuse to fake?" Corrective steps:
Help the learner prepare. Use the Presentation Notes worksheet. Practice the presentation. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can explain every choice specifically and honestly. How to prevent recurrence: The presentation is a required
component — prepare it as carefully as the photos.
K.7 Rushing the shoot — not enough Strong photos
Symptoms: The learner has only 2–3 Strong photos — not enough for a 5–8 photo set. Likely cause: The learner rushed
through the shoot, did not take enough frames, or did not review as they went. How to investigate: Count the Strong photos. If
fewer than 5, the shoot was insufficient. Corrective steps: Re-shoot. Take more frames. Review as you go. Use the shooting
checklist. How to confirm resolved: The learner has 5–8 Strong photos. How to prevent recurrence: The capstone is not a
race. Take your time. Check each shot.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual photos,
portfolio and presentation, and confirm the cause before correcting.
