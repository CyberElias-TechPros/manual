# Shooting People, Products and Events

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: "See and Shoot" (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (practical, subject-focused)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–2 — the learner should be able to produce a sharp, correctly exposed photo, apply the rule of thirds, and identify good and bad light. The 5-step sharpness routine and the four composition techniques are assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1 and 2 you learned the technical and artistic foundations. Today you apply them to the three subjects that earn money: people (portraits), products (business images) and events (moments). This session closes Week 1's foundations folder.

**Preparation before class:** A willing colleague or mannequin for the portrait demonstration; a simple product (a shoe, a bag, a plate of food, a phone accessory) for the product demonstration; a plain surface (a table, a bedsheet, a piece of cardboard) and a white card or reflector for the product scene; printed consent forms for any person photographed; the "Foundations Folder Checklist" (Section H) printed per learner; a simulated event setup (a person pouring a drink, cutting a cake, or handing over an item) for the event demonstration. Instructor note on tone: This session is about confidence with subjects. Many learners are nervous about photographing people — they fear being intrusive or getting it wrong. Create a supportive atmosphere. Emphasise consent, kindness and clear communication. Celebrate every good portrait, even imperfect ones.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Put a subject at ease before photographing them, using simple communication and direction.
2. Direct a simple portrait pose — position the subject's body, hands and face for a natural, flattering result.
3. Produce a sharp, well-lit portrait using natural light, the rule of thirds and the 5-step sharpness routine.
4. Build a clean product scene — choose a surface, manage the background, control reflections and light the product
flatteringly.
5. Produce a usable product image suitable for a WhatsApp Business catalogue or Jiji listing.
6. Work a simulated event — capture key moments sharply, adapt to changing light and stay unobtrusive.
7. Confirm consent for every person photographed, using a printed consent form.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show three portraits of the same person:
Portrait A (poor): The person is standing against a bright window (backlit — face is dark), arms stiff at their sides, looking
uncomfortable, the background is cluttered, the photo is slightly blurry.
Portrait B (acceptable): The person is in open shade, looking at the camera, arms relaxed, but the pose is stiff and the
expression is awkward — like a passport photo.
Portrait C (strong): The person is near a window (soft light on their face), body turned slightly, hands doing something
natural (holding a cup, resting on a table), a genuine smile, clean background, sharp focus on the eyes.
What the instructor says:
"Same person. Three photos. One looks like a mistake, one looks like an ID card, and one looks like a professional portrait.
Hands up — which one would you use for a business profile? … Photo C, almost everyone. Now tell me: what is different
about Photo C?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "the light is better," "she is smiling," "she looks comfortable," "the
background is clean," "you can see her face," "she looks natural." Accept all answers and write the key phrases on the board:
light, pose, expression, background, comfort.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"A good portrait is not about the person being beautiful — it is about the photographer making them look their best. The
light, the pose, the background, the moment — all of those are the photographer's choices. Today you learn to make those
choices for people, products and events. By the end of this session, you will have a portrait, a product shot and an event
shot in your foundations folder."
Why this opener works: It uses the same person in all three photos, proving that the photographer — not the subject —
makes the difference. It introduces the three subjects (people, products, events) before any technical explanation.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four composition techniques we learned in Session 2?"
Expected answer: Rule of thirds, framing, leading lines, symmetry/perspective.
Common gap: Learners may remember rule of thirds but forget the others. Clarify: "Off-centre placement, using a natural
frame, using lines to lead the eye, and using symmetry or depth."
Question 2: "What are the four natural light conditions, and which ones are flattering for portraits?"
Expected answer: Golden hour (flattering), window light (flattering), open shade (flattering), harsh midday sun (unflattering
— avoid).
Common gap: Learners may say "more light is better." Clarify: "Soft, indirect light is flattering. Direct, harsh light creates
shadows and squinting. The best light is often the gentlest light."
Question 3: "What is the 5-step sharpness routine?"
Expected answer: Steady hold, tap to focus, check exposure, check background, take several frames.
Common gap: Learners may remember the steps but not the order. Clarify: "Steady, focus, exposure, background, frames
— in that order."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have the tools: sharpness, composition and light. Today we point those tools at the three things that pay: people,
products and events. These are the subjects clients pay for. A business owner pays for product photos. A family pays for
portraits. A church or school pays for event coverage. Today you prove you can deliver all three."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the four composition techniques, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the
board before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Photographing people — the portrait
The idea. A portrait is a photograph of a person that shows who they are — their character, their mood, their professionalism.
A good portrait makes the subject look their best: comfortable, confident and well-lit. A good portrait is not about the subject
being naturally photogenic — it is about the photographer creating the conditions for a good photo.
The three things that make a good portrait (teach these and return to them all session):
Element What it means How to get it right
Light The light on the
subject's face
Use soft, directional light — window light or open shade. Never harsh midday
sun. Never backlight (unless intentional).
Pose How the subject's body
is positioned
Give the subject something to do with their hands. Turn the body slightly. Ask them
to relax. Avoid stiff, straight-on poses.
Expression The subject's face and
emotion
Talk to the subject. Make them laugh. Capture a genuine moment, not a forced
smile. Take many frames — the best expression is often between poses.
Putting the subject at ease (the most important portrait skill):
Many learners are nervous about photographing people. This is normal. The solution is communication:
1. Introduce yourself. Tell the person your name and what you are doing. "Hello, I'm Chinedu, I'm learning photography and
I'd like to take your portrait. Is that okay?"
2. Explain what you need. "I'm going to ask you to stand near this window and just be yourself. I'll take a few photos and we'll
check them together."
3. Give direction, not orders. Instead of "stand there and smile," say "why don't you lean against the wall and look out the
window for a moment?" Direction gives the subject something to do; orders make them stiff.
4. Talk while you shoot. Do not shoot in silence. Ask about their day, their work, their family. The best expressions happen
when the subject is relaxed and engaged.
5. Show them the result. After taking a few photos, show the subject the screen. "Look — that one is beautiful. See how the
light catches your face?" This builds trust and makes them more comfortable.
Simple posing basics (for beginners):
Do not overcomplicate posing. These four steps work for almost everyone:
1. Turn the body slightly. Ask the subject to turn their body about 30–45 degrees away from the camera. This is more
flattering than standing straight on — it creates a natural line and avoids the "passport photo" look.
2. Give the hands something to do. Stiff arms at the sides look awkward. Ask the subject to hold something (a cup, a phone,
a book), rest their hands on a surface, or cross their arms loosely. Hands that are doing something look natural.
3. Angle the face. Ask the subject to turn their face slightly toward the light. If the light is coming from the left, they should
turn their face left. This ensures the face is well-lit.
4. Capture the moment between poses. The best expression is often not the posed smile — it is the laugh, the thoughtful
look, the natural moment between directions. Take many frames and capture these.
Light for portraits — the two best options:
Window light: Place the subject near a window, facing the window or at a 45-degree angle to it. The window provides soft,
directional light that is flattering on the face. Never place the subject with their back to the window (backlight — the face
goes dark).
Open shade: Place the subject in the shade of a tree or building, with a bright, open area in front of them. The shade
eliminates harsh shadows and squinting. The subject should be in shade, but the light in front of them should be bright.
Background for portraits:
Clean and uncluttered. A plain wall, a blurred background, or a simple outdoor setting.
Nothing growing out of the subject's head — no poles, no branches, no distracting objects.
Use the rule of thirds — place the subject's eyes on the top horizontal line, on one of the vertical lines.
Worked example:
Weak: A person standing straight on, arms at their sides, in harsh midday sun, against a cluttered background, with a forced
smile. The photo looks like a mugshot.
Strong: The same person, turned slightly, holding a cup, near a window with soft light on their face, against a clean wall,
with a genuine laugh. The photo looks like a professional portrait.
Real-life comparison. A good portrait is like a good conversation. If you stand stiffly and say nothing, the conversation is
awkward. If you relax, gesture naturally, and engage with the other person, the conversation flows. A portrait works the same
way — the photographer's job is to make the subject relax and then capture the natural moment.
Misconception to correct. "The subject needs to be naturally photogenic." No. A good photographer can make almost anyone
look good by controlling the light, the pose and the moment. Photogenic means "photographed well" — and that is the
photographer's work, not the subject's gift.
F.2 Photographing products — the business image
The idea. A product photograph is a photograph of an item for sale — for a WhatsApp Business catalogue, a Jiji listing, an
Instagram post or a flyer. A good product photo shows the product clearly, makes it looks appealing, and gives the buyer
confidence to purchase. A poor product photo — dark, blurry, cluttered — makes the buyer walk away.
The four elements of a good product photo (teach these and return to them all session):
Element What it means How to get it right
Surface What the product sits on A clean, plain surface — a white bedsheet, a wooden table, a piece of
cardboard. No clutter.
Background What is behind the
product
A plain, uncluttered background — a wall, a sheet hung up, a blurred
background. Nothing distracting.
Light How the product is lit Soft, even light — window light or open shade. No harsh shadows. Reflector
(white card) to fill shadows.
Element What it means How to get it right
Angle Where the camera is
positioned
Eye level or slightly above. Show the product's best side. For food, shoot from
above (flat lay) or at 45 degrees.
Building a product scene — step by step:
1. Choose the surface. Find a clean, plain surface — a table, a bed, a piece of cardboard. Cover it with a white sheet, a plain
cloth or a piece of paper if needed. The surface should be clean and uncluttered.
2. Set the background. Behind the product, place a plain background — a wall, a sheet hung up, or a large piece of paper.
The background should be plain and complementary to the product. For a dark product, use a light background. For a light
product, use a dark background.
3. Position the product. Place the product on the surface. Make sure it is clean — wipe off dust, fingerprints, smudges.
Arrange it at its best angle — the side that shows the product's shape, label or detail.
4. Set the light. Place the product near a window (soft light) or in open shade. If one side of the product is in shadow, use a
white card or reflector on the shadow side to bounce light back. This "fills" the shadows and makes the product look evenly
lit.
5. Compose the photo. Use the rule of thirds — place the product on a grid intersection. Use the 5-step sharpness routine.
Take several frames. Check the result by zooming in.
Managing reflections:
Shiny products (glass, metal, polished surfaces) create reflections — the camera, the photographer, the room.
To reduce reflections: shoot from a slight angle, not straight on. Use soft light (window light, not direct sun). Place a white
card on the opposite side of the product to fill in the shadow and balance the reflection.
To eliminate reflections on glass: place a black card or cloth behind the product (not visible to the camera) to create a clean,
dark background behind the glass.
Worked example — photographing a shoe:
Weak: A shoe on a cluttered floor, in a dim corner, with other shoes and objects around it. The shoe is dark, the background
is messy, the photo is blurry. The buyer cannot see the shoe clearly.
Strong: The same shoe, on a white sheet near a window, with a white card bouncing light onto the shadow side. The shoe is
sharp, well-lit, and the background is clean. The buyer can see every detail — the colour, the texture, the sole. This photo
makes the buyer want to purchase.
Real-life comparison. A product photo is like a shop window display. A good shop window is clean, well-lit, and shows the
product at its best — with nothing distracting from it. A poor shop window is cluttered, dim, and shows the product buried
among other items. Your job as a product photographer is to create a clean, well-lit "shop window" for every product.
Misconception to correct. "I need a photo studio and professional lighting to take good product photos." No. A window, a
white sheet and a cardboard reflector are enough to produce professional-quality product photos. The principles matter more
than the equipment.
F.3 Photographing events — the moment
The idea. An event is a gathering where things happen — a birthday party, a church service, a naming ceremony, a wedding, a
shop opening, a graduation. Event photography means capturing the key moments of the event — the smiles, the actions, the
emotions — sharply and well-lit, while staying unobtrusive.
The three challenges of event photography (teach these and return to them all session):
Challenge What it means How to handle it
Changing light The light changes — indoors to
outdoors, bright to dim, one side of
the room to the other
Check the light constantly. Adjust exposure as you move. Use
HDR for high-contrast scenes. Be ready to shoot in any light.
Moving subjects People move — walking, talking,
dancing, hugging
Use the 5-step sharpness routine. Take many frames. Capture
the moment, not just the pose. Be ready — anticipate what
will happen next.
Challenge What it means How to handle it
Staying unobtrusive You are part of the event but should
not dominate it
Do not block people's view. Do not use flash (unless
necessary and permitted). Move quietly. Be respectful.
Photograph people with their knowledge and consent.
The five key moments to capture at any event:
1. The preparation — before the event starts: setting up, decorating, arranging food, people arriving. These photos tell the
story of the event.
2. The greeting — people arriving, shaking hands, hugging, greeting each other. These photos capture the welcome and the
community.
3. The main action — the key moment: cutting the cake, handing over a gift, the first dance, the speech, the performance.
This is the moment everyone remembers.
4. The emotion — the reactions: laughter, tears, surprise, joy. These photos capture the feeling of the event.
5. The detail — the small things: the decorations, the food, the flowers, the dress, the invitations. These photos complete the
story.
How to work an event like a professional:
1. Arrive early. Get there before the event starts. Scout the location — find the best light, the best angles, the spots where the
key moments will happen.
2. Plan your shots. Think about the five key moments. Where will the cake be cut? Where will the speeches happen? Where
will people gather? Position yourself near those spots.
3. Stay ready. Keep your camera on. Keep your finger near the shutter. Anticipate what will happen next. The best moments
are often unexpected — a child running, a spontaneous laugh, a sudden embrace.
4. Take many frames. Events are unpredictable. Take many photos of every moment. One frame will be sharp, one will
capture the right expression, one will be perfect.
5. Be respectful. Do not block the view. Do not use flash if it will disturb people. Do not photograph anyone who has asked
not to be photographed. Consent is just as important at events as in the studio.
Light at events:
Indoor events often have mixed light — overhead lights, windows, sometimes coloured lights. Check the white balance
(the colour of the light) and adjust if needed.
Outdoor events may have changing light — sun moving behind clouds, shade moving. Check the exposure constantly.
If the light is very dim, use a higher ISO (on phones, this is automatic) or use a tripod or steady surface to avoid blur. Do not
use flash unless it is necessary and permitted — flash can be harsh and distracting.
Worked example — a birthday party:
Weak: Photos of people standing awkwardly, with the cake not visible, in harsh flash light, with the subject's eyes closed.
The photos look like an afterthought.
Strong: Photos of the decorations being set up, guests arriving and greeting each other, the cake being cut (with the knife
visible and the candles lit), the birthday person's reaction (joy, surprise), the food on the table, and a group photo of
everyone smiling. These photos tell the complete story of the event.
Real-life comparison. Event photography is like being a storyteller. A good storyteller does not just tell you what happened —
they show you the moments, the emotions, the details. Your photos are the story of the event. The client is not just buying
photos — they are buying the memory.
Misconception to correct. "Event photography means taking hundreds of photos and hoping some are good." No. A good
event photographer plans, anticipates and captures the key moments deliberately. Taking many frames is part of it — but the
frames are taken with intention, not randomly.
F.4 Consent and respect — the photographer's responsibility
The idea. Photography is a profession built on trust. Before you photograph a person — in a portrait, at an event, in a public
place — you must ask for their permission. This is not just politeness — it is a professional and ethical obligation.
The consent rule (read this aloud and make sure every learner understands):
Ask first. Before you photograph any person, ask: "May I take your photo?" If they say yes, proceed. If they say no, put the
camera down — no argument, no exceptions.
Under 18 — parent or guardian consent. For anyone under 18, you must have written consent from a parent or guardian
before you photograph them. Without it, do not shoot.
Show respect. If someone is uncomfortable, stop. If someone asks you to delete a photo, delete it. If someone does not
want to be photographed at an event, do not photograph them.
Use a consent form. For any person photographed for practice, portfolio or client use, have them sign a simple consent
form. This protects both you and the subject.
The consent form (simple version):
```text
CONSENT TO BE PHOTOGRAPHED
```
```text
I, ________________________ (name), agree to be photographed by
________________________ (photographer's name) on ________________ (date).
```
```text
I understand that the photographs may be used for:
[ ] Practice and learning
[ ] Portfolio
[ ] Client/business use
[ ] Social media
```
```text
I give my permission freely and can ask for the photos to be deleted at any time.
```
```text
Signature: ________________________
Date: ________________________
```
```text
For subjects under 18:
Parent/Guardian name: ________________________
Parent/Guardian signature: ________________________
Date: ________________________
```
Why consent matters:
It is respectful. People have the right to control how their image is used.
It is professional. A photographer who asks consent is trusted. A photographer who does not is avoided.
It is legal. In Nigeria, using someone's image without permission — especially for commercial purposes — can create
legal problems.
It builds your reputation. Clients hire photographers they trust. Trust starts with consent.
Misconception to correct. "If I am in a public place, I can photograph anyone." Not exactly. You can photograph public scenes
and buildings, but photographing identifiable people — especially for commercial use — requires consent. When in doubt,
ask.
F.5 Summary of the lecture
A good portrait has three elements: light, pose and expression. Put the subject at ease, use soft light, give direction, and
capture the moment.
A good product photo has four elements: surface, background, light and angle. Build a clean scene, use soft light, and
compose deliberately.
Event photography has three challenges: changing light, moving subjects and staying unobtrusive. Capture the five key
moments: preparation, greeting, main action, emotion and detail.
Consent is mandatory: ask first, get parent/guardian consent for under-18s, use a consent form, and respect people's
wishes.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A willing colleague or mannequin for the portrait.
A simple product (a shoe, a bag, a plate of food) and a plain surface (white sheet, table).
A white card or reflector.
A consent form (signed by the colleague before the demo).
A simulated event setup: a person pouring a drink or cutting a cake.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the portrait (5 minutes)
Say: "I am going to photograph Adaeze. Watch how I set up the light, direct the pose and capture the moment."
Step 1 — Light: Position Adaeze near a window. "Soft light from the window, falling on her face. She is not facing the window
directly — she is at a 45-degree angle. This gives depth to her face."
Step 2 — Background: Check the background. "Clean wall behind her. Nothing distracting."
Step 3 — Pose: "Adaeze, turn your body slightly to the left. Rest your hand on the table. Now turn your face toward the light
— yes, like that. Perfect."
Step 4 — Direction: "Now look out the window for a moment — just naturally. … Now look back at me. … Tell me about
your work — what do you do? … [Adaeze talks and laughs]"
Step 5 — Capture: Take several photos while Adaeze talks. Show the best one on the projector. "See — she is relaxed, the
light is soft on her face, her hands are doing something natural, and she is laughing. That is a portrait."
Say: "The difference between a snapshot and a portrait is not the camera — it is the time you take to make the person
comfortable. Talk to them, give them something to do, and capture the moment when they forget about the camera."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — the product scene (5 minutes)
Say: "Now I am going to photograph this shoe for a WhatsApp Business catalogue. Watch how I build the scene."
Step 1 — Surface: Lay a white sheet on the table. "Clean, plain surface. No clutter."
Step 2 — Background: Hang a white sheet behind the table. "Plain background. Nothing distracting."
Step 3 — Product: Place the shoe on the sheet. Wipe it clean. "The shoe is clean — no dust, no smudges. I am placing it at a
45-degree angle so you can see the shape and the label."
Step 4 — Light: Position the shoe near a window. "Soft light from the window. One side is in shadow — I am going to fill it
with a white card." Place a white card on the shadow side. "The card bounces light back onto the shadow side. Now the shoe is
evenly lit."
Step 5 — Compose: Use the rule of thirds. "The shoe is on a grid intersection. I am using the 5-step sharpness routine —
steady, focus, exposure, background, frames."
Step 6 — Capture: Take several photos. Show the best one. "Sharp, well-lit, clean background. This photo makes the buyer
want to purchase."
Say: "A product photo is not just a photo of a product — it is a sales tool. The cleaner the scene, the more professional the
product looks, and the more confident the buyer feels."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — the event moment (4 minutes)
Say: "Now a simulated event. Adaeze is going to pour a drink. Watch how I capture the moment."
Step 1 — Anticipate: "I know she is going to pour. I am positioning myself where I can see the action clearly — not too close,
not too far."
Step 2 — Light: "The light is coming from the side. I am checking the exposure — it looks good."
Step 3 — Capture: Adaeze pours the drink. Take several photos in quick succession. "I took five frames. One will be perfect —
the liquid mid-pour, the expression on her face, the glass filling up."
Step 4 — Review: Show the best frame. "Sharp, well-lit, the action is clear. That is the moment."
Say: "Event photography is about anticipation. You cannot control the moment — but you can be ready for it. Position
yourself, check the light, and take many frames."
G.5 Questions to ask during the demo
"What is the difference between a snapshot and a portrait?" (Time, communication, light, pose.)
"Why did I use a white card on the shadow side of the shoe?" (To fill the shadows — to bounce light back.)
"Why did I take five frames of the pour?" (One will be perfect — the others may be blurry or the expression may be wrong.)
"What is the most important thing about photographing people?" (Consent and respect.)
"Can you take good product photos without a studio?" (Yes — a window, a sheet and a card are enough.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner produces a portrait, a product shot and an event shot,
completing the foundations folder.
Starting materials: Learner's own phone; a classmate who has agreed to be photographed (with signed consent form); a simple
product (a shoe, a bag, a bottle, a book); a plain surface and background; a white card or reflector; the printed worksheets.
Procedure — Part 1: Portrait (6 minutes)
1. Ask a classmate to be your subject. Explain what you are doing. Have them sign the consent form.
2. Position your subject near a window or in open shade. Check the light on their face.
3. Direct the pose: turn the body slightly, give the hands something to do, angle the face toward the light.
4. Talk to your subject. Make them comfortable. Take at least five photos.
5. Review the photos. Pick the strongest one. Tick the Portrait Checklist.
Procedure — Part 2: Product (6 minutes)
1. Choose a product — your bag, a bottle, a book, a shoe.
2. Build the scene: clean surface, plain background, product positioned at its best angle.
3. Set the light: near a window or in open shade. Use a white card to fill shadows.
4. Compose using the rule of thirds. Use the 5-step sharpness routine. Take at least five photos.
5. Review the photos. Pick the strongest one. Fill in the Product Scene Planner.
Procedure — Part 3: Event (6 minutes)
1. Work in pairs. One person is the "event" — they will perform a simple action (pouring a drink, opening a book, handing
over an item). The other is the photographer.
2. The photographer positions themselves, checks the light, and anticipates the action.
3. The "event" person performs the action. The photographer takes at least five photos in quick succession.
4. Review the photos. Pick the strongest one. Fill in the Event Moment Log.
5. Swap roles and repeat.
Expected result: One portrait, one product shot and one event shot, each with a completed worksheet.
Points to pause and predict:
After the portrait: "Is your subject comfortable? If not, what could you do to make them more relaxed?"
After the product: "Is the background clean? Is the product well-lit? Would a buyer want to purchase from this photo?"
After the event: "Did you capture the action clearly? Is the photo sharp? Did you take enough frames?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner's subject looks stiff → Suggest giving the hands something to do, or asking the subject to talk about something
they enjoy.
Product photo has harsh shadows → Have them use the white card to fill the shadow side.
Event photo is blurry → Check if the subject moved too fast. Suggest taking more frames, or asking the subject to move
more slowly.
Learner has not obtained consent → Stop the exercise. Remind them of the consent rule. No consent, no photo.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Portrait taken — subject comfortable, well-lit, sharp
Consent form signed for the portrait subject
Product shot taken — clean scene, well-lit, sharp
Event shot taken — action captured sharply
Portrait Checklist completed
Product Scene Planner completed
Event Moment Log completed
All three photos saved in the foundations folder

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama Nkechi runs a small food business in Port Harcourt — Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices. She sells homemade
soups, spices and pepper soup base through WhatsApp and Instagram. She wants to post photos of her products to attract
more customers, but she has never taken a product photo before. She asks her nephew, Chinedu, who has a smartphone, to
take some photos.
Relevant facts.
Chinedu has a recent smartphone with a good camera.
He has never studied photography — he just points and shoots.
Mama Nkechi's products are in plastic containers and glass jars, with handwritten labels.
The photos are taken in her kitchen, under fluorescent lights, with a cluttered background (pots, pans, ingredients).
The photos are dark, the labels are hard to read, and the products look unappealing.
Decision point. What should Chinedu do differently to produce photos that will help Mama Nkechi's business?
Possible approaches.
1. Take the photos in the same way but use a brighter flash.
2. Learn to build a clean product scene — use natural light, a clean surface, a plain background, and compose deliberately.
3. Ask a professional photographer to take the photos for Mama Nkechi.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Chinedu's phone is already capable of good product photos. His problem is the scene
— cluttered background, poor light, no composition. By moving the products near a window, using a clean surface and
background, and composing with the rule of thirds, he can produce photos that make Mama Nkechi's products look
professional and appealing.
Specific fixes for Chinedu:
Move the products near a window (soft, natural light).
Use a clean, plain surface — a white sheet or a clean table.
Use a plain background — a wall or a sheet hung up.
Wipe the containers clean — remove smudges and fingerprints.
Position the products at their best angle — labels facing the camera.
Use the rule of thirds — place the product on a grid intersection.
Take several frames and pick the sharpest.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (brighter flash) makes the photo brighter but does not fix the cluttered background or the unflattering light.
Flash creates harsh shadows and makes the products look worse.
Approach 2 is free, takes a few hours to learn, and the skill stays with Chinedu forever. He can photograph every new
product himself.
Approach 3 works but costs money every time. Mama Nkechi has many products; hiring a photographer for each one is not
sustainable.
Professional connection. Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices is the running example for the capstone portfolio (Track A —
Product). The skills Chinedu learns today — building a clean product scene, using natural light, composing deliberately — are
exactly the skills he will use for the capstone. This is not just a class exercise; this is real preparation for a real photo job.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Stiff, awkward portraits
Symptoms: The subject looks uncomfortable — arms stiff, forced smile, staring at the camera. Likely cause: The photographer
did not communicate with the subject or give direction. How to investigate: Ask the photographer: "Did you talk to your
subject? Did you give them something to do?" Corrective steps: Talk to the subject. Give direction — "turn slightly, rest your
hand on the table, look out the window." Take photos while the subject is relaxed and engaged. How to confirm resolved: The
subject looks natural and comfortable in the photo. How to prevent recurrence: Always talk to your subject. Never shoot in
silence.
K.2 Harsh light on the subject's face
Symptoms: Dark shadows under the eyes, nose and chin. The subject is squinting. Likely cause: Photographing in direct
midday sun or under a harsh overhead light. How to investigate: Check the direction of the shadows. If they are directly under
the subject, the light is overhead. Corrective steps: Move the subject into open shade or near a window. Use a reflector to fill
shadows if needed. How to confirm resolved: The subject's face is evenly lit, no squinting. How to prevent recurrence:
Always check the light before shooting. Avoid harsh overhead light.
K.3 Cluttered product backgrounds
Symptoms: The product is lost among other objects — pots, pans, tools, clutter. Likely cause: The photographer did not clean
up the scene before shooting. How to investigate: Look at the photo — can you identify the product immediately?
Corrective steps: Remove everything from the scene except the product and the surface. Use a plain background. How to
confirm resolved: The product is the clear focus of the photo. How to prevent recurrence: Before every product shot, scan
the entire frame and remove distractions.
K.4 Blurry event photos
Symptoms: The action is blurred — the subject is smeared or the whole photo is soft. Likely cause: The subject moved during
the exposure, or the camera was not steady. How to investigate: Is the background sharp but the subject blurry? If so, the
subject moved. Is everything blurry? If so, the camera moved. Corrective steps: For moving subjects, take many frames and
pick the sharpest. For camera shake, hold the phone steadier or use a surface. How to confirm resolved: The action is captured
sharply. How to prevent recurrence: At events, always take many frames. Hold the phone steady.
K.5 Not obtaining consent
Symptoms: The photographer takes photos of people without asking. Likely cause: The photographer does not understand the
consent rule or feels awkward asking. How to investigate: Ask the photographer: "Did you ask permission before taking that
photo?" Corrective steps: Stop the exercise. Remind the learner of the consent rule. No consent, no photo. Have them ask the
subject and sign the consent form. How to confirm resolved: The subject has given permission and signed the form. How to
prevent recurrence: Make consent the first step of every portrait or event shoot. Never skip it.
K.6 Product photos that do not show the product clearly
Symptoms: The product is too dark, the label is unreadable, or the angle hides the product's best features. Likely cause: Poor
light, poor angle, or a dirty product. How to investigate: Look at the photo — can you see the product clearly? Can you read
the label? Corrective steps: Move the product to better light. Clean the product. Change the angle to show the product's best
side. How to confirm resolved: The product is clearly visible, well-lit, and the label is readable. How to prevent recurrence:
Before every product shot, check the light, clean the product, and choose the best angle.
K.7 Missing the key moment at events
Symptoms: The photographer captures the setup and the aftermath but misses the main action — the cake cutting, the
handshake, the speech. Likely cause: The photographer was not paying attention or was not positioned correctly. How to
investigate: Ask the photographer: "Where were you standing? Were you ready when the moment happened?" Corrective
steps: Position yourself near where the key moment will happen. Anticipate what will come next. Keep your finger near the
shutter. How to confirm resolved: The key moment is captured sharply. How to prevent recurrence: At events, always be
ready. Anticipate, position, and take many frames.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual photo, ask
the photographer what they did, and confirm the cause before correcting.

INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. The three things that make a good portrait are: light (soft, flattering light on the face), pose (natural body position, hands
doing something) and expression (genuine emotion, not a forced smile). Explanation of any one: e.g., light means using soft,
directional light — window light or open shade — so the face is evenly lit without harsh shadows.

A6. Three things: (1) Talk to the subject — ask about their day, their work, their interests. (2) Give direction, not orders — "turn
slightly, rest your hand on the table, look out the window." (3) Give the hands something to do — hold a cup, rest on a surface,
cross arms loosely. (Also acceptable: show them the photos as you go, make them laugh, take photos while they are engaged in

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"A good portrait depends on the subject being photogenic." → No — the photographer creates the conditions.

the results.

predicted moment.
