# Basic Photo Editing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: "Process and Deliver"

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (practical, software-focused)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3 — the learner should have a set of raw photos (the foundations folder) and basic smartphone ability. No prior editing experience assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–3 you learned to shoot sharp, well-lit, well-composed photos. Now you learn to edit them — to crop, straighten, correct exposure, adjust colour, remove blemishes and batch-edit a set for consistency. Editing is where good photos become professional.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers with internet access (for downloading apps if needed); the instructor's phone or computer connected to the projector for live editing demonstration; Snapseed installed on the instructor's device (primary editor); Lightroom Mobile installed as a secondary option; a sample set of raw photos (product, portrait, event) for the demonstration; the "Edit Decision Log" (Section H) printed per learner; a deliberately over-edited sample image for the "honest editing" discussion. Instructor note on tone: This session must balance skill with ethics. Learners are excited about editing — they want to make their photos look amazing. Channel that excitement into honest, disciplined editing. The message is: "Do less, but do it well." Editing should enhance the photo, not transform it into something it is not.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Import and organise photos into a working folder for editing.
2. Crop and straighten a photo to improve composition and remove distractions.
3. Correct exposure — brighten dark photos, recover highlights, and balance the overall brightness.
4. Adjust colour temperature and saturation — make colours natural and appealing, not artificial.
5. Perform basic retouching and blemish removal — clean up small distractions without destroying natural skin texture.
6. Batch-edit a set of photos so they have a consistent look — matching white balance, contrast and colour.
7. State clearly what editing can fix, what it cannot fix, and what must never be faked.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner with a working camera and the foundations folder of raw photos.
Academy computers (optional) for learners who prefer editing on a larger screen.
Instructor's phone or computer connected to a projector for live editing demonstration.
Software
Snapseed (free, primary editor) — available on Android and iOS. Download from Google Play Store or Apple App Store.
Lightroom Mobile (free tier, secondary editor) — available on Android and iOS. Used for the batch editing demonstration.
No paid software required. No subscription required.
Accounts
None required for Snapseed. A free Adobe account is needed for Lightroom Mobile (optional).
Sample files
A sample set of raw photos (product, portrait, event) for the instructor demonstration — taken by the instructor or
sourced from free/royalty-free images.
A deliberately over-edited sample image — a photo with extreme saturation, heavy smoothing, replaced sky, or obvious
fakery — for the "honest editing" discussion.
Worksheets
Worksheet 4.1 — "Edit Decision Log" (a table: photo name, problem, edit applied, result).
Worksheet 4.2 — "Before and After Comparison" (a table: photo, what I changed, why, honest editing check).
Tools
A whiteboard and markers for drawing the editing workflow diagram.
Internet
Required for downloading Snapseed and Lightroom Mobile (if not already installed). Not required for the editing itself —
both apps work offline once installed.
Alternative for learners who lack a capable phone
Any smartphone can run Snapseed and Lightroom Mobile. A learner without a phone may use an academy computer with
a web-based editor (e.g., Photopea — free, browser-based) or pair with a classmate. They must tell the instructor at the
start so support can be arranged.
Ethics note: Editing must be honest. Do not change a product's true appearance, fake a background, reshape a body beyond
normal correction, or edit in a way that lies to a client or viewer. Editing cleans up a photograph; it does not manufacture a false
one.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two versions of the same photo:
Photo A (raw): A portrait of a person, slightly crooked, a bit dark, with a small distraction in the background (a trash can, a
stray object). The photo is good but not great.
Photo B (edited): The same portrait, straightened, brightened, the distraction removed, the colours natural and warm. The
photo looks professional.
What the instructor says:
"Same photo. Same person. Same moment. But one looks like it was taken by a beginner, and one looks like it was taken by
a professional. The difference is not the camera — it is the editing. Hands up — which photo would you use for a
business profile? … The edited one, almost everyone. Now tell me: what is different about the edited photo?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "it is brighter," "it is straight," "the background is cleaner," "the colours
look better," "it looks professional." Accept all answers and write the key phrases on the board: straight, bright, clean, natural,
professional.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"Editing is not cheating. Editing is part of photography. A raw photo is like a rough draft — it has potential, but it needs
work. Today you learn to edit your photos in Snapseed — a free app you can download today. You will learn to crop,
straighten, correct exposure, adjust colour, remove distractions and batch-edit a set. But you will also learn the most
important rule: edit honestly. Do less, but do it well."
Why this opener works: It uses the same photo in both versions, proving that editing — not the camera — makes the
difference. It introduces the key editing skills before any technical explanation, and it sets the ethical tone from the start.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five quality words for a good photograph?"
Expected answer: Clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose.
Common gap: Learners may forget one or two. Clarify: "Clarity = sharp. Light = correctly exposed. Composition =
intentional arrangement. Moment = right instant. Purpose = does the job."
Question 2: "What is the 5-step sharpness routine?"
Expected answer: Steady hold, tap to focus, check exposure, check background, take several frames.
Common gap: Learners may remember the steps but not the order. Clarify: "Steady, focus, exposure, background, frames."
Question 3: "What are the four natural light conditions, and which ones are flattering?"
Expected answer: Golden hour (flattering), window light (flattering), open shade (flattering), harsh midday sun
(unflattering).
Common gap: Learners may say "more light is better." Clarify: "Soft, indirect light is flattering. Direct, harsh light creates
shadows and squinting."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have learned to shoot well — sharp, correctly exposed, well-composed photos in good light. But even the best
photographers edit their photos. A raw photo is a starting point, not a finished product. Today you learn to edit — to take
your good photos and make them great. But you also learn the limits: editing can fix small problems, not big ones. And
editing must always be honest."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five quality words, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the board before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What editing is and why it matters
The idea. Editing is the process of adjusting a photo after it has been taken — improving the composition, correcting the
exposure, adjusting the colour and removing distractions. It is the second half of photography: shooting is capturing the image;
editing is refining it.
Plain-English definition. Editing is making deliberate changes to a photo to improve it — to make it sharper, brighter, cleaner
and more appealing, while keeping it honest and true to the original scene.
Why it matters. Even the best photographers edit their photos. A raw photo straight from the camera is rarely perfect — it may
be slightly crooked, slightly dark, slightly off-colour, or have a small distraction in the background. Editing fixes these small
problems and makes the photo look professional. For business, a well-edited photo makes the product or person look more
appealing — which means more sales.
What editing can fix:
Cropping — removing distractions at the edges of the frame, improving composition.
Straightening — fixing a tilted horizon or a crooked building.
Exposure — brightening a dark photo, recovering detail in bright areas.
Colour — adjusting the warmth or coolness of the photo, making colours natural.
Distractions — removing small objects (a trash can, a stray wire, a blemish).
Sharpness — enhancing detail slightly (but cannot fix a blurry photo).
What editing cannot fix:
A blurry subject — if the subject is out of focus, editing cannot make it sharp.
Poor composition — if the subject is lost in a cluttered scene, cropping can help but cannot completely rescue the photo.
Bad light — if the light is harsh or the subject is a silhouette, editing can improve it slightly but cannot transform it.
A missed moment — if the subject blinked or the action is over, editing cannot bring it back.
What editing must never do (the honest editing rules):
Do not change a product's true appearance — do not make a faded product look new, or a small product look large.
Do not fake a background — do not replace the sky, add elements that were not there, or remove people.
Do not reshape a body beyond normal correction — do not slim a person, enlarge eyes, or alter facial features.
Do not mislead the viewer — if a client or viewer would be deceived by the edit, do not do it.
Real-life comparison. Editing is like grooming. A person can shower, comb their hair, wear clean clothes and look their best —
but they are still the same person. Editing does the same for a photo: it cleans up the presentation without changing the reality.
But just as you cannot make a short person tall with grooming, you cannot make a blurry photo sharp with editing.
Misconception to correct. "Editing is cheating — real photographers do not edit." False. Almost all professional photographs
are edited — from magazine covers to wedding photos to product catalogues. The question is not whether to edit, but how to
edit honestly.

The idea. Editing is not random — it follows a logical order. Each step builds on the previous one. If you edit in the wrong
order, you may undo your own work or create problems.

Real-life comparison. The editing workflow is like building a house. You do not paint the walls before you build them. You
build the structure (crop and straighten), then install the electrical (exposure), then paint (colour), then add the finishing
touches (retouch), then polish (sharpen). Each step depends on the one before it.
Misconception to correct. "I can just use an auto-fix button and be done." Auto-fix is a starting point, not a finished product. It
may improve the photo slightly, but it cannot make the decisions that make a photo look professional. Learn the workflow — it
takes only a little longer and produces much better results.
F.3 Cropping and straightening
The idea. Cropping means cutting away parts of the photo — usually the edges — to improve the composition. Straightening
means rotating the photo slightly to fix tilted lines — a crooked horizon, a leaning building, a slanted table.
When to crop:
To remove distractions at the edges — a trash can, a stray object, a person's arm, empty space.
To improve the composition — move the subject to a grid intersection, create more space in the direction the subject is
facing.
To change the aspect ratio — make the photo square (1:1) for Instagram, or wider (16:9) for a banner.
When not to crop:
Do not crop so tightly that you lose important context — a portrait needs some space around the subject, a product shot
needs some breathing room.
Do not crop to fix a blurry photo — cropping a blurry photo makes it smaller and blurrier, not sharper.
How to crop in Snapseed:
1. Open the photo in Snapseed.
2. Tap Tools > Crop.
3. Choose an aspect ratio (Free, 1:1, 4:3, 16:9, etc.) or drag the corners freely.
4. Drag the frame to position the subject where you want it.
5. Tap the checkmark to apply.
How to straighten in Snapseed:
1. Open the photo in Snapseed.
2. Tap Tools > Rotate.
3. Use the slider to rotate the photo until the lines are straight.
4. Tap the checkmark to apply.
Worked example:
Raw: A portrait of a person, slightly tilted, with a trash can in the bottom left corner and too much empty space above the
person's head.
Edited: Cropped to remove the trash can and the empty space, straightened so the background lines are horizontal, with the
person's eyes on the top third line. The photo looks intentional and professional.
Real-life comparison. Cropping is like framing a picture for the wall. You choose the best part of the image and cut away the
rest. A good crop makes the subject stand out and removes anything that distracts.
Common mistake. Cropping too aggressively — cutting so much that the photo becomes a tiny, pixelated mess. Always check
the resolution after cropping. If the photo looks soft or pixelated, you have cropped too much.
F.4 Correcting exposure
The idea. Exposure correction means adjusting the brightness of the photo — making dark areas brighter, bright areas darker,
and the overall image balanced. The goal is a photo where you can see detail in both the shadows and the highlights — not
too dark, not too bright.
The three exposure adjustments (teach these and return to them all session):
Adjustment What it does When to use it
Brightness Makes the whole photo brighter or darker Use first — to set the overall brightness
Highlights Adjusts only the brightest areas of the photo Use to recover detail in bright areas (a bright sky, a window)
Shadows Adjusts only the darkest areas of the photo Use to brighten dark areas (a face in shadow, a dark corner)
How to correct exposure in Snapseed:
1. Open the photo in Snapseed.
2. Tap Tools > Tune Image.
3. Swipe up or down to choose Brightness, Highlights or Shadows.
4. Swipe left or right to adjust.
5. Tap the checkmark to apply.
The exposure correction workflow:
1. First, adjust Brightness to set the overall level.
2. Then, adjust Highlights to recover detail in bright areas.
3. Then, adjust Shadows to brighten dark areas.
4. Check the result — is the subject clearly visible? Are there any pure white or pure black areas with no detail?
Worked example:
Raw: A portrait where the person's face is slightly dark (in shadow) and the background is slightly bright.
Edited: Brightness increased slightly, Shadows increased to brighten the face, Highlights decreased slightly to recover detail
in the background. The result is a balanced photo where both the face and the background have visible detail.
Real-life comparison. Exposure correction is like adjusting the lights in a room. If the room is too dim, you turn up the
brightness. If one corner is too dark, you add a lamp (shadows). If one area is too bright, you dim it (highlights). The goal is
even, comfortable light throughout.
Common mistake. Over-correcting — brightening a dark photo so much that it looks washed out and unnatural. Small
adjustments are usually better than large ones. If the photo needs a huge exposure correction, it was probably badly exposed in
the camera — prevention is better than cure.
F.5 Adjusting colour — white balance and saturation
The idea. Colour adjustment means making the colours in the photo look natural and appealing. Two adjustments matter for
beginners: white balance (the warmth or coolness of the photo) and saturation (the intensity of the colours).
White balance:
Every light source has a colour — sunlight is warm (golden), shade is cool (blue), fluorescent light is greenish,
incandescent light is orange.
White balance adjusts the photo so that white objects look white — not blue, not orange, not green.
If the photo looks too warm (too orange/yellow), cool it down. If it looks too cool (too blue), warm it up.
In Snapseed: Tools > White Balance — adjust the temperature slider.
Saturation:
Saturation controls how intense the colours are.
Low saturation = muted, subtle colours. High saturation = vivid, intense colours.
The goal is natural, appealing colours — not oversaturated, cartoonish colours.
In Snapseed: Tools > Tune Image > Saturation — adjust the slider.
The colour adjustment workflow:
1. First, set the white balance — make the colours look natural.
2. Then, adjust saturation — make the colours appealing but not artificial.
3. Check the result — do the colours look like the real scene, or do they look fake?
Worked example:
Raw: A product photo taken under fluorescent light — the product looks greenish and unnatural.
Edited: White balance adjusted to remove the green cast, saturation increased slightly to make the product's colours pop.
The result is a photo where the product looks natural and appealing.
Real-life comparison. White balance is like choosing the right light bulb. A warm bulb makes a room feel cozy, a cool bulb
makes it feel clean. You choose the bulb that fits the mood. In photography, you choose the white balance that makes the
colours look true to the scene.
Common mistake. Oversaturation — cranking the saturation so high that the colours look cartoonish and unnatural. A little
saturation goes a long way. The goal is to make the photo look like a better version of reality, not a different reality.
F.6 Retouching and blemish removal
The idea. Retouching means removing small distractions and imperfections — blemishes on skin, dust spots on a product, a
stray object in the background. The goal is to clean up the photo without changing the subject's appearance.
What retouching can do:
Remove a pimple or blemish on skin (while keeping natural skin texture).
Remove a dust spot or smudge on a product.
Remove a small distraction in the background (a wire, a leaf, a piece of litter).
Remove a stray hair or a piece of lint on clothing.
What retouching must not do:
Do not smooth skin so much that it looks plastic — keep natural texture.
Do not remove wrinkles, scars or moles that are part of the person's identity.
Do not remove or add elements that change the story of the photo.
Do not reshape the body or face.
How to retouch in Snapseed:
1. Open the photo in Snapseed.
2. Tap Tools > Healing.
3. Zoom in on the blemish or distraction.
4. Tap or brush over the area you want to remove.
5. Snapseed automatically fills the area with surrounding texture.
6. Tap the checkmark to apply.
The retouching rule (say this slowly):
"Retouch to clean up, not to change. Remove the pimple, not the person. Remove the dust spot, not the detail. Keep the
texture, keep the truth."
Worked example:
Raw: A portrait with a small pimple on the person's forehead and a dust spot on their shirt.
Edited: The pimple and dust spot are removed using the Healing tool. The skin still has natural texture — it does not look
airbrushed. The person looks like themselves, just cleaned up.
Real-life comparison. Retouching is like cleaning a window. You remove the dust and smudges so you can see through clearly
— but you do not change what is on the other side of the glass. Retouching removes distractions so the subject stands out —
but it does not change the subject.
Common mistake. Over-retouching — smoothing skin so much that it looks like plastic, or removing so much that the person
looks like a different person. The goal is a clean, natural look — not an artificial one.
F.7 Batch editing — making a set consistent
The idea. When you have a set of photos — for a product catalogue, a portfolio, or a client delivery — they should look like
they belong together. They should have the same white balance, the same contrast, the same overall feel. Batch editing means
applying the same edits to multiple photos so they look consistent.
Why consistency matters:
A set of product photos with different colours and brightness looks unprofessional — like they were taken by different
people.
A portfolio with inconsistent edits looks careless — like the photographer did not pay attention.
A client expects a cohesive set — photos that look like they came from the same shoot.
How to batch edit in Snapseed:
1. Edit one photo in the set — crop, exposure, colour, retouch.
2. Tap the Stack icon (three stacked squares) in the top right.
3. Tap Copy edits — this saves the edits you made.
4. Open the next photo in the set.
5. Tap the Stack icon > Paste edits — the same edits are applied.
6. Review the result — make small adjustments if needed (each photo may need slight tweaks).
7. Repeat for all photos in the set.
How to batch edit in Lightroom Mobile (alternative):
1. Edit one photo in the set.
2. Tap the three dots (…) > Copy settings.
3. Select the other photos in the set.
4. Tap Paste settings — the same edits are applied to all selected photos.
5. Review each photo and make small adjustments if needed.
The batch editing rule:
"Copy the edits, paste them, then review each photo individually. Batch editing gets you 90% of the way — the last 10% is
checking each photo and making small adjustments."
Worked example:
A set of five product photos for Mama Nkechi's Soups & Spices. Each photo was taken in the same light, but small
differences in angle and distance mean the exposure and colour vary slightly.
The first photo is edited — white balance set, exposure corrected, saturation adjusted. The edits are copied and pasted to
the other four photos. Each photo is reviewed and small adjustments are made. The result is a set of five photos that look
like they came from the same shoot — consistent, professional, ready for the client.
Real-life comparison. Batch editing is like making a batch of garri. You mix the ingredients once, then pour into individual
cups. Each cup is slightly different — a bit more here, a bit less there — but they all taste the same. Batch editing does the
same for photos: one set of edits, applied to all, with small individual adjustments.
Common mistake. Pasting edits without reviewing — assuming the same edits work perfectly for every photo. Each photo is
different — different angle, different light, different distance. Always review and adjust.
F.8 The honest editing line — what must never be faked
The idea. Editing is a tool for improvement, not deception. There is a line between honest editing (cleaning up the photo) and
dishonest editing (changing the reality). Every photographer must know where that line is.

Why these rules matter:
They protect the client — the client pays for an honest representation of their product or themselves.
They protect the viewer — the viewer deserves to see the truth.
They protect the photographer — a photographer who is caught faking edits loses their reputation and their business.
They protect the profession — photography is built on trust. Dishonest editing destroys that trust.
The over-edited photo (show this on the projector):
A photo with extreme saturation (colours look cartoonish), heavy skin smoothing (skin looks plastic), a replaced sky (the sky
is clearly fake), and a reshaped body (the person looks unnaturally thin).
Ask the class: "What is wrong with this photo?" They should identify the fakery.
Say: "This is what happens when editing crosses the line. The photo looks fake, the person looks fake, and the viewer feels

Real-life comparison. Honest editing is like putting on makeup. A little makeup can enhance your natural features and make
you look your best. But so much makeup that you look like a different person is not enhancement — it is disguise. The goal is
to look like the best version of yourself, not a different person.
Misconception to correct. "If the client wants it, I should do it." No. A client may ask for dishonest edits — a faked
background, a reshaped body, a changed product. The professional photographer explains why the edit is dishonest and offers
an honest alternative. If the client insists, the photographer walks away. Honesty is not negotiable.
F.9 Summary of the lecture
Editing is the second half of photography — refining the raw photo into a finished image.
The editing workflow: crop and straighten, correct exposure, adjust colour, retouch, sharpen.
Cropping removes distractions and improves composition. Straightening fixes tilted lines.
Exposure correction balances brightness — brightness, highlights and shadows.
Colour adjustment makes colours natural — white balance and saturation.
Retouching removes small distractions — blemishes, dust spots, stray objects.
Batch editing makes a set consistent — copy edits, paste, review each photo.
The honest editing line: do not change a product's appearance, fake a background, reshape a body, or mislead the viewer.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Blessing is a photographer in Port Harcourt. She was hired to photograph a small restaurant's menu items for their
Instagram page. She took the photos, edited them in Snapseed, and delivered them. The restaurant owner was happy — the
photos looked professional and the food looked appetising. But then a customer visited the restaurant, ordered a dish, and
posted a photo of the real dish next to Blessing's photo. The real dish looked nothing like the photo — the portion was smaller,
the colours were duller, and the presentation was different. The customer accused the restaurant of false advertising.
Relevant facts.
Blessing edited the photos honestly — she did not change the food's appearance or fake anything.
But she increased the saturation significantly, making the colours look more vivid than reality.
She used a slight blur on the background to make the food stand out — this is a common and acceptable technique.
She did not tell the restaurant owner that she had increased the saturation.
Decision point. Did Blessing do something wrong? If so, what?
Possible approaches.
1. Blessing did nothing wrong — all photographers edit their photos, and the restaurant owner approved the final images.
2. Blessing should have been more careful with the saturation — the colours should look natural, not exaggerated.
3. Blessing should have told the restaurant owner what edits she made, so the owner could decide if they were acceptable.
Recommended solution. Approaches 2 and 3. Blessing's editing crossed the line from enhancement to exaggeration. The
saturation was increased so much that the food looked different from reality. While she did not fake anything, she changed the
food's appearance enough to mislead the customer. The lesson: edit to make the photo look like the best version of reality —
not a different reality. And always communicate with the client about significant edits.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 ignores the customer's experience — the customer felt deceived, and the restaurant's reputation was
damaged.
Approach 2 is the minimum — the colours should look natural, not exaggerated. Small adjustments are usually better.
Approach 3 is the professional standard — the client should know what edits were made and have the right to approve or
request changes.
Professional connection. This case study illustrates the honest editing line in practice. Editing is a tool for improvement, not
deception. A photographer who edits honestly — enhancing the photo without changing the reality — builds trust with
clients and viewers. A photographer who edits dishonestly — exaggerating, faking, or misleading — loses that trust. The
honest editing line is not just an ethical rule — it is a business strategy.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Over-editing — colours look cartoonish
Symptoms: The photo looks unnatural — colours are too vivid, skin looks plastic, the overall image looks fake. Likely cause:
Saturation increased too much, or skin smoothing applied too heavily. How to investigate: Compare the edited photo to the
original. Does the edited version look like a better version of reality, or a different reality? Corrective steps: Reduce the
saturation. Reduce the skin smoothing. Aim for natural, appealing colours — not artificial ones. How to confirm resolved: The
photo looks like a better version of the original, not a different image. How to prevent recurrence: Make small adjustments.
Step back and look at the photo after each edit. If it looks too much, reduce it.
K.2 Cropping too aggressively
Symptoms: The photo is very small, pixelated, or has lost important context. Likely cause: Cropping so much that the
resolution is too low, or cutting away important parts of the scene. How to investigate: Check the photo's resolution after
cropping. Is it still high enough for the intended use? Corrective steps: Crop less. If you need a tighter crop, take the photo
from closer next time — do not rely on cropping to fix a distant subject. How to confirm resolved: The photo is sharp and has
sufficient resolution. How to prevent recurrence: Before cropping, ask: "Do I need this much crop? Can I achieve the same
result by moving closer?"
K.3 Inconsistent batch edits
Symptoms: The set of photos looks mismatched — different colours, different brightness, different overall feel. Likely cause:
The photos were taken in different light, and the batch edits were pasted without reviewing each photo individually. How to
investigate: Compare the photos side by side. Are the colours and brightness consistent? Corrective steps: Review each photo
individually. Make small adjustments to white balance, exposure and saturation as needed. How to confirm resolved: All
photos in the set look like they came from the same shoot. How to prevent recurrence: After pasting batch edits, always
review each photo and adjust.
K.4 Over-retouching — skin looks plastic
Symptoms: The person's skin looks unnaturally smooth — like plastic or airbrushed. Likely cause: The Healing tool or a
smoothing filter was applied too heavily. How to investigate: Zoom in on the skin. Can you see natural texture — pores, fine
lines, skin grain? Corrective steps: Reduce the retouching. Keep natural skin texture. Remove blemishes, not texture. How to
confirm resolved: The skin looks clean but natural — like the person, not a mannequin. How to prevent recurrence: Use the
Healing tool sparingly. Remove the blemish, not the skin.
K.5 Editing a blurry photo
Symptoms: The photographer spends time editing a blurry photo, trying to make it sharp. Likely cause: The photo was out of
focus or the subject moved during the exposure. How to investigate: Zoom in on the subject. Is it sharp or blurry? Corrective
steps: Accept that editing cannot fix a blurry photo. Use the photo only if the blur is minor and the composition is strong.
Otherwise, discard it and retake. How to confirm resolved: The photographer stops trying to fix the unfixable and focuses on
prevention — better focus technique, faster shutter, or a steadier hold. How to prevent recurrence: Always focus-check in
the camera. Take sharp photos from the start.
K.6 Faking edits — changing reality
Symptoms: The edited photo shows something that was not in the original — a replaced sky, a removed person, a reshaped
body, a changed product. Likely cause: The photographer used editing to deceive — to make the photo look like something it
is not. How to investigate: Compare the edited photo to the original. Are there elements that were not in the original?
Corrective steps: Undo the faked edits. Edit honestly — enhance, do not deceive. How to confirm resolved: The edited photo
is a better version of the original, not a different image. How to prevent recurrence: Follow the honest editing rules. If an edit
would mislead the viewer, do not do it.
K.7 Not saving the edited photo
Symptoms: The learner edits the photo but forgets to export or save it. The edits are lost. Likely cause: The learner does not
know how to save the edited photo in Snapseed. How to investigate: Ask the learner to show you the edited photo in their
gallery. If it is not there, they did not save it. Corrective steps: Show them how to export: Tap Export > Save (saves a copy) or
Export > Save a copy (keeps the original). How to confirm resolved: The edited photo is in the gallery. How to prevent
recurrence: Make saving the final step of every edit. Always export before closing the app.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual photo,
compare it to the original, and confirm the cause before correcting.
