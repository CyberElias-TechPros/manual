# Building a Portfolio and Pricing Photography

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: "Process and Deliver"

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (practical, business-focused)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–4 — the learner should have a foundations folder of raw photos, an edited set, and the ability to shoot and edit to a usable standard. No prior business or pricing knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–4 you learned to shoot and edit professional-quality photos. Now you learn to select your strongest work, assemble a cohesive portfolio, price your services in naira, and work with clients professionally. This session prepares you for the capstone — the themed photo shoot and mini portfolio.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers with internet access and Canva installed or accessible; the instructor's computer connected to the projector for live Canva demonstration; a sample portfolio (printed or projected) showing a strong and a weak portfolio; a sample price list for a fictional Port Harcourt photographer; printed consent and release forms; the "Portfolio Selection Sheet" (Section H) printed per learner; a short fictional client brief for the timed simulation. Instructor note on tone: This session is about professionalism and business. Many learners are creative but uncomfortable with the business side — pricing, talking about money, presenting their work. Create a supportive, practical atmosphere. Emphasise that pricing is not greedy — it is fair. Presenting your work is not arrogance — it is professionalism. The goal is to help learners see themselves as professionals, not hobbyists.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Select the strongest images from their body of work, using clear quality criteria.
2. Sequence a portfolio so it flows as a cohesive set — not just a collection of individual photos.
3. Assemble a first portfolio page in Canva, with a title, images and brief descriptions.
4. Build a package price list in naira with at least three packages, each with clear deliverables.
5. Describe a client workflow from brief to delivery: brief, quote, shoot, edit, deliver, follow-up.
6. Use consent and release forms correctly, including parent/guardian consent for under-18s.
7. Complete a timed simulation — plan, shoot, edit and present a delivery-ready set from a short client brief.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two portfolios:
Portfolio A (strong): A set of five product photos for a fictional food business. The photos are consistent — same style,
same light, same background. They are sequenced to tell a story: the ingredients, the preparation, the finished dish, the
packaging, the happy customer. The portfolio has a title, the photographer's name, and a brief description of each photo.
Portfolio B (weak): A set of ten photos — some product shots, some portraits, some landscapes, some event photos. They
are in random order, with different styles and quality levels. There is no title, no description, no flow.
What the instructor says:
"Two portfolios. Both contain good photos. But one looks like a professional photographer made it, and one looks like
someone just dumped all their photos in a folder. Hands up — which portfolio would you hire? … Portfolio A, almost
everyone. Now tell me: what is different about Portfolio A?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "it looks organised," "the photos match," "it tells a story," "it has a
title," "it looks professional," "it is not mixed up." Accept all answers and write the key phrases on the board: consistent,
organised, story, title, professional, flow.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"A portfolio is not 'all my best photos.' It is the right photos in the right order, telling the right story. Today you learn to select
your strongest work, sequence it so it flows, and assemble it in Canva. You also learn to price your work in naira —
because photography is a skill, and skills have value. And you learn to work with clients professionally — from the first
brief to the final delivery. By the end of this session, you will have a first portfolio page, a price list, and a client workflow
sheet."
Why this opener works: It uses two portfolios with the same quality of photos but very different presentation, proving that
selection and sequencing — not just photo quality — make the difference. It introduces the business side of photography
before any technical explanation.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is the editing workflow? Name the steps in order."
Expected answer: Crop and straighten, correct exposure, adjust colour, retouch, sharpen.
Common gap: Learners may forget the order. Clarify: "Crop first, then exposure, then colour, then retouch, then sharpen."
Question 2: "What are the honest editing rules? Name two."
Expected answer: Do not change a product's true appearance; do not fake a background; do not reshape a body beyond
normal correction; do not mislead the viewer.
Common gap: Learners may remember one rule but not others. Clarify: "Enhance, do not deceive. If a client or viewer
would be misled, do not do it."
Question 3: "What is batch editing, and why does it matter?"
Expected answer: Batch editing means applying the same edits to multiple photos so they look consistent. It matters
because a set of photos should look like they belong together — not like they were taken by different people.
Common gap: Learners may think batch editing means pasting edits without reviewing. Clarify: "Copy, paste, review each
photo, adjust. Batch editing gets you 90% of the way — the last 10% is individual review."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You can shoot and edit professional-quality photos. But a photographer is not just a person who takes photos — a
photographer is a professional who delivers a product to a client. Today you learn the professional side: selecting your best
work, building a portfolio, pricing your services, and working with clients. This is where your skill becomes a business."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the editing workflow, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing it on the board before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a portfolio is and why it matters
The idea. A portfolio is a curated collection of your best work, presented in a way that shows your skill, your style and your
professionalism. It is not a dump of every photo you have ever taken — it is a deliberate selection of your strongest images,
sequenced to tell a story.
Plain-English definition. A portfolio is a small, carefully chosen set of your best photographs, organised to show a potential
client what you can do. It is your professional face — the first thing a client sees when they consider hiring you.
Why it matters:
It shows your skill. A portfolio proves you can deliver professional-quality work.
It shows your style. A portfolio shows the kind of photography you do best — portraits, products, events.
It builds trust. A professional portfolio makes a client feel confident hiring you.
It wins work. A strong portfolio is the difference between getting hired and being passed over.
The three rules of a strong portfolio (teach these and return to them all session):
Rule What it means Why it matters
1. Select ruthlessly
Include only your strongest images — not
every photo you like
A portfolio with one weak photo is a weak
portfolio. Quality over quantity.
2. Sequence for flow
Arrange the photos so they tell a story —
not random order
A portfolio that flows feels professional. A random
collection feels careless.
3. Show consistency
The photos should look like they belong
together — same style, same quality
Consistency shows you can deliver a reliable
standard. Inconsistency shows you cannot.
What a portfolio is NOT:
It is not "all my best photos." It is the right photos — the ones that show your skill and style for the work you want to do.
It is not a random collection. It is a curated, sequenced set.
It is not a place for experiments. It is a professional presentation — save experiments for your personal work.
Real-life comparison. A portfolio is like a job resume. A resume is not a list of everything you have ever done — it is a curated
selection of your most relevant experience, presented clearly and professionally. A portfolio does the same for your
photography: it shows the client the best of what you can do, in a way that makes them want to hire you.
Misconception to correct. "I need to have hundreds of photos before I can build a portfolio." No. A strong portfolio can be five
photos. A weak portfolio is weak even with five hundred. Quality and curation matter more than quantity.
F.2 Selecting your best work
The idea. Selecting your best work is the most important skill in building a portfolio. It requires honesty — you must be able to
look at your own photos and say "this one is not good enough." This is hard, because you have an emotional attachment to
your photos. But a professional learns to select ruthlessly.
The selection criteria (teach these and return to them all session):
Criterion What to ask yourself Why it matters
Sharpness Is the subject sharp? Can I see the detail when I zoom in? A blurry photo is unusable — no
exceptions.
Exposure Is the photo correctly exposed — not too dark, not too bright? A poorly exposed photo looks
amateur.
Composition Is the composition intentional? Does the photo look like someone
made choices?
A poorly composed photo looks
accidental.
Light Is the light flattering? Does the subject look their best? Bad light ruins an otherwise good
photo.
Moment Does the photo capture the right instant? Does it feel alive? A missed moment is a missed
opportunity.
Honesty Is the photo honestly edited? Does it look like the real scene? A dishonest photo destroys trust.
The selection process:
1. Gather all your photos from the course — the foundations folder, the edited set, any other photos you are proud of.
2. Review each photo against the six criteria.
3. Rate each photo: Strong (meets all criteria), Acceptable (meets most criteria), Weak (fails one or more criteria).
4. Select only the Strong photos for your portfolio. If you do not have enough Strong photos, shoot more — do not lower
your standard.
5. Aim for 5–8 photos for a mini portfolio. Quality over quantity.
The ruthless selection rule (say this slowly):
"If a photo is not Strong, it does not go in the portfolio. Not 'almost Strong.' Not 'good enough.' Strong. A portfolio with one
weak photo is a weak portfolio."
Real-life comparison. Selecting your best work is like a chef choosing ingredients for a signature dish. The chef does not use
every ingredient in the kitchen — they choose the best, the freshest, the most flavourful. A portfolio is your signature dish —
use only your best ingredients.
Common mistake. Including a photo because you like the memory, not because the photo is strong. A photo of a beautiful
sunset that is slightly blurry does not belong in your portfolio — no matter how much you enjoyed the sunset. The portfolio is
about the photo, not the memory.
F.3 Sequencing a portfolio — telling a story
The idea. A portfolio is not just a collection of strong photos — it is a sequence that tells a story. The order matters. A well-
sequenced portfolio flows naturally, building from one photo to the next, creating a cohesive experience for the viewer.
The sequencing principles (teach these and return to them all session):
Principle What it means Example
1. Start strong
Your first photo should be your strongest — it sets
the impression
Open with your most impactful image
2. Build gradually
Move from strong to strongest — build the story Start with a wide shot, move to details, end
with the hero shot
3. Create contrast
Vary the photos — different angles, different scales,
different moods
Do not put three similar photos next to
each other
4. End strong
Your last photo should be memorable — it is what
the viewer remembers
Close with your most emotional or
impactful image
5. Keep it consistent
All photos should look like they belong together —
same style, same quality
Do not mix heavily edited and unedited
photos
The story arc for a product portfolio:
1. The hero shot — the product at its best, the first impression.
2. The detail shot — a close-up of the product's texture, label or key feature.
3. The context shot — the product in use or in its environment.
4. The variety shot — showing the range of products or options.
5. The closing shot — a memorable image that leaves a lasting impression.
The story arc for a portrait portfolio:
1. The establishing shot — a wide portrait showing the person in their environment.
2. The character shot — a closer portrait showing the person's personality.
3. The detail shot — hands, eyes, or a meaningful object.
4. The emotion shot — a genuine laugh, a thoughtful look, a moment of joy.
5. The closing shot — a strong, memorable portrait that captures the person's essence.
Real-life comparison. Sequencing a portfolio is like telling a story. A good story has a beginning (the hook), a middle (the
development) and an end (the resolution). A portfolio does the same — it draws the viewer in, builds interest, and leaves a
lasting impression.
Common mistake. Putting all your strongest photos at the beginning and letting the quality drop off at the end. The viewer
remembers the last photo most — make it count.
F.4 Building a portfolio in Canva
The idea. Canva is a free, browser-based design tool that makes it easy to assemble a professional-looking portfolio. You do not
need design experience — Canva provides templates, drag-and-drop editing and professional layouts.
How to build a portfolio page in Canva:
1. Create a free account at canva.com (or download the Canva app).
2. Choose a template — search for "portfolio" or "photo gallery" in the template library. Choose a clean, simple template —
do not choose anything too busy or colourful.
3. Add a title — your name, the course name and the date. Example: "Chinedu Okafor — CEA Photography — October
2026."
4. Add your photos — drag and drop your selected photos into the template. Arrange them in your sequenced order.
5. Add brief descriptions — under each photo, add one sentence explaining what the photo shows and why it is in the
portfolio. Example: "Product shot — Mama Nkechi's pepper soup base. Natural window light, rule of thirds composition."
6. Review and adjust — check the layout, the spacing, the alignment. Make sure everything looks clean and professional.
7. Export — download the portfolio as a PDF or PNG. This is your portfolio file.
The portfolio page checklist:
Title with your name, course and date
5–8 photos, sequenced for flow
Brief description under each photo
Clean, professional layout
Consistent style across all photos
Exported as a PDF or PNG
Real-life comparison. Building a portfolio in Canva is like arranging a shop window. You choose the best products, arrange
them in a pleasing order, add clear labels, and make sure everything looks clean and professional. Canva is your shop window
— make it inviting.
Common mistake. Using a template that is too busy or colourful — the template competes with your photos for attention.
Choose a clean, simple template that lets your photos stand out.
F.5 Pricing your photography — packages in naira
The idea. Pricing is one of the hardest parts of photography — especially for beginners. You do not want to charge too little
(you devalue your work) or too much (you price yourself out of the market). The solution is to build clear packages with defined
deliverables, so the client knows exactly what they are getting for their money.
The two pricing models:
Model What it means When to use it
Per hour You charge for the time you spend shooting Good for events, where the time is the main variable
Per package You charge a fixed price for a defined set of
deliverables
Good for portraits and products, where the deliverables
are clear
The package pricing model (recommended for beginners):
A package is a fixed price for a clear set of deliverables. The client knows what they are getting, and you know what you are
delivering. This removes ambiguity and builds trust.
Three sample packages for a product photographer in Port Harcourt:
Package What is included
Price
(example)
Basic 5 edited product photos, 1 background option, 3-day delivery ₦15,000
Standard 10 edited product photos, 2 background options, 2-day delivery, 1 revision round ₦30,000
Premium 20 edited product photos, 3 background options, 1-day delivery, 2 revision rounds, social media
captions
₦60,000
Three sample packages for a portrait photographer in Port Harcourt:
Package What is included Price (example)
Basic 30-minute shoot, 5 edited photos, 1 location, 3-day delivery ₦10,000
Standard 1-hour shoot, 10 edited photos, 2 locations, 2-day delivery, 1 revision round ₦25,000
Premium 2-hour shoot, 20 edited photos, 3 locations, 1-day delivery, 2 revision rounds, printed photos ₦50,000
How to set your prices:
1. Research the market — what do other photographers in Port Harcourt charge? Ask around, check social media, look at
competitor pricing.
2. Calculate your costs — your time, transport, editing time, any props or materials.
3. Set a fair price — not so little that you lose money, not so much that you price yourself out. As a beginner, you may
charge less than an experienced photographer — but do not work for free.
4. Offer packages — packages make it easy for the client to say yes. They know what they are getting, and you know what
you are delivering.
5. Be transparent — state your prices clearly. Do not hide them or make the client guess.
The pricing rules (teach these and return to them all session):
Never work for free (except for practice or an agreed favour). Free work devalues your skill and sets an expectation that
your work is worth nothing.
State your prices clearly — do not be vague or apologistic. Your skill has value.
Deliver what you promise — if you say 10 photos, deliver 10 photos. If you say 3-day delivery, deliver in 3 days.
Revise your prices as you grow — as your skill and reputation grow, your prices should grow too.
Real-life comparison. Pricing is like a market trader setting prices for her goods. She knows her costs, she knows what the
market will bear, and she sets a fair price. She does not give her goods away for free, and she does not charge so much that no
one buys. A photographer does the same — set a fair price for a clear deliverable.
Misconception to correct. "I am a beginner, so I should charge very little or nothing." No. You have a skill that took time and
effort to learn. Charge a fair price — less than an experienced photographer, perhaps, but not nothing. Free work devalues your
skill and makes it harder to charge fairly later.
F.6 The client workflow — from brief to delivery
The idea. A professional photographer does not just take photos — they manage a process. The client workflow is the step-by-
step process from the first conversation to the final delivery. It ensures nothing is missed, the client knows what to expect, and
the work is delivered professionally.
The six-step client workflow (teach these and return to them all session):
Step What happens Who is responsible
1. Brief
The client explains what they need — the purpose, the subject, the
style, the deadline
Client provides, photographer
listens and asks questions
2. Quote
The photographer provides a price based on the brief — a package
price or an hourly rate
Photographer
3. Shoot
The photographer plans and executes the shoot — location, light,
composition, consent
Photographer
4. Edit
The photographer edits the photos — crop, exposure, colour,
retouch, batch edit
Photographer
5. Deliver
The photographer delivers the final photos — in the agreed format,
by the agreed deadline
Photographer
6. Follow-up
The photographer checks that the client is happy, asks for feedback,
and requests a testimonial or referral if appropriate
Photographer
The brief — the most important step: The brief is the foundation of the entire job. A clear brief means a clear delivery. A
vague brief means a confused client and a missed expectation.
The brief should answer these questions:
What is the purpose of the photos? (Product catalogue, portrait, event coverage, social media?)
What is the subject? (A specific product, a person, an event?)
What style do you want? (Clean and minimal, warm and natural, dramatic?)
How many photos do you need?
What is the deadline?
What is the budget?
Are there any specific requirements? (Background, props, locations, consent?)
The quote — clear and professional:
State the package or price clearly.
State what is included — number of photos, delivery time, revision rounds.
State what is not included — extra photos, rush delivery, additional locations.
State the payment terms — deposit (e.g., 50% before the shoot), balance on delivery.
The shoot — planned and professional:
Arrive on time.
Check the light, the background, the composition.
Obtain consent from any person photographed.
Take more photos than needed — you can always edit down.
The edit — honest and consistent:
Use the editing workflow: crop, exposure, colour, retouch, sharpen.
Batch-edit for consistency.
Review each photo individually.
The delivery — on time and in the agreed format:
Deliver the photos in the agreed format (JPEG, PNG, PDF).
Deliver by the agreed deadline.
Include a brief note explaining what was delivered and how to use the photos.
The follow-up — build the relationship:
Check that the client is happy.
Ask for feedback — what went well, what could be improved.
If the client is happy, ask for a testimonial or a referral.
Stay in touch — a past client is the best source of future work.
Real-life comparison. The client workflow is like a restaurant experience. The brief is the customer telling the waiter what they
want. The quote is the menu price. The shoot is the kitchen preparing the meal. The edit is the chef plating the dish. The
delivery is the waiter serving the meal. The follow-up is the manager asking if everything was satisfactory. A good restaurant
manages all six steps — a good photographer does the same.
Common mistake. Skipping the brief — just showing up and shooting without understanding what the client wants. This leads
to missed expectations, wasted time and an unhappy client. Always start with a clear brief.
F.7 Consent and releases — the legal and ethical foundation

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Emeka is a beginner photographer in Port Harcourt. He has been shooting for a few months and has a collection of
photos he is proud of. He wants to start charging for his work, but he does not know how to present himself or price his
services. He posts his photos on Instagram with the caption "DM for prices" and waits for clients. After three months, he has had
only two enquiries and no bookings.
Relevant facts.
Emeka has no portfolio — just individual Instagram posts.
He has no price list — he tells people to "DM for prices."
He has no client workflow — he does not know how to handle a brief, quote or delivery.
He has no consent forms — he photographs people without asking.
His photos are actually good — sharp, well-lit, well-composed.
Decision point. What is the main reason Emeka is not getting clients?
Possible approaches.
1. Emeka needs better photos — his current work is not good enough.
2. Emeka needs to present himself professionally — a portfolio, a price list, a clear workflow.
3. Emeka needs to post more often on Instagram — consistency on social media is the key.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Emeka's photos are good — his problem is presentation. A potential client who sees
individual Instagram posts with "DM for prices" does not know what Emeka offers, what it costs, or what to expect. A
professional portfolio, a clear price list and a defined workflow make it easy for a client to say yes.
Specific fixes for Emeka:
Build a portfolio: Select his strongest 5–8 photos, sequence them, assemble them in Canva.
Create a price list: Three packages with clear deliverables in naira.
Define a client workflow: Brief, quote, shoot, edit, deliver, follow-up.
Obtain consent: Ask before photographing people, use consent forms.
Present clearly: Instead of "DM for prices," post his portfolio and price list. Let the work speak.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is unnecessary — Emeka's photos are already good. Better photos will not fix a presentation problem.
Approach 2 addresses the actual gap — presentation and professionalism. It is free (Canva is free, pricing is just a
document) and immediately makes Emeka look professional.
Approach 3 is partially true — consistency on social media matters — but without a portfolio and price list, more posts
will not convert followers into clients.
Professional connection. This case study illustrates the difference between a hobbyist and a professional. A hobbyist takes
photos and posts them. A professional presents their work, prices their services and manages the client relationship. The skill is
the same — the presentation is what makes the difference. Emeka has the skill; he needs the presentation.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Including too many photos in the portfolio
Symptoms: The portfolio has 15, 20 or 30 photos — it feels overwhelming and unfocused. Likely cause: The learner is
emotionally attached to their photos and cannot bear to leave any out. How to investigate: Count the photos. Is the number
between 5 and 8? If not, it is too many. Corrective steps: Reduce to 5–8 photos. Select only the Strong ones. Quality over
quantity. How to confirm resolved: The portfolio has 5–8 Strong photos. How to prevent recurrence: Set a limit before you
start. "I will select exactly 7 photos. No more."
K.2 Portfolio with no flow — random order
Symptoms: The photos are in no particular order — a product shot, then a portrait, then a landscape, then another product
shot. Likely cause: The learner selected strong photos but did not think about sequencing. How to investigate: Look at the
portfolio — does it tell a story? Does it flow naturally from one photo to the next? Corrective steps: Sequence the photos: start
strong, build gradually, end strong, create contrast, keep it consistent. How to confirm resolved: The portfolio flows naturally
— it tells a story. How to prevent recurrence: Before finalising the portfolio, ask: "What story does this tell? Does it start
strong and end strong?"
K.3 Pricing too low — devaluing the work
Symptoms: The learner charges ₦2,000 for a set of 10 professional product photos — far below the market rate. Likely cause:
The learner is afraid of losing the client, or does not understand the value of their skill. How to investigate: Compare the
learner's prices to the market rate for similar work in Port Harcourt. Corrective steps: Research the market. Calculate costs. Set a
fair price — less than an experienced photographer, perhaps, but not a fraction of the market rate. How to confirm resolved:
The prices are fair — not too low, not too high. How to prevent recurrence: Remember: your skill has value. Free or near-free
work devalues your skill and sets an expectation that your work is worth nothing.
K.4 Pricing too high — pricing yourself out
Symptoms: The learner charges ₦100,000 for a basic product shoot — far above the market rate for a beginner. Likely cause:
The learner does not understand the market, or is trying to charge experienced-photographer rates as a beginner. How to
investigate: Compare the learner's prices to the market rate for a beginner in Port Harcourt. Corrective steps: Research the
market. As a beginner, charge less than an experienced photographer. Build your reputation, then raise your prices. How to
confirm resolved: The prices are competitive — fair for a beginner. How to prevent recurrence: Start with fair beginner
prices. Raise them as your skill and reputation grow.
K.5 Skipping the brief — shooting without understanding
Symptoms: The learner shows up to a shoot without knowing what the client wants — the purpose, the subject, the style, the
deadline. Likely cause: The learner did not ask for a brief, or did not listen to the client's answers. How to investigate: Ask the
learner: "What did the client ask for? What is the purpose? What is the deadline?" Corrective steps: Always start with a brief. Ask
the client: What is the purpose? What is the subject? What style? How many photos? What is the deadline? What is the budget?
How to confirm resolved: The learner can state the client's needs clearly. How to prevent recurrence: Make the brief the first
step of every job. Never skip it.
K.6 Not obtaining consent or releases
Symptoms: The learner photographs people without asking, or uses images in a portfolio without a release. Likely cause: The
learner does not understand the consent and release requirements. How to investigate: Ask the learner: "Did you ask
permission before photographing that person? Do you have a signed release?" Corrective steps: Stop. Obtain consent before
photographing. Obtain a release before using the image. Use the printed forms. How to confirm resolved: Consent and release
forms are signed and filed. How to prevent recurrence: Make consent the first step of every portrait or event shoot. Make
releases the first step of every portfolio or client use.
K.7 Not following up after delivery
Symptoms: The learner delivers the photos and never contacts the client again. Likely cause: The learner does not know that
follow-up is part of the professional workflow. How to investigate: Ask the learner: "Did you check that the client was happy?
Did you ask for feedback?" Corrective steps: After delivery, follow up. Check that the client is happy. Ask for feedback. If the
client is happy, ask for a testimonial or referral. How to confirm resolved: The learner has a follow-up process. How to
prevent recurrence: Make follow-up the final step of every job. A past client is the best source of future work.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual portfolio,
price list or workflow, and confirm the cause before correcting.

"Consent is only needed for commercial work." → No — consent is needed before photographing any person, always.
"Follow-up is optional." → No — follow-up builds relationships and generates referrals.
"I do not need a price list — I will just negotiate." → No — a clear price list makes it easy for the client to say yes.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Selection drill: Give the learner 20 photos and have them select the strongest 5. Review together — are they truly the
strongest?
2. Sequencing drill: Give the learner 5 photos and have them sequence them for flow. Review — does it start strong and end
strong?
3. Pricing research: Have the learner research three photographers in Port Harcourt and compare their prices. Discuss what is
fair for a beginner.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a partially completed portfolio template in Canva with the photos already inserted — the
learner adds descriptions and adjusts the layout. Provide a price list with the packages already named — the learner fills in
the deliverables and prices.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full lecture, demonstration, guided practical, simulation and assignment.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to build a complete portfolio (not just one page) with a title page, an about page, a
services page and a contact page. They should also create a second price list for a different type of photography (e.g., if
they did products, they do portraits) and write a short marketing plan — how they will find their first three clients.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners building a portfolio, creating a price list and completing the simulation (Sections H, I
and the assignment). If the room is slow, shorten the lecture's pricing section to the key points — packages, fair prices, never
free — and protect the practical and simulation time. A learner who leaves without a portfolio, a price list and a completed
simulation has no business foundation for the capstone.
