# Photography Foundations

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: "See and Shoot" (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with photography)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Basic smartphone use — opening apps, taking a photo, typing. No prior photography knowledge assumed. If a learner struggles with the phone basics, pair them with a confident learner for the first practical.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. There is no previous photography session. It connects to everyday life: almost every learner has a phone camera and has taken photos that came out dark, blurry or crooked.

**Preparation before class:** Print the three "same subject, three results" comparison sets (Section D); a projector connected to the instructor's phone for live camera demonstration; a whiteboard for the exposure triangle diagram; the "Camera Setup Checklist" (Section H) printed per learner; learners' phones fully charged with at least 1 GB free storage; confirm the class understands this is a practical, hands-on course with a consent rule for photographing people. Instructor note on tone: This session must feel empowering, not technical. Learners should leave feeling "I understand what my phone camera is doing and I can control it," not "photography is too complicated." Keep the mood practical, encouraging and connected to their real lives — their shops, their churches, their families.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words what makes a good photograph, using five key words: clarity, light, composition, moment and
purpose.
2. Describe the exposure triangle in simplified terms — aperture, shutter speed and ISO — and explain why an image comes
out dark, bright or blurry.
3. Distinguish between a phone camera and a dedicated camera, explaining what each is good for and what megapixels
actually tell you.
4. Explain how focus works on a phone and demonstrate tap-to-focus, focus lock and focus check.
5. Set up a phone camera sensibly: grid lines on, high resolution, HDR deliberate, beauty filters off.
6. Produce a sharp, correctly exposed photograph of a real subject using tap-to-focus, exposure adjustment and a steady
hold, without any filter or beautification.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner with a working camera (Android or iPhone; any phone made in the last several years is
capable).
Instructor's phone connected to a projector or large screen for live camera demonstration.
A phone tripod or improvised stand (stack of books, selfie stick) for the instructor demonstration.
An academy reflector or improvised white card (A4 paper works) for bouncing light.
Software
The phone's built-in camera app (default shooting tool — no special app needed for this session).
No editing software needed today; editing begins in Session 4.
Accounts
None required this session.
Sample files
Three comparison sets printed or projected (Section D): each set shows the same subject photographed three ways —
well, poorly and acceptably — so learners can name the difference.
Camera Setup Checklist (Section H) — one printed page per learner.
Worksheets
Worksheet 1.1 — "What Makes a Good Photo?" (a table with the five quality words and room for learners to write their own
definition).
Worksheet 1.2 — "Diagnose the Image" (four sample images; learners label each as dark, bright, blurry or flat and explain
why).
Tools
A whiteboard and markers for drawing the exposure triangle.
A ruler or straight edge for drawing the triangle neatly.
Internet
Not required. The session works fully offline.
Alternative for learners who lack a capable phone
Any phone with a working camera works. A learner without a phone may pair with a classmate, use an academy phone or
use an academy computer with a webcam for the focus and exposure exercises. They must tell the instructor at the start so
support can be arranged.
Safety note for the instructor (read aloud at the start): Before we photograph any person in this class — including
classmates — we ask for their permission first. If someone says no, we put the camera down. No exceptions. This is a rule of the
academy, not a suggestion.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show the same subject — a bottle of water on a table — photographed three ways:
Photo A (good): Sharp, well-lit, clean background, subject centred with space around it.
Photo B (poor): Dark, blurry, cluttered background, subject barely visible.
Photo C (acceptable): Better than B but not great — slightly crooked, a bit dark, background distracting.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Look at these three photos. Same bottle, same table, same room. But one looks like it could be in a shop's
advert, one looks like a mistake, and one is somewhere in between. Hands up — which one would you use for your
business? … Photo A, almost everyone agrees. Now, turn to the person next to you and tell them: what is different about
Photo A? You have 90 seconds."
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "it is brighter," "you can see it clearly," "the background is clean," "it is
not blurry," "it looks professional." Accept all answers and write the key words on the board: bright, sharp, clean, clear,
professional.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"Those words — bright, sharp, clean, clear — are not luck. They are choices a photographer makes. Today you will learn
to make those choices on your own phone. By the end of this session, you will be able to look at a photo and say exactly
what is wrong with it, and then fix it."
Why this opener works: It uses a subject every learner can recognise, it produces instant opinions ("I would never use Photo
B"), and it introduces the five quality words before any technical explanation, so the lecture has a hook to hang on.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What happens when you press the shutter button on your phone camera? What does the camera actually do?"
Expected answer: It opens a hole (the lens), lets light in for a moment, and records what it sees. If a learner says "it takes a
picture," press gently: "Yes, but how? What has to happen for the picture to look right?"
Common gap: Learners treat the camera as a black box. Clarify: "The camera is a light-capturing machine. Light comes in
through the lens, and the camera decides how much light to let in and for how long. Those decisions determine whether
your photo is bright, dark or blurry."
Question 2: "Have you ever taken a photo that came out too dark or too bright? What do you think went wrong?"
Expected answer: Learners will describe real experiences — a night photo that was black, a photo of a person against a
window where the face was dark, a photo in bright sun that looked washed out.
Common gap: Learners blame the phone. Clarify: "The phone did exactly what it was told — but it was told the wrong
thing. Today you learn to tell it the right thing."
Question 3: "What is the difference between a smartphone camera and a 'proper' camera — like a DSLR?"
Expected answer: Learners may say the proper camera is better, or bigger, or more expensive. Accept this and refine: "A
dedicated camera has a bigger lens and more manual control. But the phone in your pocket can produce excellent
photographs when you understand the same principles. The principles matter more than the price tag."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already have the tool — your phone. Today we make sure you understand what it is doing when you press that
button, so you can control it. Think of it like driving a car: right now most of us are pressing the accelerator and hoping.
Today you learn what the brakes and steering wheel do."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes opening the camera app on the projector and
pointing out the shutter button, the viewfinder and the settings icon before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What makes a good photograph
The idea. A good photograph is not an accident. It is the result of a photographer making deliberate choices about five things:
what the photo is for, how the subject is arranged, how light falls on the subject, whether the subject is sharp, and whether the
right moment was captured.
The five quality words (teach these and return to them all session):
Quality word Plain-English meaning How to recognise it
Clarity The subject is sharp — you can see the details, not
a blurry mess
Zoom in on the subject: can you see texture, edges,
fine details?
Light The subject is correctly exposed — not too dark,
not too bright
Look at the subject's face or the product: can you see
it clearly without squinting or losing detail?
Composition The subject is arranged in the frame in a way that is
pleasing and intentional
Does the photo look like someone chose where
everything goes, or did they just point and shoot?
Moment The photo captures the right instant — the smile,
the action, the expression
Does the photo feel alive, or does it feel like a stiff,
awkward snapshot?
Purpose The photo does the job it was meant to do — sell
a product, tell a story, capture a memory
If someone asked you to take this photo, would they
be happy with the result?
Why these five words matter. They are the vocabulary you will use for the rest of the course. In Session 2 you will learn
composition techniques. In Session 3 you will learn to capture the right moment. Today you focus on the first two — clarity
and light — because without those, nothing else matters. A beautifully composed photo that is blurry is still a bad photo.
Real-life comparison. Think of a plate of food at a restaurant. If the food is beautifully arranged on the plate (composition), the
chef is skilled (moment), and the dish is exactly what you ordered (purpose) — but the lights are so dim you cannot see the
food (light), and the plate is spinning so fast everything is a smear (clarity) — you would send it back. A photograph works the
same way. Light and clarity are the foundation; everything else is built on top.
Important limitation / misconception. Learners often think good photographs require expensive cameras or professional
lighting. This is false. The phone in your pocket, used well, can produce photographs that are sharp, well-lit and usable for a
business. The difference between a snapshot and a photograph is not the camera — it is the photographer's choices.
How a learner recognises a good photograph. Show any photo and ask five questions: Is the subject sharp? Is the light right? Is
the composition intentional? Did it capture the right moment? Does it serve its purpose? If the answer is yes to all five, it is a
good photograph.
F.2 The exposure triangle — simplified
The idea. Every camera — phone or professional — makes three decisions when you press the shutter button. Together, these
three decisions determine how bright or dark your photo is. Photographers call these three decisions the exposure triangle.
Plain-English definition. The exposure triangle is a way of understanding three camera settings that control how much light
reaches the sensor:
Setting What it does Simple analogy What happens when it is wrong
Aperture How wide the lens
opening is — how much
light comes in
Like a window: a big
window lets in more light
than a small window
If the aperture is too small (or the light is low),
the photo comes out dark
Shutter speed How long the shutter
stays open — how long
light is collected
Like blinking: a slow blink
collects more light than a
fast blink
If the shutter is too fast in low light, the photo
comes out dark; if the subject moves during a
slow shutter, the photo comes out blurry
ISO How sensitive the
camera's sensor is to
light
Like turning up the volume
on a quiet radio
A high ISO brightens a dark scene but adds
grain (noise); a low ISO gives a cleaner image
but needs more light
The key insight (say this slowly):
"These three settings work together. If you make one of them let in less light, you must compensate with one of the others.
A photographer's job is to balance them so the photo is correctly exposed — not too dark, not too bright."
How this works on a phone (the crucial bridge):
On a professional camera, the photographer controls aperture, shutter speed and ISO directly. On a phone, the camera app
makes these decisions automatically — but you can influence them:
Tap to focus: Tapping the screen tells the camera where to focus and how much light to let in for that spot. Tapping a dark
area brightens the photo; tapping a bright area darkens it.
Exposure adjustment slider: On most phone cameras, after you tap, a small sun icon or slider appears. Slide it up to
brighten, down to darken.
HDR (High Dynamic Range): The camera takes several photos at different brightness levels and combines them. Useful for
high-contrast scenes (a person against a bright window). Can be turned on or off in camera settings.
Why photos come out dark (the three usual reasons):
1. Not enough light in the scene — the room is dim, it is evening, the subject is in shadow.
2. The camera exposed for the wrong part of the scene — you photographed a person against a bright window; the
camera exposed for the window (bright) and the face went dark.
3. The shutter was too fast — in low light, the camera used a fast shutter to avoid blur, which collected too little light.
Why photos come out bright (washed out):
1. Too much light — shooting in bright sun without adjusting exposure.
2. The camera exposed for a dark area — tapping a dark shadow makes the camera brighten everything, and the rest of the
photo becomes overexposed.
Why photos come out blurry (the two usual reasons):
1. Camera shake — the photographer's hands moved during the exposure. Most common in low light when the shutter is
slower.
2. Subject movement — the subject moved during the exposure. Common with children, pets, people walking, or anything
that is not still.
3. Focus error — the camera focused on the wrong thing (the background instead of the subject, or nothing at all in very low
light).
Real-life comparison. The exposure triangle is like cooking rice. The aperture is the amount of water, the shutter speed is the
cooking time, and ISO is the heat. Too little water or too short a time and the rice is hard (dark photo). Too much heat and the
rice burns (grainy photo from high ISO). A good cook balances all three; a good photographer does the same.
Misconception to correct. "If my photo is dark, I can just brighten it later in editing." Partially true — but brightening a very
dark photo in editing also brightens the noise and grain, making it look rough. It is always better to get the exposure right in the
camera. Editing can fix small problems; it cannot fix a badly exposed photo completely.

The idea. A phone camera and a dedicated camera (DSLR or mirrorless) are different tools with different strengths.

What megapixels actually tell you (and what they do not):
A megapixel is one million pixels — tiny dots of colour that make up a digital image. A 12-megapixel camera produces images
made of 12 million dots. A 48-megapixel camera produces images made of 48 million dots.
What megapixels tell you: How large you can print the photo or how much you can crop it and still have a usable image. A
12-megapixel image can be printed at A4 size and look sharp. A 48-megapixel image can be cropped to a small portion and
still print sharply.
What megapixels do NOT tell you: How good the photo looks. A 12-megapixel photo from a large sensor with a good lens
will look better than a 48-megapixel photo from a small sensor with a poor lens, especially in low light. Megapixels are
about quantity of detail, not quality of light.
Why this matters for this course: Learners sometimes feel their phone is inferior because it has "only" 12 megapixels. Reassure
them: for WhatsApp Business catalogues, Instagram, Facebook and most small-business uses, 12 megapixels is more than
enough. The sensor size and lens quality matter more for image quality, and the photographer's choices matter most of all.
Real-life comparison. A phone camera is like a bicycle: it is always available, it gets you where you need to go, and it costs
nothing extra. A dedicated camera is like a car: it is faster, more comfortable on long journeys, and more capable — but you
have to plan to bring it, it costs money, and it does not help you if it is at home. For most everyday photography, the bicycle is
exactly what you need.
F.4 How focus works on a phone
The idea. Focus is the camera's ability to make the subject sharp. On a phone, focus is automatic — but you can and should
control it deliberately.
The three focus skills every photographer needs:
1. Tap to focus.
Open the camera app.
Look at the screen. Tap the subject you want to be sharp.
A focus square or circle appears around where you tapped. The camera now focuses on that spot.
On most phones, tapping also sets the exposure (brightness) for that spot — this is why tapping a bright window makes
the rest of the photo dark, and tapping a dark face brightens the photo.
2. Lock focus and exposure.
After tapping, press and hold the focus square for 2–3 seconds (on iPhone) or tap the lock icon (on many Android
phones).
The focus and exposure are now locked. You can move the camera slightly without the focus changing.
This is useful when you want to focus on a subject but compose the photo differently — focus first, then reframe.
3. Focus check.
After taking the photo, zoom in on it (pinch to zoom in the gallery).
Look at the subject's eyes (for a person) or the key detail (for a product). Are they sharp?
If they are blurry, the photo is not usable — no amount of editing will fix a blurry subject.
Common focus mistakes and how to avoid them:
Not tapping: Letting the camera choose the focus point automatically. The camera may focus on the background, a
distracting object, or nothing in particular. Always tap your subject.
Tapping too late: Tapping and immediately shooting. The camera needs a moment to adjust focus. Tap, wait half a second,
then shoot.
Moving after tapping: Tapping to focus, then moving the camera significantly before shooting. The focus may shift. Lock
focus if you need to move.
Real-life comparison. Focus is like using a magnifying glass to look at something small. You move the glass until the subject is
sharp. If you move your hand, the subject blurs. Tap-to-focus is you placing the magnifying glass exactly where you need it.
F.5 Setting up your phone camera
The idea. Before you take a serious photograph, set up your camera so it behaves predictably. Four settings matter for
beginners:
1. Grid lines on.
Grid lines are two horizontal and two vertical lines that divide the screen into nine equal rectangles (a 3×3 grid).
They help you compose photos using the rule of thirds (taught in Session 2).
How to turn on: iPhone — Settings > Camera > Grid. Android — open the camera app, tap the settings icon (usually a
gear), look for "Grid" or "Grid lines," turn it on.
2. High resolution.
Make sure your camera is set to take photos at the full resolution of the sensor.
iPhone: Settings > Camera > Formats — choose "High Efficiency" (default) or "Most Compatible."
Android: Open camera app > Settings > Photo size or Resolution — choose the highest number of megapixels available.
Why this matters: if you accidentally set a low resolution, your photos will look soft when printed or viewed on a larger
screen.
3. HDR — know what it does, use it deliberately.
HDR (High Dynamic Range) takes multiple photos at different brightness levels and combines them.
Useful when: the scene has very bright and very dark areas (a person against a bright window, a landscape with a bright sky
and dark foreground).
Turn it off when: photographing moving subjects (HDR can create ghosting or blur) or when you want a specific artistic
effect.
iPhone: Settings > Camera — turn HDR on or off. Android: HDR setting is usually in the camera app itself.
4. Beauty filters off.
Many phone cameras and social media apps apply automatic "beauty" filters that smooth skin, enlarge eyes, or alter facial
features.
For this course: turn them off. We want photographs that look like the real person, not a filtered version.
Why: beauty filters create unrealistic expectations and can make a person look unnatural. In business photography, they can
be misleading.
How to turn off: Look for "Beauty," "Smooth," "Face," or "Filters" in your camera app settings and turn them off.
The Camera Setup Checklist (Worksheet 1.3): Before every photo shoot in this course, run through this checklist:
Grid lines are ON
Resolution is set to the highest available
HDR is set appropriately for the scene (on for high contrast, off for movement)
Beauty filters are OFF
Lens is clean (wipe with a soft cloth)
Storage has at least 500 MB free
Real-life comparison. Setting up your camera is like preparing a kitchen before cooking. You wash your hands, check you have
the right ingredients, and make sure the stove is working. If you skip preparation, the meal may still be edible — but it will not
be as good as it could be.
F.6 The sharpness routine — getting sharp photos every time
The idea. Sharpness is the foundation of a good photograph. A blurry photo is unusable, no matter how good the composition
or light. Here is a repeatable routine for getting sharp photos:
The 5-Step Sharpness Routine:
Step 1: Hold the phone steady.
Hold the phone with both hands.
Tuck your elbows into your sides.
If possible, lean against a wall or rest the phone on a solid surface.
If using a tripod or improvised stand (books, selfie stick), lock it in place.
Step 2: Tap to focus on the subject.
Tap the subject on the screen.
Wait half a second for the camera to adjust.
Check the focus square — is it on the right spot?
Step 3: Check the exposure.
Is the subject correctly exposed (not too dark, not too bright)?
If not, use the exposure slider (slide up to brighten, down to darken).
Step 4: Check the background.
Is the background clean and uncluttered?
Is anything distracting growing out of the subject's head?
If so, move the camera or the subject.
Step 5: Take several frames.
Take at least 3 photos of the same scene.
Do not delete any until you have reviewed them on a larger screen.
Why: one frame may be slightly blurry, one may have the subject blinking, one may be perfect. Taking several frames
increases your chances of getting a sharp, usable image.
Why taking several frames matters: Even professional photographers take multiple frames. The difference between a
professional and an amateur is not that the professional always gets it right on the first try — it is that the professional takes
enough tries to guarantee a good result.
Real-life comparison. The sharpness routine is like checking your work before submitting it. A student who writes an exam
answer and hands it in without checking will make silly mistakes. A student who checks their work catches those mistakes. The
sharpness routine is checking your work before you commit to it.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
A good photograph has five qualities: clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose.
The exposure triangle — aperture, shutter speed and ISO — controls how bright or dark your photo is.
On a phone, you influence the exposure through tap-to-focus and the exposure slider.
A phone camera is a legitimate tool for business and everyday photography; megapixels measure detail quantity, not
quality.
Tap to focus, lock focus and focus check are the three focus skills every photographer needs.
Set up your camera: grid on, high resolution, HDR deliberate, beauty filters off.
The 5-step sharpness routine — steady, focus, check exposure, check background, take several frames — produces sharp
photos reliably.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A phone connected to the projector, camera app open.
A simple subject on a table: a bottle of water, a notebook, or a piece of fruit.
A second subject in a different location: a person (a willing colleague or a mannequin/photograph of a person) against a
bright window.
A stack of books or a phone tripod for the steady-hold demonstration.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the exposure triangle in action (5 minutes)
Say: "Watch the screen. I am going to take three photos of this bottle. Same bottle, same position. But I will change one thing
each time — and you will see the result."
Take Photo 1: Tap the bottle, expose normally. Show the result on the projector. "This is correctly exposed — you can see the
label, the water level, the colours."
Take Photo 2: Tap the background (the wall behind the bottle). "Now I tapped the wall. The camera exposed for the wall, which
is bright. What happens to the bottle?" Show the result. "The bottle is darker — almost a silhouette. The camera let in less light
because I tapped something bright."
Take Photo 3: Tap the bottle, then use the exposure slider to brighten. "Now I tapped the bottle and slid the exposure up. What
happens?" Show the result. "The bottle is brighter — maybe too bright. This is what happens when you over-expose."
Say: "One tap, one slider — that is how you control exposure on a phone. The camera does the maths; you make the
decisions."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — focus control (4 minutes)
Say: "Now watch focus. I am going to focus on the bottle, then on the background."
Focus on the bottle: Tap the bottle. Show the focus square. "The square is on the bottle. The bottle is sharp."
Focus on the background: Tap the wall behind the bottle. "Now the square is on the wall. What happened to the bottle?" Show
the result. "The bottle is blurry — out of focus. The camera focused on what I tapped, not on the bottle."
Lock focus: Tap and hold the focus square. "Now I locked the focus. Watch — I can move the camera slightly and the focus
stays on the bottle." Move the camera a little, take a photo. Show it. "Sharp. Lock focus is useful when you want to compose the
photo differently after focusing."
Say: "Focus is not automatic in the way most people think. The camera focuses where you tell it. If you do not tell it, it guesses
— and it guesses wrong often enough to ruin photos."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — the sharpness routine (4 minutes)
Say: "Now I will run the 5-step sharpness routine on a real subject. Watch and count the steps."
Step 1: Hold the phone with both hands, elbows tucked. "Steady hold."
Step 2: Tap the subject on screen. "Tap to focus."
Step 3: Check the exposure, adjust the slider. "Check exposure."
Step 4: Check the background — move the camera slightly to remove a distracting object. "Check background."
Step 5: Take three photos in a row. "Take several frames."
Say: "Five steps, every time. It becomes a habit — like brushing your teeth. You do not think about it after a while, but your
photos are sharp because you do it."
G.5 Demonstration 4 — before and after (2 minutes)
Show a blurry, poorly exposed photo (taken earlier or from the sample files). "This is what happens without the routine —
dark, blurry, unusable."
Show a sharp, well-exposed photo (taken using the routine). "This is what happens with the routine. Same phone, same room,
same subject. The difference is the photographer, not the camera."
Questions to ask during the demo:
"What happened when I tapped the bright wall?" (The subject went dark.)
"What is the difference between tapping the subject and tapping the background?" (The camera focuses and exposes for
wherever you tap.)
"Why take three photos instead of one?" (One might be blurry, one might have the subject blinking, one will be good.)
"Can you fix a blurry photo in editing?" (No — editing cannot create detail that was not captured. Prevention is the only
cure.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner sets up their phone camera, applies the sharpness routine and
produces three sharp, correctly exposed photographs of a real subject.
Starting materials: Learner's own phone; the printed Camera Setup Checklist; a subject in the classroom (a classmate who has
agreed, a bag, a plant, a book, a water bottle, or any object).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Open your camera app. Turn on grid lines in your settings. (Instructor waits while learners find the setting; help those who
cannot.)
2. Set resolution to the highest available. Check that beauty filters are off.
3. Choose your subject. It can be a classmate (ask their permission first — this is the consent rule), your bag, a plant, or any
object on your desk.
4. Step 1 — Steady hold: Hold the phone with both hands, elbows tucked. If you have a surface nearby, rest your hands on
it.
5. Step 2 — Tap to focus: Tap your subject on the screen. Wait half a second.
6. Step 3 — Check exposure: Is the subject too dark or too bright? Use the exposure slider to adjust.
7. Step 4 — Check background: Is anything distracting in the background? Move slightly to remove it.
8. Step 5 — Take three frames: Take three photos of the same scene without moving.
9. Focus check: Open each photo in your gallery and pinch to zoom in on the subject. Is it sharp? If not, repeat steps 4–8.
10. Repeat steps 4–9 for a second subject and a third subject.
Expected result: Nine sharp, correctly exposed photographs (three per subject, three subjects) in the learner's gallery.
Points to pause and predict:
After step 5: "Look at the focus square — is it on your subject? If it is on the background, what will happen?" (The subject
will be blurry.)
After step 8: "Why three frames? What might be wrong with frame one that is not wrong with frame three?" (Frame one
might be slightly blurry from movement; the subject might blink in frame two; frame three is perfect.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner cannot find the grid setting → Show them on their phone. The location differs between iPhone and Android; walk
to each learner and help.
Learner's photos are consistently dark → Check if they are tapping a bright part of the scene. Have them tap the subject
directly.
Learner's photos are blurry → Check if they are moving the camera after tapping. Have them hold steady or rest the phone
on something.
Learner has a beauty filter on and cannot turn it off → Check the camera app settings; some phones have a separate
"Beauty" mode in the camera app. Turn it off or switch to "Photo" mode.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Grid lines are ON
Resolution is set to highest
Beauty filters are OFF
Three photos taken of subject 1, all sharp and correctly exposed
Three photos taken of subject 2, all sharp and correctly exposed
Three photos taken of subject 3, all sharp and correctly exposed
Every photo has been focus-checked by zooming in

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task: Photograph the same subject in three different lighting situations and diagnose the result.
Setup: Choose one subject — a classmate, your bag, a bottle, or a plant. You will photograph it in three places:
1. Near a window or in open shade (good light).
2. In a dim corner or against a bright background (challenging light).
3. In direct bright sun (harsh light).
Requirements:
1. For each lighting situation, take at least one photo using the 5-step sharpness routine.
2. After each photo, use Worksheet 1.2 ("Diagnose the Image") to label the photo: is it dark, bright, blurry or flat?
3. Write one sentence explaining why you think it came out that way.
Constraints:
Use only the built-in camera app — no filters, no editing.
Photograph a classmate only if they have agreed. If not, use an object.
Do not leave the building — find the three lighting situations inside or near the classroom.
Time allowance: 15 minutes.
Success criteria:
Three photos taken in three different lighting situations.
Each photo is labelled with a diagnosis (dark / bright / blurry / flat) and a one-sentence reason.
At least one photo is sharp and correctly exposed (the good-light photo).
Hints available if stuck:
Hint 1: "Good light" means the subject is clearly visible without squinting or being in shadow. A window with indirect light
is good light.
Hint 2: "Challenging light" means the subject is darker than the background, or the background is much brighter than the
subject.
Hint 3: If your photo is blurry, go back to Step 1 of the sharpness routine — hold the phone steadier.
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (model solution — do not show learners):
Photo 1 — near a window (good light): Sharp, correctly exposed, subject clearly visible. Diagnosis: none — good photo.
Reason: "The light is soft and even, and I tapped to focus on the subject."
Photo 2 — dim corner against bright background: Subject is dark, almost a silhouette. Diagnosis: dark. Reason: "The

Photo 3 — direct bright sun: Harsh shadows on the subject's face or the object. Diagnosis: flat or overly bright. Reason: "Direct

Three photos taken in three different lighting situations (3)

Reason given is accurate (2)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu runs a small phone accessory shop in Port Harcourt. He wants to post photos of his products — phone
cases, earbuds, chargers — on his WhatsApp Business catalogue and Instagram. He takes photos on his phone during the day,
in his shop, and posts them directly. His photos are dark, blurry and have cluttered backgrounds (other products, his hands, the
counter). Customers rarely order from the photos; they prefer to come to the shop to see the products in person.
Relevant facts.
Chinedu's phone is a recent model with a good 12-megapixel camera.
He takes photos standing in his shop, holding the phone in one hand, the product in the other.
He does not tap to focus — he just points and shoots.
He has never changed any camera settings.
His shop has a window that lets in good natural light, but he usually photographs under the fluorescent ceiling lights.
Decision point. What is the main reason Chinedu's photos are not helping his business?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a better phone with more megapixels.
2. Learn to use the camera he already has — tap to focus, expose correctly, use natural light, hold steady.
3. Hire a professional photographer once to take all his product photos.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Chinedu's phone is already capable of good photographs. His problem is not the camera
— it is the photographer's choices. By learning to tap to focus, expose correctly, use the natural light from his window and hold
the phone steady, he can produce photos that look professional and help sell his products.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 costs money (a new phone costs ₦150,000–₦400,000) and does not solve the problem — a better phone
used badly still produces bad photos.
Approach 2 is free, takes a few hours to learn, and the skill stays with Chinedu forever. He can photograph new products
himself whenever he needs to.
Approach 3 works but costs money every time (a product photographer in Port Harcourt may charge ₦5,000–₦20,000
for a small set). Chinedu has many products; hiring a photographer for all of them is expensive.
Professional connection. Small business owners across Nigeria lose sales every day because of poor product photographs. A
business that can produce its own clean, sharp product photos saves money and looks more professional. This is a skill that
pays for itself many times over.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Not tapping to focus
Symptoms: Photos where the subject is blurry but the background is sharp, or where everything looks slightly soft. Likely
cause: The photographer let the camera choose the focus point automatically. How to investigate: Ask the learner to open a
photo and zoom in on the subject. Is the subject sharp, or is something else sharp? Corrective steps: Tap the subject on the
screen before every photo. Wait half a second before shooting. How to confirm resolved: The subject is sharp when zoomed
in. How to prevent recurrence: Make tapping a habit — tap before every single photo.
K.2 Photos consistently too dark
Symptoms: Every photo looks dim, the subject is hard to see, details are lost in shadow. Likely cause: The photographer is
tapping a bright part of the scene (or not tapping at all), so the camera exposes for the bright area and the subject goes dark.
How to investigate: Check if the learner is tapping the subject or the background. Check if the exposure slider is being used.
Corrective steps: Tap the subject directly. If it is still dark, slide the exposure slider up. How to confirm resolved: The subject is
clearly visible with detail. How to prevent recurrence: Always tap the subject; adjust exposure after tapping.
K.3 Photos consistently too bright (washed out)
Symptoms: The photo looks pale, colours are faded, bright areas are pure white with no detail. Likely cause: Over-exposure —
too much light reaching the sensor, often from tapping a dark area and not adjusting, or from shooting in very bright
conditions without reducing exposure. How to investigate: Check the exposure slider — is it set too high? Check if the scene is
very bright. Corrective steps: Tap the subject. If it is too bright, slide the exposure slider down. How to confirm resolved: The
subject has natural colour and visible detail in both highlights and shadows. How to prevent recurrence: Check the photo
immediately after taking it. If it looks washed out, adjust and retake.
K.4 Blurry photos from camera shake
Symptoms: The whole photo is blurred, as if smeared slightly. Likely cause: The photographer's hands moved during the
exposure, especially in low light when the shutter is slower. How to investigate: Ask the learner how they were holding the
phone. Were they using one hand? Were they standing still or moving? Corrective steps: Hold the phone with both hands,
elbows tucked. Rest on a solid surface if possible. Take the photo gently — do not jab the shutter button. How to confirm
resolved: The photo is sharp, not smeared. How to prevent recurrence: Always use the two-handed hold. In low light, use a
surface or a tripod.
K.5 Blurry photos from subject movement
Symptoms: The subject is blurry but the background is sharp. Likely cause: The subject moved during the exposure — a
person turned their head, a child moved, a hand shifted. How to investigate: Is the background sharp but the subject blurry? If
so, the camera was steady but the subject moved. Corrective steps: Ask the subject to hold still. Take the photo quickly after
focusing. Take several frames and pick the sharpest. How to confirm resolved: The subject is sharp. How to prevent
recurrence: For moving subjects, take many frames and choose the best. For portraits, ask the person to hold still and breathe
out before you shoot.
K.6 Leaving beauty filters on
Symptoms: People in photos look unnatural — skin is too smooth, eyes are too large, facial features are distorted. Likely
cause: A beauty or smooth filter is active in the camera app or social media app. How to investigate: Look at a portrait photo
— does the person's skin look like skin, or like plastic? Corrective steps: Turn off all beauty, smooth and face filters in the
camera app settings. Turn off any "beauty mode" in the social media app before posting. How to confirm resolved: People in
photos look like real people with natural skin texture. How to prevent recurrence: Check camera settings before every shoot.
Make "filters off" part of the pre-shoot routine.
K.7 Not taking several frames
Symptoms: The photographer takes one photo, it is slightly blurry or the subject blinked, and they have no alternative. Likely
cause: The habit of taking a single frame and moving on. How to investigate: Ask the learner how many photos they took of
their last subject. If the answer is one, this is the problem. Corrective steps: Take at least three frames of every scene. Review
them and pick the best. How to confirm resolved: The learner always has a sharp, usable photo. How to prevent recurrence:
Make "take three" a rule. It costs nothing — storage is cheap.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual photo, ask
the learner what they did, and confirm the cause before correcting. Teaching learners to diagnose before fixing is part of
becoming a photographer.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. In your own words, what makes a good photograph? Name the five quality words and explain one of them.
Q2. Multiple choice. Your photo of a person against a bright window comes out with the person very dark and almost invisible.
What is the most likely reason? a) The phone's camera is broken. b) The camera exposed for the bright window, not for the
person. c) The photo needs a beauty filter. d) The person was standing too close to the window.
Q3. Explain in your own words. What is the exposure triangle? Name the three parts and explain what happens when one of
them is set wrong.
Q4. Practical identification. A classmate's photo is blurry — the whole image looks smeared. What are the two most likely
causes, and how would you fix each one?
Q5. Matching. Match each term to its plain-English meaning: aperture, shutter speed, ISO. a) How sensitive the sensor is to
light b) How wide the lens opening is c) How long the shutter stays open
Q6. Scenario. You are photographing a friend's product (a handbag) for her WhatsApp Business. The photo comes out sharp
but the handbag is too dark to see the details. What are two things you could do to fix this?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. A good photograph has five qualities: clarity (sharpness), light (correct exposure), composition (intentional arrangement),
moment (right instant) and purpose (does the job it was meant to do). Explanation of any one: e.g., clarity means the subject is
sharp and you can see the details when you zoom in. (Accept any answer that names the five words and explains one correctly.)
A2. (b) The camera exposed for the bright window, not for the person. Fix: tap the person on the screen to set focus and
exposure on them, then use the exposure slider if needed.
A3. The exposure triangle is the three camera settings that control how bright or dark a photo is: aperture (how wide the lens
opening is), shutter speed (how long the shutter stays open) and ISO (how sensitive the sensor is to light). If aperture is too small
or shutter is too fast in low light, the photo comes out dark. If ISO is too high, the photo comes out grainy. If the shutter is too
slow and the subject moves, the photo comes out blurry. (Accept any answer that names all three parts and explains at least one
consequence correctly.)
A4. The two most likely causes: (1) camera shake — the photographer's hands moved during the exposure. Fix: hold the phone
with both hands, elbows tucked, and rest on a solid surface. (2) subject movement — the subject moved during the exposure.
Fix: ask the subject to hold still and take several frames. (Accept both causes with correct fixes.)
A5. aperture = (b); shutter speed = (c); ISO = (a).
A6. Two things: (1) Tap the handbag on the screen to set focus and exposure on it — this tells the camera to expose for the
handbag, not the bright background. (2) Use the exposure slider to brighten the photo after tapping. (3) Alternatively, move the
handbag away from the bright background into more even light. (Accept any two correct actions.)

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that a good photograph is not luck — it is the result of deliberate choices. You
learned the five quality words: clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose. You learned the exposure triangle in simple
terms — aperture, shutter speed and ISO — and how they control whether your photo is bright, dark or blurry. You learned
that a phone camera is a legitimate tool, what megapixels really mean, and how to control focus by tapping, locking and
checking. You set up your camera properly — grid on, high resolution, beauty filters off — and you practised the 5-step
sharpness routine: steady, focus, check exposure, check background, take several frames. You took nine photos and diagnosed
why some worked and some did not.
Five important takeaways:
1. A good photograph has five qualities: clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose.
2. The exposure triangle — aperture, shutter speed and ISO — controls brightness; on a phone you influence it through tap-
to-focus and the exposure slider.
3. Megapixels measure detail quantity, not quality — a 12-megapixel phone photo is more than enough for business use.
4. Tap to focus, lock focus and focus check — focus is a choice, not an accident.
5. The 5-step sharpness routine produces sharp photos reliably: steady, focus, check exposure, check background, take several
frames.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I name the five quality words and explain what each means?

Can I set up my phone camera: grid on, high resolution, beauty filters off?
Can I tap to focus, lock focus and focus check?
Can I run the 5-step sharpness routine without looking at my notes?
Can I take three photos of the same subject and pick the sharpest one?
Connection to the next session. You now know how to make a photo sharp and correctly exposed. But a sharp, well-lit photo
can still be boring — if the composition is poor. Next session we train the eye. You will learn the rule of thirds, framing, leading

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner taps the subject before every photo without being reminded.
Learner holds the phone with both hands and checks the background before shooting.
Learner diagnoses their own photos accurately ("this one is dark because I tapped the bright wall").
Learner can explain the exposure triangle in their own words, even if imperfectly.
Learner takes multiple frames and selects the best.
Learner asks permission before photographing a person.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"A better phone will fix my photos." → No — the photographer's choices matter more than the camera.
"I can fix everything in editing." → Editing can improve a good photo; it cannot rescue a badly exposed or blurry one.
"Focus is automatic — I do not need to think about it." → The camera focuses where you tap; if you do not tap, it guesses.
"Beauty filters make photos look better." → They create unrealistic images that can mislead clients.
"One photo is enough." → Always take several frames; one might be blurry or the subject might blink.
"Megapixels are the most important spec." → Megapixels measure detail quantity; sensor quality and the photographer's
choices matter more.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Tap-and-focus drill: Place three objects at different distances. The learner taps each one in turn and takes a photo. Review
all three — each subject should be sharp.
2. Exposure slider drill: Photograph the same subject with the slider at minimum, middle and maximum. Review all three —
the middle or slightly above should be correctly exposed.
3. Diagnosis drill: Show five photos (dark, bright, blurry, good, flat). The learner labels each and explains why. Review together.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a printed step-by-step card with the 5-step sharpness routine and the camera setup checklist.
Pair the learner with a confident learner for the practical.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full lecture, demonstration, guided practical and independent exercise.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to photograph the same subject using all three exposure triangle variables deliberately
— tap to change aperture effect (by tapping different brightness areas), use a slower shutter (by photographing a moving
subject like a fan or flowing water), and explain what happened in each case. They should also research and present one
additional camera setting not covered in the session (e.g., white balance or scene modes).
Timing guidance. The priority is learners taking photos with the sharpness routine (Sections H and the assignment). If the room
is slow, shorten the exposure triangle lecture (F.2) to the key points — the three settings and how to influence them on a phone
— and protect the practical time. A learner who leaves without taking nine sharp photos has no foundation for Session 2.
