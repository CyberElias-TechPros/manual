# Composition and Lighting

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: "See and Shoot" (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (practical, eye-training focus)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 — the learner should be able to tap to focus, adjust exposure and produce a sharp, correctly exposed photo. The 5-step sharpness routine is assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 you made photos sharp and correctly exposed. Today you learn to make them beautiful — by composing with intention and using light deliberately. A sharp, well-lit photo with poor composition is still a weak photo.

**Preparation before class:** Print the four-technique composition cards (rule of thirds, framing, leading lines, symmetry/perspective) as visual examples for the opening; a projector connected to the instructor's phone for live composition demonstration; two identical small objects (two cups, two bottles) for the good-light/bad-light comparison; a window with natural light and a shaded outdoor area (or two very different indoor lighting spots) for the light study; the "Composition Technique Log" (Section H) printed per learner. Instructor note on tone: This session is about training the eye. Keep it visual — show more than you tell. Learners should feel the difference between good and bad light physically (squinting, leaning in) before they can name it. Celebrate every "I see it now" moment.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Apply the rule of thirds to a live subject, placing the subject on the grid intersection rather than dead centre, and explain
why this is stronger.
2. Use framing — shooting through a natural frame (a doorway, window, tree branches) to add depth and focus attention on
the subject.
3. Identify and use leading lines — paths, edges, walls, railings that draw the viewer's eye toward the subject.
4. Recognise and use symmetry and perspective — balanced compositions for formal effect, and converging lines to
create depth.
5. Identify golden hour, window light and open shade as flattering natural light, and explain why harsh midday sun and
backlight are unflattering.
6. Produce a four-image composition series (one image per technique) and a four-image light study (same subject in four
different lighting conditions), each image visibly demonstrating the named technique.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner with a working camera.
Instructor's phone connected to a projector or large screen for live demonstration.
A phone tripod or improvised stand for the instructor demonstration.
Two identical small objects (two cups, two bottles, two fruits) for the light comparison.
A window with natural light and a shaded spot (or two contrasting indoor lighting areas) for the light study.
Software
The phone's built-in camera app (default shooting tool).
No editing software needed today; editing begins in Session 4.
Accounts
None required this session.
Sample files
Four composition technique cards (Section F) — each card shows a photograph demonstrating one technique with a
brief explanation.
Good light / bad light comparison — two photos of the same subject in flattering and unflattering light, printed or
projected.
Worksheets
Worksheet 2.1 — "Composition Technique Log" (a table: technique name, description, my photo description, why it
works).
Worksheet 2.2 — "Light Study Record" (a table: lighting condition, time/place, description of light, result).
Tools
A whiteboard and markers for drawing composition diagrams.
A ruler or straight edge for drawing grid lines.
Internet
Not required. The session works fully offline.
Alternative for learners who lack a capable phone
Any phone with a working camera works. A learner without a phone may pair with a classmate, use an academy phone or
use an academy computer with a webcam. They must tell the instructor at the start so support can be arranged.
Safety note: When photographing outdoors or near windows, be aware of surroundings. Do not stand in roads, do not lean out
of windows, and do not photograph anyone without their consent.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show the same subject — a chair in a room — photographed three ways:
Photo A (centred, flat): The chair is dead centre in the frame, shot from standing height, with a cluttered background. It
looks like a random snapshot.
Photo B (rule of thirds, intentional): The chair is placed on the right third of the frame, shot from a slightly lower angle,
with a clean background. It looks deliberate and pleasing.
Photo C (with leading lines): The chair is framed by a doorway or at the end of a corridor, with lines drawing the eye toward
it. It looks like a designed image.
What the instructor says:
"Same chair, same room. But Photo B and Photo C look like someone made them — they look like they belong in a
magazine or a shop advert. Photo A looks like something you would delete. Hands up — which photo would you use for a
furniture business? … B and C, almost everyone. Now tell me: what is different about them?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "the chair is not in the middle," "it looks nicer," "the background is
cleaner," "it looks professional," "it has depth." Accept all answers and write the key phrases on the board: off-centre, clean
background, depth, intentional, pleasing.
Then reveal the lesson: Say clearly:
"Composition is not a mystery — it is a set of choices. Where do you put the subject in the frame? What do you include
around it? What angle do you shoot from? Today you learn four composition techniques that professionals use every day.
And then you learn about light — the difference between a snapshot and a photograph is often not the camera, it is the
light. By the end of this session, you will take photos that look like someone made them on purpose."
Why this opener works: It uses a subject every learner can recognise, it produces instant visual recognition of quality, and it
introduces the four techniques and the light concept before any technical explanation.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five quality words for a good photograph? Which two did we focus on in Session 1?"
Expected answer: Clarity, light, composition, moment and purpose. Session 1 focused on clarity (sharpness) and light
(correct exposure).
Common gap: Learners may forget the five words. Clarify: "Clarity means sharp. Light means correctly exposed. Today we
add composition — how the subject is arranged — and we go deeper into light."
Question 2: "What is the 5-step sharpness routine? Name the steps."
Expected answer: Steady hold, tap to focus, check exposure, check background, take several frames.
Common gap: Learners may remember the steps but not the order. Clarify: "The order matters. Steady first, then focus, then
exposure, then background, then frames."
Question 3: "What happens when you tap a bright part of the scene instead of the subject?"
Expected answer: The camera exposes for the bright area, so the subject goes dark.
Common gap: Learners may say "the photo gets brighter." Clarify: "The background gets correctly exposed, but the subject
goes dark. The camera exposes for wherever you tap."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 1 you learned to make photos sharp and correctly exposed. That is the foundation. But a sharp, well-lit photo of
a chair in the middle of the frame is still not a great photo. Today you learn to arrange the photo — composition — and to
choose the light. These are the two skills that turn a snapshot into a photograph."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the 5-step routine, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing it on the board before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What composition is and why it matters
The idea. Composition is how you arrange everything inside the frame — the subject, the background, the empty space, the
lines, the shapes. It is the difference between pointing a camera and making a photograph.
Plain-English definition. Composition is deciding what goes in the photo, where everything sits in the frame, and what angle
you shoot from. It is the photographer's way of guiding the viewer's eye to what matters.
Why it matters. A well-composed photo is pleasing to look at. It feels intentional — like someone made choices. A poorly
composed photo feels accidental — like the camera just happened to be pointed that way. For business, a well-composed
product photo makes the product look more valuable. For a portrait, a well-composed photo makes the person look more
confident and appealing.
The key insight (say this slowly):
"Composition is not about following rules blindly. It is about understanding a few principles and then making deliberate
choices. Today you learn four techniques. After this session, you will see them everywhere — in adverts, in magazines, in
Instagram posts. And you will start using them yourself."
Real-life comparison. Composition is like arranging a shop display. You can throw everything on the shelf randomly, or you
can place the most important item at eye level, give it space around it, and remove anything distracting. Both are displays, but
one makes people stop and look, and the other makes them walk past. Composition is arranging the photo so people stop and
look.
Misconception to correct. "Composition rules are rigid — you must always follow them exactly." No. The rules are starting
points. Once you understand them, you can break them deliberately for creative effect. But you must first learn the rules before
you can break them with intention.
F.2 Technique 1 — The rule of thirds
The idea. The rule of thirds is the most fundamental composition technique. It says: do not put the subject in the centre of the
frame. Instead, place it on one of the grid intersections — the points where the horizontal and vertical grid lines cross.
How it works:
1. Turn on grid lines in your camera settings (you did this in Session 1).
2. The grid divides the screen into nine equal rectangles.
3. The four points where the grid lines intersect are the "power points."
4. Place your subject — or the most important part of the subject (usually the eyes in a portrait) — on or near one of these
four points.
Why it works:
A centred subject feels static and balanced — but also boring. The eye has nowhere to travel.
An off-centred subject creates tension and movement. The eye moves around the frame, exploring.
The empty space around the subject (called "negative space") gives the subject room to breathe and makes it feel
important.
How to use it in practice:
For a portrait: place the subject's eyes on the top horizontal line, on one of the vertical lines. Leave space in the direction the
person is looking.
For a product: place the product on one of the four intersections, with the label facing the camera.
For a landscape: place the horizon on the top or bottom horizontal line — not in the middle.
Worked example:
Weak: A photo of a person, dead centre, looking at the camera, with equal space on both sides. The photo feels flat and
stiff.
Strong: The same person, placed on the right vertical line, with their eyes on the top horizontal line, looking toward the left
side of the frame (into the empty space). The photo feels dynamic and intentional.
Real-life comparison. The rule of thirds is like seating at a dinner table. If you sit exactly in the middle of the table, you are the
centre of attention but the table feels stiff. If you sit slightly to one side, the table feels more natural and conversational, and
people can still see you clearly. The rule of thirds is placing your subject slightly off-centre so the photo feels natural.
Common mistake. Placing the subject on the grid intersection but leaving no space in the direction they are looking. If a person
is looking left, place them on the right side of the frame so they have space to "look into."
F.3 Technique 2 — Framing
The idea. Framing means using elements in the scene to create a frame within the frame — a natural border around the
subject that draws the eye inward.
What can be used as a frame:
A doorway or window
Tree branches or leaves
An archway or gate
A corridor or tunnel
Two walls creating a natural frame
A person's arms or hands (in creative portraits)
How it works:
1. Find a natural frame in the environment — a doorway, a window, branches.
2. Position yourself so the frame surrounds your subject.
3. Shoot through the frame, keeping both the frame and the subject in focus (if possible).
4. The frame draws the viewer's eye toward the subject and adds depth to the photo.
Why it works:
The frame creates a sense of depth — the photo feels like you are looking into a scene, not just at it.
The frame focuses attention — the viewer's eye is guided to the subject, not distracted by the surroundings.
The frame adds context — a doorway tells you the person is inside, branches tell you the person is outdoors.
Worked example:
Weak: A photo of a person standing in an open field, with nothing around them. The photo is flat — there is no sense of
place.
Strong: The same person, photographed through a doorway or an archway. The door frame creates a natural border, the
person is framed within it, and the photo feels like a window into a moment.
Real-life comparison. Framing is like hanging a picture on a wall. The wall is the background, the frame is the border around
the picture, and the picture is the subject. Without a frame, the picture blends into the wall. With a frame, the picture stands out.
In photography, you use the environment to create that frame.
Common mistake. Using a frame that is too busy or distracting — the frame competes with the subject for attention. The
frame should enhance the subject, not overpower it.
F.4 Technique 3 — Leading lines
The idea. Leading lines are lines in the scene — paths, roads, walls, railings, fences, rivers, shadows — that lead the viewer's
eye toward the subject.
What can be leading lines:
A road or path leading to a building
A fence or wall leading to a person
A row of trees leading to a house
A river or stream leading to a bridge
Shadows created by light
Railway lines, power lines, staircases
How it works:
1. Look at the scene and identify any lines — straight or curved.
2. Position yourself so the lines lead from the foreground (near the camera) toward the subject (in the distance).
3. The lines act as arrows, guiding the viewer's eye through the photo to the subject.
Why it works:
Lines create a sense of journey — the viewer's eye travels from the foreground to the subject.
Lines add depth — they make the photo feel three-dimensional.
Lines create interest — a photo with leading lines feels more dynamic than a photo without them.
Worked example:
Weak: A photo of a market stall, shot straight on, with no lines. The photo is flat — everything is at the same distance.
Strong: The same market stall, shot from the end of the aisle, with the aisle lines leading the eye toward the stall. The photo
feels like you are walking toward the stall.
Real-life comparison. Leading lines are like the yellow line on a road. The line guides your eyes forward, toward where you are
going. In a photograph, leading lines guide the viewer's eyes toward the subject.
Common mistake. Lines that lead the eye away from the subject or out of the frame. Always check: do the lines lead toward the
subject, or do they lead somewhere else? If they lead away, reposition yourself.
F.5 Technique 4 — Symmetry and perspective
The idea. Symmetry means balance — both sides of the photo are similar or identical. Perspective means using depth and
distance to create a three-dimensional feeling. Together, they create formal, structured compositions.
Symmetry:
When to use it: architecture, reflections, formal portraits, anything with a clear centre line.
How to use it: place the subject in the centre of the frame, with both sides of the photo mirroring each other as closely as
possible.
Why it works: symmetry feels stable, formal and pleasing. It works well for buildings, doorways, faces and reflections.
Example: A building photographed straight on, with both sides equal. Or a reflection in a lake or a puddle — the top half
and bottom half are mirror images.
Perspective:
When to use it: any scene with depth — corridors, roads, hallways, rows of objects.
How to use it: place the camera at the start of the depth, with the subject at the far end. The converging lines (the sides of
the corridor, the edges of the road) create a strong sense of depth.
Why it works: perspective makes the photo feel immersive — like you could step into it.
Example: A photo down a long corridor, with the walls converging toward a door at the far end. The viewer feels like they
are standing at the start of the corridor.
Worked example:
Weak: A photo of a corridor, shot from the side, with no sense of depth. The photo is flat.
Strong: The same corridor, shot from one end, with the walls converging toward the far end. The photo feels deep and
immersive.
Real-life comparison. Symmetry is like a face — both eyes, both ears, both sides roughly the same. A symmetrical face feels
balanced and pleasing. Perspective is like looking down a long road — the road narrows in the distance, and you feel the
depth. Both are powerful composition tools.
Common mistake. For symmetry, even a small tilt ruins the effect. Use the grid lines to make sure the centre line is truly
centred. For perspective, if the camera is too far from the start of the depth, the effect is weak — get close to the beginning of
the lines.
F.6 What light is and why it matters
The idea. Light is the raw material of photography. Without light, there is no photograph. But not all light is equal. The quality,
direction and colour of light can make a subject look beautiful, dramatic, flat, harsh or unflattering.
The three qualities of light (teach these and return to them all session):
Quality Plain-English meaning Effect on the subject
Direction Where the light comes from
— front, side, top, behind
Front light is flat and safe; side light is dramatic and dimensional; top light
(midday sun) is harsh and unflattering; backlight (behind the subject) creates
silhouettes
Softness How spread out the light is
— soft or hard
Soft light (cloudy day, window light) is flattering and even; hard light (direct
sun) creates strong shadows and bright highlights
Colour The warmth or coolness of
the light — measured in
Kelvin
Warm light (sunrise, sunset, indoor bulbs) feels cozy and golden; cool light
(shade, overcast sky) feels clean and blue; neutral light (overcast day, open
shade) is balanced and true
Why light matters more than the camera. A ₦500,000 camera in bad light produces a bad photo. A ₦50,000 phone in good
light produces a beautiful photo. The camera records the light; the photographer chooses the light. This is why professional
photographers talk about light constantly — it is the most important variable.
Real-life comparison. Think of a person's face. In the soft light of a window, their face looks smooth, warm and pleasing. In the
harsh midday sun, their face has dark shadows under the eyes and nose, and they are squinting. Same person, same face —
completely different light, completely different result. Light is the difference.
Misconception to correct. "More light is always better." No. Too much light (direct midday sun) is harsh and unflattering. The
best light is often soft and indirect — the light of a cloudy day, a window, or open shade. Quality matters more than quantity.
F.7 The four natural light conditions every photographer must know
1. Golden hour (the best light of the day).
When: The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset.
What it looks like: Warm, golden, soft, directional light with long shadows.
Why it is beautiful: The low angle of the sun creates soft, flattering light with a warm glow. Skin looks smooth, colours look
rich, and everything feels magical.
How to use it: Shoot during golden hour whenever possible. Place the subject facing the sun (front-lit) for a warm glow, or
at an angle to the sun (side-lit) for dramatic shadows.
Nigerian context: In Port Harcourt, golden hour is roughly 6:00–7:00 AM and 5:30–6:30 PM (varies by season). The light
is consistent year-round near the equator.
2. Window light (the easiest indoor light).
When: Any time during the day, near a window.
What it looks like: Soft, directional light coming from one side.
Why it is beautiful: The window acts as a large, soft light source. The light is even and flattering, especially for portraits and
products.
How to use it: Place the subject near the window, facing the window (or at a 45-degree angle to it). Do not place the subject
with their back to the window — this creates a silhouette.
Nigerian context: Almost every home and shop in Port Harcourt has a window. This is the most accessible good light.
3. Open shade (the safest outdoor light).
When: Any time during the day, in a shaded area.
What it looks like: Soft, even, cool light — no direct sun on the subject.
Why it is beautiful: The shade eliminates harsh shadows and squinting. The light is even and flattering, especially for
portraits.
How to use it: Place the subject in the shade of a tree, a building, or an umbrella — but with a bright, open area in front of
them (not a dark wall). The subject should be in shade, but the light in front of them should be bright.
Nigerian context: Open shade is everywhere in Port Harcourt — under trees, under awnings, in covered walkways. It is the
easiest good light to find.
4. Harsh midday sun (the worst light — avoid when possible).
When: Roughly 11:00 AM to 2:00 PM.
What it looks like: Hard, overhead light with strong shadows under the eyes, nose and chin. Subjects squint. Colours look
washed out.
Why it is unflattering: The light comes from directly above, creating shadows in the worst places. It is too harsh for portraits
and can create washed-out colours.
How to handle it if you must shoot in it: Move the subject into open shade. If you cannot, use a reflector or white card to
bounce light into the shadow areas. Or embrace the harsh light for creative effect (strong shadows can be dramatic).
Nigerian context: The midday sun in Port Harcourt is extremely strong. Avoid shooting portraits or products in direct
midday sun unless you have no choice.
Backlight — the most common mistake:
What it is: Light coming from behind the subject, toward the camera.
What happens: The subject becomes a silhouette — dark, with no detail. The background is bright.
Why it happens: The camera exposes for the bright background, not the dark subject.
How to fix it: Move the subject so the light is coming from the front or side, not behind. Or tap the subject on the screen
and brighten the exposure. Or use a reflector to bounce light onto the subject's face.
Golden hour exception: Backlight during golden hour can be beautiful — it creates a warm glow around the subject (called
"rim light"). This is intentional backlight, not accidental backlight.
Real-life comparison. The four light conditions are like four different shirts:
Golden hour is a beautiful traditional outfit — rich, warm, special.
Window light is a clean white shirt — simple, professional, always works.
Open shade is a casual polo — relaxed, comfortable, safe.
Harsh midday sun is a fluorescent vest — bright, harsh, unflattering, best avoided.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Composition is how you arrange the subject, background and space within the frame.
The rule of thirds: place the subject on a grid intersection, not dead centre.
Framing: use a natural frame (doorway, window, branches) to draw the eye to the subject.
Leading lines: use lines in the scene to guide the viewer's eye toward the subject.
Symmetry and perspective: use balance and depth for formal, immersive compositions.
Light has three qualities: direction, softness and colour.
The four natural light conditions: golden hour (best), window light (easy), open shade (safe), harsh midday sun (avoid).
Backlight is the most common mistake — fix it by repositioning or adjusting exposure.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A phone connected to the projector, camera app open, grid lines on.
A subject: a bottle, a cup, or a piece of fruit on a table.
A second subject for the light study: two identical objects (two cups or two bottles).
A window with natural light.
A shaded area (indoor corner or under a tree/awning outside).
A white card or reflector (A4 paper works).
G.2 Demonstration 1 — rule of thirds (3 minutes)
Say: "Watch the screen. Grid lines are on. I am going to place the bottle on one of the four grid intersections."
Step 1: Move the phone so the bottle is on the right vertical line, on the top horizontal line. "The bottle is on a power point. It is
off-centre."
Step 2: Take the photo. Show the result. "See how the bottle has space on the left? It feels intentional, not accidental."
Step 3: Move the bottle to the centre. Take the photo. Show the result. "Now it is centred. Compare — which one feels more
dynamic?" The class should say the off-centre one.
Say: "The rule of thirds is not magic — it is simply about not putting everything in the middle. The eye likes to move around
the frame. Off-centre gives it somewhere to go."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — framing (2 minutes)
Say: "Now I will use a doorway to frame the bottle. Watch."
Step 1: Position the camera so the bottle is visible through a doorway. The door frame surrounds the bottle.
Step 2: Tap the bottle to focus. Take the photo. Show the result.
Say: "The doorway is a natural frame. It draws your eye to the bottle. Without the frame, the bottle is just sitting on a table. With
the frame, the bottle feels like it is in a scene — you are looking into something."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — leading lines (2 minutes)
Say: "Now I will use leading lines. I am going to place the bottle at the end of a row of books, so the edges of the books lead the
eye toward it."
Step 1: Arrange a row of books leading toward the bottle. Position the camera at the start of the row.
Step 2: Tap the bottle to focus. Take the photo. Show the result.
Say: "The edges of the books are lines. They lead your eye from the foreground — near the camera — to the bottle in the
distance. Without the lines, the bottle is just there. With the lines, your eye travels to it."
G.5 Demonstration 4 — light study (6 minutes)
Say: "Now the most important part — light. I have two identical cups. I am going to photograph the same cup in four different
lights and you will see the difference."
Light 1 — Window light: Place the cup near a window. Photograph it. Show the result. "Soft, even, flattering. You can see the
colour and texture of the cup clearly."
Light 2 — Open shade: Place the cup in a shaded area (under a tree, in a covered walkway). Photograph it. Show the result.
"Even, cool, safe. No harsh shadows. This is the easiest good outdoor light."
Light 3 — Direct sun (if available) or a bright indoor spot with harsh overhead light: Place the cup in direct bright light.
Photograph it. Show the result. "Harsh shadows, bright highlights, the cup looks washed out and unflattering. This is the light to
avoid."
Light 4 — Backlight: Place the cup with a bright window behind it. Photograph it without adjusting. Show the result. "The cup
is dark — a silhouette. The camera exposed for the bright window." Then tap the cup, brighten the exposure, retake. Show the
result. "Now the cup is visible. Same cup, same position — the only thing I changed was the exposure."
Say: "Same cup, same camera. The only thing that changed was the light. That is why light matters more than the camera. A
photographer's job is to find good light — or fix bad light."
G.6 Questions to ask during the demo
"Which photo feels more dynamic — the centred one or the off-centre one?" (Off-centre — rule of thirds.)
"What does the doorway do for the bottle?" (It frames it — draws the eye in.)
"What do the edges of the books do?" (They lead the eye to the bottle — leading lines.)
"Which light made the cup look best?" (Window light or open shade.)
"Why was the cup dark when the window was behind it?" (Backlight — the camera exposed for the bright window, not the
cup.)
"Can you fix backlight?" (Yes — tap the subject and brighten the exposure, or move the subject.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner produces a four-image composition series (one photo per
technique) and begins a four-image light study.
Starting materials: Learner's own phone with grid lines on; a subject in the classroom (a bottle, a cup, a bag, a plant, or a
classmate who has agreed); the printed Composition Technique Log.
Procedure — Part 1: Composition Series (12 minutes)
The instructor calls out each technique. Learners find a subject and apply the technique. Instructor walks the room.
1. Rule of thirds: Find a subject. Turn on grid lines. Place the subject on one of the four grid intersections. Take three photos.
Pick the strongest.
2. Framing: Find a natural frame — a doorway, a window, a gap between two objects. Shoot through the frame so the subject
is framed. Take three photos. Pick the strongest.
3. Leading lines: Find or create leading lines — the edge of a table, a row of books, a corridor. Position yourself so the lines
lead to the subject. Take three photos. Pick the strongest.
4. Symmetry or perspective: Find a symmetrical scene (a door, a face, a reflection) or a perspective scene (a corridor, a row
of objects). Compose for symmetry or depth. Take three photos. Pick the strongest.
For each photo, the learner writes one line in the Composition Technique Log: "Technique: ______. What I did: ______."
Procedure — Part 2: Light Study (6 minutes)
5. Choose one subject (a bottle, a cup, or a classmate's hand — something that can stay still).
6. Photograph it in two different lighting situations available in or near the classroom:
Near a window (soft light).
In a shaded area or a dim corner (different light).
7. For each photo, write one line in the Light Study Record: "Light: ______. What I see: ______."
Expected result: Four composition photos (one per technique) and two light study photos, each with a written description.
Points to pause and predict:
After the rule of thirds photo: "Is your subject on a grid intersection, or is it still in the middle? Check the grid."
After the framing photo: "Does the frame surround your subject, or is it just something in the background? If it does not
surround, it is not a frame."
After the light study: "Compare your two light photos. Which one looks better? Why?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner cannot find a natural frame → Suggest a doorway, a window, or even the gap between two chairs.
Subject is centred despite trying → Have them look at the grid lines on the screen, not just the subject. Move the camera,
not the subject.
Leading lines are not leading anywhere → Ask: "Where do the lines go? Do they go to the subject or somewhere else?
Reposition."
Light study photos look the same → The learner may have photographed in the same light twice. Have them move to a
genuinely different lighting situation.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Rule of thirds photo — subject on a grid intersection
Framing photo — subject framed by a natural frame
Leading lines photo — lines lead to the subject
Symmetry or perspective photo — balanced or deep
Light study photo 1 — near a window
Light study photo 2 — in shade or a different light
Composition Technique Log completed for all four techniques
Light Study Record completed for both photos

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task: Complete the light study — photograph the same subject in four different lighting conditions and diagnose the light.
Setup: Choose one subject — a bottle, a cup, a fruit, or a classmate's hand (with permission). The subject must stay in the
same position; only the light changes.
You will photograph it in four places (find these in or near the classroom):
1. Near a window (soft, directional light).
2. In open shade (soft, even light — under a tree, in a covered area, or in a shaded corner).
3. In direct bright light (harsh light — in a sunny spot or under a bright overhead light).
4. With a bright background behind it (backlight — place the subject in front of a window or bright opening).
Requirements:
1. For each lighting condition, take at least one photo using the 5-step sharpness routine (from Session 1).
2. After each photo, label it in the Light Study Record: is the light soft or hard, warm or cool, flattering or unflattering?
3. Write one sentence explaining what you see in each photo.
Constraints:
Use only the built-in camera app — no filters, no editing.
The subject must be the same in all four photos (same position, same angle if possible).
Do not leave the building — find the four lighting situations inside or immediately outside.
Time allowance: 15 minutes.
Success criteria:
Four photos taken in four different lighting conditions.
Each photo is labelled with a light diagnosis (soft/hard, warm/cool, flattering/unflattering).
At least one photo is clearly flattering (the window or shade photo).
The backlight photo shows a visible difference from the others.
Hints available if stuck:
Hint 1: "Soft light" means no harsh shadows — the light is spread out. "Hard light" means strong, defined shadows.
Hint 2: If you cannot find open shade, a shaded corner of a room works — anywhere the subject is not in direct light.
Hint 3: For backlight, place the subject between the camera and a bright window. The subject will look dark — that is the
point.
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (model solution — do not show learners):
Photo 1 — near a window: Soft, directional, flattering light. The subject is clearly visible with gentle shadows. Diagnosis: soft,
neutral-warm, flattering. Reason: "The window acts as a large, soft light source."
Photo 2 — open shade: Soft, even, cool light. The subject is evenly lit with no harsh shadows. Diagnosis: soft, cool, flattering.
Reason: "The shade eliminates direct sun, creating even light."
Photo 3 — direct bright light: Hard, harsh, unflattering light. Strong shadows, bright highlights, the subject may look washed
out. Diagnosis: hard, warm, unflattering. Reason: "Direct light creates harsh shadows and bright spots."
Photo 4 — backlight: The subject is dark (silhouette) against a bright background. Diagnosis: hard, unflattering (as shot).
Reason: "The camera exposed for the bright background, not the subject. Fix: tap the subject and brighten."
Learner marking (out of 10):
Four photos taken in four different lighting conditions (4)
Each photo correctly diagnosed (soft/hard, warm/cool, flattering/unflattering) (4)
At least one photo clearly flattering (1)
Written reasons are accurate (1)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze is a tailor in Port Harcourt. She makes beautiful ankara dresses and sells them through WhatsApp and
Instagram. She takes photos of her dresses on a mannequin in her shop, usually in the afternoon, and posts them. Her photos
have the dresses looking flat, washed out, and unflattering — the colours look dull, and the fabric texture is hard to see.
Customers often say the dresses look better in person than in the photos.
Relevant facts.
Adaeze's phone is a recent model with a good camera.
She photographs the mannequin in the middle of her shop, under fluorescent ceiling lights.
She does not think about composition — she just points and shoots.
Her shop has a large window with beautiful natural light, but she never uses it.
The background of her photos is cluttered — fabric rolls, scissors, other dresses, her sewing machine.
Decision point. What is the main reason Adaeze's photos are not showing her dresses at their best?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a better phone with a bigger camera sensor.
2. Learn to use natural light (her window), improve her composition, and clean up her background.
3. Hire a professional fashion photographer for every new dress.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Adaeze's phone is already capable of good photographs. Her problem is threefold: poor
light (fluorescent ceiling lights instead of natural window light), poor composition (centred, cluttered, no intention), and no
attention to the background. By moving the mannequin near her window, using the rule of thirds, and cleaning up the
background, she can produce photos that show her dresses beautifully — for free.
Specific fixes for Adaeze:
Move the mannequin near the window (soft, natural light).
Place the mannequin on a grid intersection, not dead centre (rule of thirds).
Remove everything from the background except a plain wall or a clean surface (clean background).
Photograph in the morning or late afternoon when the light is softest (avoid harsh midday sun).
Take several frames and pick the sharpest.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 costs money (₦150,000–₦400,000) and does not solve the problem — a better phone used in bad light with
poor composition still produces bad photos.
Approach 2 is free, takes a few hours to learn, and the skill stays with Adaeze forever. She can photograph every new dress
herself.
Approach 3 works but costs money every time. Adaeze makes many dresses; hiring a photographer for each one is not
sustainable for a small business.
Professional connection. Across Nigeria, small businesses lose sales because their product photos do not show the products at
their best. A tailor, a food vendor, a hair stylist, a jeweller — all need good photographs. The difference between a photo that
sells and a photo that does not is often not the product or the camera — it is the light and the composition.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Centring every subject
Symptoms: Every photo has the subject dead centre in the frame, with equal space on both sides. Likely cause: The
photographer is pointing at the subject without thinking about placement. How to investigate: Look at the grid lines on the
screen. Is the subject on the vertical centre line? Corrective steps: Move the subject to one of the four grid intersections. Leave
more space on the side the subject is facing or looking toward. How to confirm resolved: The subject is on a grid intersection,
not the centre line. How to prevent recurrence: Make "off-centre" a habit. Before every photo, ask: "Is my subject in the
middle? If so, where should it go?"
K.2 Cluttered backgrounds
Symptoms: The subject is lost in a busy background — objects, people, patterns competing for attention. Likely cause: The
photographer is focusing on the subject and ignoring everything else in the frame. How to investigate: Look at the photo —
can you identify the subject immediately, or does your eye wander? Corrective steps: Move the camera to change the
background. Or move the subject. Or use a wider aperture (on phones, use portrait mode if available) to blur the background.
Or simply clean up the background before shooting. How to confirm resolved: The subject is the clear focus of the photo; the
background is clean and uncluttered. How to prevent recurrence: Before every photo, scan the entire frame — not just the
subject. Ask: "What else is in this photo? Does it help or distract?"
K.3 Harsh midday sun for portraits
Symptoms: The subject is squinting, with dark shadows under the eyes, nose and chin. Colours look washed out. Likely cause:
Photographing in direct midday sun (roughly 11 AM–2 PM). How to investigate: Check the time of day and the direction of
the shadows. If shadows are directly under the subject, the sun is overhead. Corrective steps: Move the subject into open shade
(under a tree, in a covered area). Or wait for a better time (morning or late afternoon). Or use a reflector to bounce light into the
shadows. How to confirm resolved: The subject is not squinting, shadows are soft or absent, colours are natural. How to
prevent recurrence: Plan outdoor shoots for morning or late afternoon. If you must shoot at midday, find shade.
K.4 Backlight — subject is a silhouette
Symptoms: The subject is very dark, almost a silhouette, against a bright background. Likely cause: The light is coming from
behind the subject, toward the camera. The camera exposes for the bright background. How to investigate: Is there a bright
area (window, sky, light source) behind the subject? Corrective steps: Move the subject so the light comes from the front or
side. Or tap the subject on the screen and brighten the exposure. Or use a reflector to bounce light onto the subject. How to
confirm resolved: The subject is clearly visible with detail, not a dark silhouette. How to prevent recurrence: Before every
photo, check where the light is coming from. If it is behind the subject, reposition.
K.5 Lines that lead nowhere
Symptoms: Leading lines in the photo lead the eye to the edge of the frame or to a distracting object, not the subject. Likely
cause: The photographer saw lines but did not check where they lead. How to investigate: Trace the lines with your finger.
Where do they go? To the subject or somewhere else? Corrective steps: Reposition yourself so the lines lead to the subject. If
they cannot lead to the subject, find different lines or a different angle. How to confirm resolved: The lines lead the viewer's
eye directly to the subject. How to prevent recurrence: Before taking the photo, trace the lines and confirm they lead where
you want the eye to go.
K.6 Using a frame that distracts
Symptoms: The natural frame (doorway, branches) is so busy or colourful that it competes with the subject. Likely cause: The
photographer saw a frame and used it without considering how it looks. How to investigate: Look at the photo — is the frame
more interesting than the subject? Corrective steps: Find a simpler frame. Or change the angle so the frame is less prominent.
Or use a wider aperture (portrait mode) to blur the frame slightly. How to confirm resolved: The frame enhances the subject,
not competes with it. How to prevent recurrence: Choose frames that are simple and complementary to the subject.
K.7 Symmetry that is not symmetrical
Symptoms: A supposedly symmetrical photo where one side is slightly different from the other — a door frame is tilted, one
side has an object the other does not. Likely cause: The camera is not perfectly aligned with the centre line of the subject. How
to investigate: Look at the grid lines. Is the centre of the subject exactly on the vertical centre line? Corrective steps: Adjust the
camera position until the subject is perfectly centred. Use the grid lines as a guide. Take the photo from directly in front of the
subject, not at an angle. How to confirm resolved: Both sides of the photo are as identical as possible. How to prevent
recurrence: For symmetry, take time to align carefully. Small tilts ruin the effect.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — look at the actual photo,
trace the lines, check the light direction, and confirm the cause before correcting.

A2. (b) On the right vertical line, looking left into the empty space. The person should have space in the direction they are

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that composition is how you arrange the photo — where the subject sits, what is
around it, and what angle you shoot from. You learned four techniques: the rule of thirds (off-centre is stronger than centred),
framing (use a natural frame to draw the eye in), leading lines (use lines to guide the viewer to the subject), and symmetry and
perspective (balance and depth). Then you learned about light — the raw material of photography. You learned the four
natural light conditions: golden hour (best), window light (easiest), open shade (safest) and harsh midday sun (avoid). You
learned that backlight is the most common mistake and how to fix it. You took a four-image composition series and a four-
image light study.
Five important takeaways:
1. Composition is a set of choices — the rule of thirds, framing, leading lines, symmetry and perspective.
2. Off-centre is stronger than centred — place the subject on a grid intersection.
3. Light matters more than the camera — a phone in good light beats an expensive camera in bad light.
4. Golden hour, window light and open shade are your friends; harsh midday sun is not.
5. Backlight turns your subject into a silhouette — fix it by repositioning or adjusting exposure.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I place a subject on a grid intersection without thinking about it?
Can I find a natural frame in any environment?
Can I identify where the light is coming from — front, side, top or behind?
Can I explain the difference between soft light and hard light?
Can I choose a flattering time and place to shoot a portrait?
Can I take a four-photo light study and explain why each photo looks the way it does?
Connection to the next session. You now know how to make a photo sharp, correctly exposed, well-composed and beautifully
lit. Next session you apply all of that to the three subjects that earn money: people, products and events. You will learn to direct
a simple portrait pose, build a clean product scene, and work an event to capture key moments sharply. Consent is confirmed
for every person photographed. This is where your foundations folder comes together.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner places the subject off-centre without being reminded.
Learner scans the background before taking a photo and removes distractions.
Learner identifies the direction and quality of light before shooting.
Learner moves the subject or themselves to find better light.
Learner produces photos where the technique is visible — you can look at the photo and name the technique used.
Learner diagnoses their own photos accurately.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Composition rules are too restrictive — I want to be creative." → Learn the rules first, then break them deliberately.
"I can fix the light in editing." → Editing can improve exposure slightly; it cannot fix harsh shadows or a silhouette.
"More light is better." → Soft, indirect light is often better than bright, direct light.
"The background does not matter." → A cluttered background ruins an otherwise good photo.
"Backlight is always bad." → Intentional backlight (e.g., golden hour rim light) can be beautiful. The problem is accidental
backlight that creates a silhouette.
"I need to shoot at midday because that is when I am free." → Choose the light, then fit your schedule around it.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Grid drill: Take 10 photos of any subject, each with the subject on a different grid intersection. Review — all should be off-
centre.
2. Frame hunt: Walk around the building and find five natural frames (doorways, windows, gaps between objects).
Photograph a subject through each one.
3. Light comparison: Photograph the same subject in four different lights. Label each photo with the light condition and why
it looks the way it does.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a printed sheet with the four techniques illustrated with simple diagrams. Pair the learner with
a confident learner for the practical.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full lecture, demonstration, guided practical and assignment.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to combine two techniques in one photo (e.g., rule of thirds + leading lines, or framing
+ symmetry) and explain why the combination works. They should also research and present one additional composition
technique not covered in the session (e.g., negative space, filling the frame, or the golden ratio).
Timing guidance. The priority is learners taking photos with good composition and good light (Sections H and the assignment).
If the room is slow, shorten the lecture's composition techniques to the key demonstrations (rule of thirds and framing) and
protect the practical time. A learner who leaves without taking a four-photo composition series and a light study has no
material for the Week 1 mini-project.
