## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Strategy

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with social media management)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Everyday smartphone use — scrolling Instagram, Facebook, TikTok or X, sending WhatsApp messages, taking photos. No marketing knowledge, no business experience and no existing followers required. Learners who are not yet comfortable installing an app should be paired with a confident learner for the first practical.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. There is no previous session. It connects to everyday life: nearly every learner already uses social media for fun — today we turn that familiarity into a paid, professional skill.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the five fictional "who hires a manager" scenario cards (Section D) printed or ready on screen; the services-menu worksheet (Section H) printed per learner; a whiteboard or flipchart for the live services-menu build; the running client brief for Taste of Rivers Kitchen (below) printed for every learner; the ethics statement (Section F.6) read aloud and displayed; academy Wi-Fi confirmed working. The running client for the whole course. Every session uses one fictional client so learners build real, connected work instead of random exercises. Read this aloud in Session 1 and keep it visible all course. CLIENT BRIEF — TASTE OF RIVERS KITCHEN (fictional). Taste of Rivers Kitchen is a small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt, Rivers State. It sells local meals — jollof rice, fried rice, small chops, plantain and fish — for pickup and delivery within Port Harcourt. Prices run from ₦1,500 for a plate of jollof to ₦8,000 for a small-chops tray. The owner, "Chef Nkechi," cooks with two assistants and takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Her Instagram and Facebook pages exist but have been silent for about three months. She wants someone to post consistently for 30 days, reply to customers, and bring more WhatsApp orders — with no advertising budget. She is busy, not technical, and she has been burned before by someone who bought her fake followers. This client is invented for the course. No real business is contacted. Instructor note on tone. This session must feel possible, not overwhelming. Many learners believe social media management is "for people with big followings" or "for marketing graduates." Your job in the first 105 minutes is to prove, with naira figures and Nigerian examples, that this is a phone-based skill they can start this month — and that honesty is the whole business.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define social media management in their own words and explain the five core tasks of the job — planning, creating,
posting, engaging and reporting.
2. List the services a social media manager sells and match each service to a realistic naira price for a small Port Harcourt
client.
3. Describe what a client expects from a manager — consistency, prompt replies, professionalism, confidentiality and honest
numbers — and explain why each expectation exists.
4. Survey the five main platforms (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, X, LinkedIn) and state, in plain language, what each is mainly
used for and who uses it in Nigeria.
5. Explain honest earning expectations for an entry-level manager in Nigeria, including what the course does and does not
promise.
6. Apply the course ethics rules — no bought followers, no fake reviews, no inflated reports, respect privacy and consent,
follow platform rules — and explain why each rule protects the client and the manager.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show five short scenario cards, one at a time. Each describes a real Nigerian situation where
someone needs help with social media. Do not explain which is "right" — just read each and ask a show of hands.
Card 1 — The silent page. "Chinedu runs a barbershop in Rumuokuta. His Instagram page has 400 followers and his last post
was four months ago. He is good with customers but he does not know what to post or when."
Card 2 — The burned owner. "Mama Put sells rice and beans in Diobu. Someone offered to 'grow her page' for ₦15,000. She
paid. She got 2,000 followers in one week — and zero new customers. Now she does not trust anyone."
Card 3 — The busy professional. "Dr. Ada sees patients all week. She wants to share health tips and grow her clinic, but she
has no time to post or reply to messages."
Card 4 — The event that flopped. "A friend is planning a birthday party for 200 people. She made a flyer, posted it once, and
30 people came. She does not know what went wrong."
Card 5 — The side hustle. "Emeka is a corps member. He is good on his phone. He wants to earn extra naira during service
year, but he does not know what people actually pay for."
What the instructor says:
"Hands up if you can see yourself in at least one of these five cards. … Almost everyone. Good. Here is the secret: every
one of these five people has the same problem — not a phone problem, not a talent problem. They have a management
problem. They need someone to plan what to post, post it consistently, reply to people, and show the owner whether it is
working. That someone could be you. By the end of today, you will know exactly what that job is, what it pays, and why
doing it honestly is the whole advantage."
Expected learner response: Learners will nod, point at cards, say "that is my aunty," "that is my church," "I am Emeka." Accept
all answers. Draw out the common thread: someone has to do the work, and someone will pay for it.
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"This course does not promise you followers, income or clients. It teaches you a real skill with free tools. Money comes after
you do the work, build proof and deliver for real people. We say that in Session 1 and we will say it again in Session 9. Now
— what exactly is the job?"
Why this opener works: It uses situations every Nigerian learner recognises, it produces instant identification ("that is my
aunty"), and it frames the entire course as a paid, professional skill — not a hobby — within the first six minutes.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. Keep it moving — this is a confidence check, not a test.
Question 1: "Which social media apps do you already use, and what do you mainly use them for?"
Expected answer: Instagram for photos and reels, Facebook for family and groups, TikTok for videos and comedy, X for
news and arguments, LinkedIn for jobs. Accept anything honest.
Common gap: some learners use only one app. Say: "Good — you already understand one platform deeply. Today we add
the other four to your map."
Question 2: "Have you ever followed a business page — a restaurant, a salon, a phone seller? What made you follow it, or what
made you unfollow it?"
Expected answer: followed because the food looked good or they replied fast; unfollowed because they posted once in six
months or never replied to comments.
Common gap: learners think "posting" is the whole job. Clarify: "You just described the two things clients complain about
most — inconsistency and silence. Remember those words."
Question 3: "If a customer sent your shop a WhatsApp message at 9 p.m. and you replied at 9 a.m. the next day, what would the
customer think?"
Expected answer: they would think you do not care, or they would have already gone somewhere else.
Common gap: learners do not yet see response time as a professional standard. Say: "That — right there — is a service a
manager sells. It is called community management, and we teach it properly in Session 5."
Question 4: "What is the difference between a personal account and a business account on Instagram or Facebook?"
Expected answer: a business account shows a category, a contact button, and insights (numbers about who saw your post).
A personal account does not.
Common gap: many learners have never switched. Say: "We will do that together in Session 6. For now, just know: business
accounts are free and they give you the numbers you need to prove your work."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already know more than you think. You know what makes you follow a page, you know what makes you leave, and
you know that slow replies feel bad. A social media manager is simply the person a business hires to do all of that —
professionally, consistently, and with honest numbers. Let us now define the job properly."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 3 extra minutes asking learners to open one app they have
never used and find one business page — just to see what a business page looks like.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What social media management actually is
The idea. Social media management is the job of running a business's or person's social media presence — planning what to
post, creating the content, posting it on time, replying to the community, and reporting the results — so the owner can focus
on their real work.
Plain-English definition. A social media manager is the person a business hires to be its voice and its eyes online. The owner
makes the food; the manager makes sure people see the food, feel hungry, and know how to order.
Why it exists. Across Nigeria, customers now check Instagram or Facebook before they buy or visit. A 2024 survey by a Lagos
market-research firm found that 6 out of 10 Nigerian consumers under 35 look at a business's social page before deciding to
buy — and most small businesses post only when they remember, because nobody taught them how to keep it up and they
cannot afford an agency. That gap is the job.
The five core tasks of the job. Every social media management job in Nigeria — from a church volunteer to a Lagos agency
— is built from these five tasks:

Real-life comparison. Think of a restaurant. The owner is the chef — they cook. The manager is the person who designs the
menu, trains the waiters, greets customers, handles complaints, and counts the money at night. The chef cannot do all of that
and cook well. A social media manager is that person for the online restaurant.
Important limitation / misconception. Social media management is not "posting and hoping." It is not buying followers and
calling it growth. It is not making a business go viral once and disappearing. It is a management job — planning, doing,
measuring, improving — repeated every week. The managers who earn well are the ones who are consistent and honest, not
the ones who chase tricks.
F.2 Who hires a social media manager — and why
The idea. The manager's client is usually someone who is good at their real work but too busy, too technical or too inconsistent
to run their own social media well.
The five main client types in Nigeria:
1. Small-business owners — restaurant owners, tailors, salon and barber shops, bakeshops, phone sellers, fashion designers,
event vendors. They need orders and they need their page to look alive. They are the biggest market for an entry-level
manager.
2. Professionals — doctors, lawyers, real estate agents, fitness coaches, faith leaders. They need trust and visibility, not
necessarily daily posting.
3. Churches, schools and NGOs — they need announcements, event coverage and a consistent, respectful voice.
4. Event planners and entertainers — comedians, musicians, MCs. They need hype before an event and proof of reach
after.
5. Personal brands — students, corps members, creators who want to grow honestly and maybe monetise later.
Why they hire instead of doing it themselves. Three reasons, every time:
Time. The owner is busy doing the real job. A manager costs less than the owner's time.
Skill. Good captions, clean graphics and consistent scheduling are learnable skills — most owners never learned them.
Honesty about results. A good manager shows real numbers. Most owners cannot tell if their page is working — a
manager can.
What the client expects — the five expectations. This is the heart of the job. A client hires a manager and expects five
things:
Expectation What it means Why the client cares
Consistency Posting regularly — not three posts in one
day and nothing for three weeks.
A silent page looks closed. Consistency keeps the
business visible and trusted.
Prompt replies Answering comments and DMs quickly —
same day, ideally within a few hours.
A slow reply feels like no reply. Customers go to the

Say this to the class:
"A client is not buying posts. They are buying trust. They trust you with their account, their customers and their name. Every
one of those five expectations is about keeping that trust. The moment you break it — a missed week, a rude reply, a fake
number — you are not just a bad employee. You are a person who cannot be trusted with a business."

The key lesson — choose with reasons, not vibes. A manager should never say "let us be on TikTok because it is trendy."

F.5 Honest earning expectations — what this course does and does not promise
The idea. Learners need a clear, honest picture of money — not hype, not fear. This course teaches a real skill; the learner must
still do the work to earn.
What the course promises:
A real, in-demand skill that small businesses and organisations in Nigeria pay for.
Free tools only — no subscription, no ad budget, no paid software needed to learn or to start.
A complete client-ready package (the capstone) that proves the skill.
An honest certificate that means something because it is backed by real work.
What the course does not promise:
Followers, income or clients on graduation.
That a certificate alone gets you hired — proof and delivery get you hired.
That you will earn like a senior agency manager — you are entry-level, and that is fine.
Any guarantee of viral content or instant results.
Realistic earning picture for an entry-level manager in Nigeria (2026):
One small client on a Starter package: ₦15,000 – ₦25,000 per month.
Two to three small clients on Growth packages: ₦60,000 – ₦120,000 per month — a realistic first-year goal for a
dedicated beginner.
A full-time junior role in a company or agency: ₦80,000 – ₦150,000 per month, depending on the city and the
employer.
A senior agency or media-buyer role: much more — but that requires years of experience and further study, and this
course is the first step, not the last.
Say this to the class:
"Let me be honest with you, because a client will be honest with you too. After three weeks, you will not be a senior
marketer. You will be a capable entry-level manager — someone who can take on one or two small clients and deliver
honest, professional work. That is a real skill. That is worth real money. And if you keep learning — our Content Creation
course, then digital marketing, then paid advertising — you can build a serious career. But it starts here, with doing the
basics well."
F.6 The ethics of real growth — the non-negotiable rules
The idea. A client hands over their brand, their account access and their reputation. Trust is the whole job. These rules are not
suggestions — they are the difference between a professional and a cheat.
The eight ethics rules (read aloud, display, and return to them every session):
1. No fake growth. Never buy followers, likes, views, comments or reviews. Never use engagement pods, bots or fake
giveaways. Real growth is slower and worth more.
2. Honest reporting. Report real numbers, good and bad. Never hide a bad month, never round a 3% engagement rate up to
30%, never screenshot someone else's results and call them yours.
3. Privacy and consent. Get permission before posting a person's face, a customer's message or a child's photo. Never publish
a customer's contact or order details. Handle client access confidentially.
4. Follow platform rules. Post only original or properly licensed content. Never repost or rebrand another creator's work
without credit and permission.
5. No impersonation or deception. Never create fake accounts to praise or defend a client, pose as a customer, or attack a
competitor.
6. Professional disagreement. Reply calmly. Never argue publicly, insult or threaten a commenter, or rally others to attack
someone.
7. Clear money. Quote honestly, deliver what was agreed, do not take on a client you cannot serve.
8. Consequences. Breaking these rules is a serious breach of academy conduct and may end participation.
Why each rule protects the manager too. Bought followers inflate the client's expectations and make real results look small by
comparison — the manager looks bad. Fake reviews get the client's page flagged or banned — the manager loses the
account. Inflated reports get caught the moment the client checks their own insights — the manager is fired and named.
Honest work is not just right; it is the only sustainable business model.
Say it with the class (repeat together):
"No fakes. No inflated numbers. Real work, real results, real proof."
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor. A doctor who lies about a patient's results might make the patient happy for a week
— but when the truth comes out, the doctor loses their licence and their reputation. A social media manager is the same. The
client's trust is the licence.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Social media management is the job of planning, creating, posting, engaging and reporting for a business's social media
— so the owner can focus on their real work.
The five core tasks are planning, creating, posting, engaging and reporting.
The main clients are small businesses, professionals, churches and schools, event vendors and personal brands.
The five client expectations are consistency, prompt replies, professionalism, confidentiality and honest numbers.
The services a manager sells are content creation, scheduling, community management, reporting, basic paid promotion
and strategy — usually sold as packages.
Realistic entry-level pricing for a small Port Harcourt client runs from ₦15,000 to ₦100,000 per month depending on the
package.
The five platforms each have a main use and a main audience — choose with reasons, not vibes.
Honest earning is real but not instant — one or two small clients is a realistic first goal.
The ethics rules are non-negotiable: no fakes, no inflated numbers, real work, real results, real proof.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Walk the class through a complete "day in the life" of a social media manager for the running client, Taste of Rivers
Kitchen, so learners see the five tasks as one connected job — not five separate ideas.
Setup: On the projector, show a blank "Day in the Life" timeline with five time blocks: Morning, Midday, Afternoon, Evening,
Night. Have the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief visible.
G.1 Demonstration 1 — The morning: plan and create (4 minutes)
Say: "It is 8 a.m. Chef Nkechi is in the kitchen. I am the manager. What do I do first?"
Walk through the morning block:
Check the calendar. "I open my content calendar and see: today is Friday, and the plan says 'jollof video, 5 p.m., Instagram
and Facebook.' I know exactly what to post — I am not guessing."
Create the content. "Yesterday I filmed Chef Nkechi frying jollof — 30 seconds, phone, natural light. This morning I write
the caption: hook, value, CTA. I design one graphic in Canva with the price — ₦1,500 a plate — and the WhatsApp order
number. That is the creating task."
Prepare the post. "I save the video and the graphic to a folder called 'Taste of Rivers — Friday.' I write the caption in a
Google Doc so Chef Nkechi can read it before I post."
Point out: "Notice — I have not posted anything yet. A good manager does not post in the morning and hope. I plan, I create, I
prepare. Posting is the last step, not the first."
G.2 Demonstration 2 — Midday: the approval step (3 minutes)
Say: "Before anything goes live, the client sees it. This is called the approval workflow, and it is what separates a professional
from a poster."
Walk through the midday block:
Send for approval. "I send Chef Nkechi the caption and the graphic on WhatsApp at 12 p.m. I say: 'This is Friday's post.
Please check the price and the wording. I will post at 5 p.m. unless you tell me to change something.'"
Handle feedback. "She replies: 'Change ₦1,500 to ₦1,600 — I changed the price this week.' I update the graphic and the
caption. I confirm: 'Updated. Posting at 5 p.m.'"
Why this matters. "If I had posted at 8 a.m. without checking, the page would show the wrong price for a whole day. The
client would lose trust. The approval step takes ten minutes and prevents that."
G.3 Demonstration 3 — Afternoon: post and engage (4 minutes)
Say: "It is 5 p.m. The post is approved. Now I do the posting and engaging tasks."
Walk through the afternoon block:
Post. "I open Meta Business Suite, upload the video, paste the caption, and schedule it for 5 p.m. on both Instagram and
Facebook. It goes live automatically — I do not have to be online at 5 p.m."
Engage. "By 6 p.m., the comments start. Someone writes: 'Do you deliver to GRA?' I reply: 'Yes! We deliver to GRA in 45
minutes. Send us a DM to order.' Someone else writes: 'This jollof looks fire.' I reply: 'Thank you! Come and taste it —
₦1,500 a plate.'"
The engagement rule. "I reply to every real comment within a few hours. I never argue, I never ignore a question, and I
never post a customer's private details. That is community management — and it is a service the client is paying for."
G.4 Demonstration 4 — Evening and night: measure and report (4 minutes)
Say: "The week ends. Now I do the reporting task — the one that makes the client trust me."
Walk through the evening and night blocks:
Pull the numbers. "On Sunday night, I open Instagram and Facebook Insights. I write down: how many people saw our
posts (reach), how many liked, commented, saved or shared (engagement), how many clicked the WhatsApp link (clicks),
and how many new followers we gained."
Write the report. "I fill in a one-page report: numbers against our goal, the top post, what worked, what did not, and one
recommendation for next month. I send it to Chef Nkechi with screenshots."
The honest rule. "If the month was bad, I say so. 'We reached 5,000 people but only got 8 orders. The jollof video worked;
the plantain graphic did not. Next month we post more jollof content and try a different graphic style.' That honesty is why
she keeps me."
Questions to ask during the demo:
"At what point did I post? Was it the first thing I did?" (No — planning and creating came first.)
"Why did I send the caption to Chef Nkechi before posting?" (Approval — to catch mistakes and build trust.)
"What did I do at 6 p.m. when the comments came?" (Engaged — replied quickly and warmly.)
"What did I do on Sunday night?" (Reported — real numbers, honest insights.)
Expected result: Learners see the five tasks as one connected day — plan, create, approve, post, engage, report — and they
see that the job is mostly before and after the post, not the post itself.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Blessing's Bakeshop is a small bakery in Rumuokwuta, Port Harcourt. It makes small chops, meat pies, cakes and
doughnuts. The owner, Blessing, is an excellent baker but she is not online. Her Instagram page has 350 followers — mostly
friends — and her last post was two months ago. She gets orders by word of mouth and a handwritten note at her gate. A
friend told her she needs "social media," so she paid a young man ₦10,000 to "handle her page." He posted three times in one
week, then disappeared. He also bought her 1,000 followers. The page now has 1,350 followers, still no orders, and Blessing feels
cheated.
Relevant facts.
Blessing's products are genuinely good — her small chops sell out every Saturday.
The 1,000 followers bought for her are fake — they never like, comment or order.
The young man posted three times in one week and nothing for the next two months.
Blessing has no content calendar, no caption plan and no way to know if social media is working.
She is willing to pay ₦25,000 a month for someone she can trust.
Decision point. What should Blessing do now — and what should a honest manager do differently from the young man?
Possible approaches.
1. Abandon social media entirely — "it does not work, I tried it."
2. Hire another cheap person and hope for better results.
3. Hire a professional manager who plans, creates, posts consistently, engages and reports honestly — and who refuses to
buy followers.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — but with a specific, honest plan. A good manager would:
Start with a strategy session: who is Blessing's customer (students, families, event planners), which platform (Instagram,
because the food is visual), and what is the goal (more WhatsApp orders).
Set a realistic cadence: 3 posts a week — a small-chops photo, a behind-the-scenes video, a customer testimonial — not
three posts in one week and silence.
Create real content: phone photos of the small chops, a short video of the doughnut glaze, captions with prices and the
WhatsApp order number.
Engage daily: reply to every comment and DM within a few hours.
Report monthly: real numbers — reach, engagement, DMs, orders — with honest insights.
Refuse fake growth: explain clearly that the 1,000 fake followers are worthless and that real growth, though slower, is what
actually brings orders.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is safe but leaves Blessing invisible — her competitors are online and she is not.
Approach 2 is cheap but likely to repeat the same failure — no plan, no consistency, no honesty.
Approach 3 costs more than ₦10,000, but it is the only approach that builds something real: a page that looks alive, a
community that trusts her, and a manager who can prove the results.
Professional connection. This is exactly the situation a new manager will face — a client who has been burned and is
suspicious. The manager's first job is not to post; it is to rebuild trust. That means honest pricing, a clear plan, consistent
delivery and real numbers. A manager who does that will never lack work, because a satisfied small-business owner tells her
friends.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Thinking "posting" is the whole job
Symptoms: Learner believes a manager's job is to post content and nothing else. Likely cause: They have only ever been a
social media user, not a manager — they see posts, not the work behind them. How to investigate: Ask: "What happens
before a post goes live? What happens after?" Corrective steps: Walk through the five tasks again — planning, creating,
posting, engaging, reporting. Emphasise that posting is one of five. How to confirm resolved: Learner can list all five tasks and
give an example of each. Prevention: Use the "Day in the Life" demonstration in every session.
K.2 Believing bought followers are a shortcut
Symptoms: Learner asks "but what if we just buy a few followers to start?" Likely cause: They have seen pages with big
followings and assumed the following was bought — and that it worked. How to investigate: Ask: "If you buy 1,000 followers,
how many of them will ever buy from the client?" (None — they are fake.) Corrective steps: Explain that fake followers inflate
the client's expectations, make real results look small, and get pages flagged. Real growth is slower and worth more. How to
confirm resolved: Learner can explain why bought followers hurt the client. Prevention: Repeat the ethics line every session:
No fakes. No inflated numbers. Real work, real results, real proof.
K.3 Quoting a price without knowing what it costs to deliver
Symptoms: Learner quotes ₦100,000 for a Full Management package they have never delivered. Likely cause: They want to
earn big quickly and have not thought about the hours involved. How to investigate: Ask: "How many posts is that? How
many hours will that take you? What is your hourly rate?" Corrective steps: Walk through the pricing table — Starter, Growth,
Full Management — and the realistic naira ranges. Emphasise: start at the lower end, deliver well, raise prices as you build
proof. How to confirm resolved: Learner can justify a price with the work involved. Prevention: Discuss pricing honestly in
every session; never let a learner quote without a reason.
K.4 Promising results the manager cannot guarantee
Symptoms: Learner says "I will get you 10,000 followers in one month" or "I will make your post go viral." Likely cause: They
want to win the client and do not know what is realistic. How to investigate: Ask: "Can you control the algorithm? Can you
guarantee a viral post?" (No.) Corrective steps: Teach honest promises: "I will post consistently, engage daily, and report real
numbers. I cannot guarantee followers or virality — no honest manager can." How to confirm resolved: Learner can state an
honest promise and an honest limit. Prevention: Repeat the honest-promise line in Session 1 and Session 9.
K.5 Ignoring the client's real business
Symptoms: Learner designs a beautiful page that brings no orders. Likely cause: They focused on looks, not on the client's goal
— which is usually orders, bookings or calls. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the client's business goal? Does this post move
toward it?" Corrective steps: Connect every post to a goal. A jollof video is not just a video — it is a tool to get WhatsApp
orders. How to confirm resolved: Learner can state the client's goal and explain how a post serves it. Prevention: Always ask
"what is the goal?" before "what should we post?"
K.6 Treating all platforms the same
Symptoms: Learner wants to be on all five platforms at once for every client. Likely cause: They think more platforms = more
results. How to investigate: Ask: "Who is the client's customer? Where do they spend time?" Corrective steps: Teach platform
choice by audience — one or two platforms with reasons, not five by default. How to confirm resolved: Learner can choose
one or two platforms for a client and explain why. Prevention: The platform survey (Section F.4) and Session 2's strategy work.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

Plain-English summary. Today you learned what social media management really is. It is not "posting and hoping" — it is a
management job built on five tasks: planning, creating, posting, engaging and reporting. You learned who hires a manager
(small businesses, professionals, churches, event vendors, personal brands), what clients expect (consistency, prompt replies,
professionalism, confidentiality and honest numbers), and what services a manager sells (content creation, scheduling,
community management, reporting, basic paid promotion and strategy). You surveyed the five platforms and learned to choose
them with reasons, not vibes. You learned realistic naira pricing for a small Port Harcourt client, and you learned the honest
earning picture — one or two small clients is a realistic first goal. Finally, you met the ethics rules that make the job
trustworthy: no fakes, no inflated numbers, real work, real results, real proof.
Five important takeaways:
1. Social media management = planning, creating, posting, engaging and reporting — five tasks, one job.
2. Clients buy trust — consistency, prompt replies, professionalism, confidentiality and honest numbers.
3. Services are sold as packages — Starter, Growth and Full Management — with realistic naira prices.
4. Choose platforms by audience, not vibes — one or two platforms with written reasons.
5. Honesty is the business model — no bought followers, no fake reviews, no inflated reports.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I explain what a social media manager does, in my own words?
Can I list the five core tasks and give an example of each?
Can I name three services a manager sells and a realistic price for each?
Can I explain why a client cares about consistency and prompt replies?
Can I state two ethics rules and explain why they protect the client?
Connection to the next session. You now know what the job is. But a manager who does not know who they are talking to is
just posting into the dark. Next session we learn to think before posting: how to research an audience with free tools, set
measurable goals, choose one or two platforms with written reasons, scan competitors, and write a positioning statement. You
will build a complete strategy sheet for our running client, Taste of Rivers Kitchen — and that sheet becomes part of your
capstone.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner defines social media management using the five tasks — not just "posting."
Learner lists realistic services and prices without being prompted.
Learner explains why a client cares about consistency and prompt replies.
Learner refuses fake growth automatically and gives a reason.
Learner connects the platform choice to the audience, not to fashion.
Learner states honest earning expectations — no promises of followers or income.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Posting is the job." → No — posting is one of five tasks; the work is mostly before and after.
"Bought followers are a shortcut." → They bring no customers and destroy trust.
"More platforms = more results." → Choose one or two with reasons.
"A certificate guarantees clients." → Proof and delivery get you hired; the certificate confirms the skill.
"I can promise followers to win a client." → No honest manager can; the promise will fail and end the relationship.
"Social media management is for people with big followings." → It is a phone-based skill anyone can learn.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Five-task matching drill: give five scenarios (a silent page, a rude reply, a wrong price, a bad month, a new client) and ask
the learner to name which task each one belongs to.
2. Pricing justification drill: give a package price and ask the learner to list the work that justifies it — posts per week, hours,
services.
3. Ethics scenario drill: give three situations (a client asks for fake followers, a client asks you to hide a bad month, a
competitor's post goes viral) and ask the learner what they would do and why.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed services menu — package names and one service each — and ask the
learner to add the remaining services, frequencies and prices.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written — three full packages for a new client.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a short "honest pitch" to a skeptical client who has been burned before —
explaining, in plain language, what the manager will do, what they will not do, and why the client should trust them. The
pitch must include one ethics rule and one realistic price.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners understanding the role and the ethics (Sections F.1–F.6). If the room is slow, shorten
the platform survey (F.4) to a quick overview and protect the guided practical (Section H) — the ability to map the five tasks to
a real day is the core skill. If the room is fast, extend the case study discussion and let learners present their services menus to the
class.
