# Creating and Sourcing Content for Clients

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Create and Manage

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first hands-on content creation)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, build a strategy (audience, goals, platforms, pillars, cadence and calendar) and explain the ethics rules. Basic smartphone photography assumed. No design or video editing experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 3 we built a two-week content calendar with pillars, formats and caption ideas. Today we fill those calendar rows with real content. We write a creative brief, repurpose one idea into four post formats, design a clean Canva graphic, and learn to source photos and video honestly — own shots, client photos with permission, and free stock within licence.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the free Canva account ready on academy computers; the creative brief template (Section F.2) printed per learner; the repurposing worksheet printed; the Canva brand-kit example ready on screen; sample phone photos of food (prepared by the instructor — no copyrighted images); the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief and the Session 3 calendar available; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This is the session where learners make real things. Many will say "I am not creative" — your job is to show them that content creation is a process, not a gift. A creative brief, a phone camera, Canva and a clear goal are all they need. Keep the mood hands-on and encouraging. Walk the room constantly during the practical — this is where learners need you most.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Write a creative brief that turns a calendar row into a clear content instruction.
2. Repurpose one idea into four post formats — a Reel, a carousel, a story and a caption set.
3. Design one readable, on-brand Canva post at the correct size with consistent colours, fonts and a logo.
4. Source photos and video honestly — own shots, client photos with permission, and free stock within licence.
5. Explain how to work with editors and photographers — brief, references, feedback, deadlines, payment and credit.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two versions of the same Instagram post for the running client, Taste of Rivers Kitchen.
Both promote the same thing — jollof rice, ₦1,500 a plate. Do not explain which is "better" yet.
Post A — "No brief." A blurry photo of jollof, taken in dim light, with a hand partially covering the frame. The caption says:
"Our jollof is available. Order now." No price visible in the image. No WhatsApp number. The hashtags are #food #jollof #rice
#yummy #instagood #photooftheday #love.
Post B — "With a brief." A clear, well-lit photo of jollof in a clean bowl, with a simple Canva price graphic — "Jollof Rice —
₦1,500" — and the WhatsApp order number. The caption reads: "Smoky jollof, cooked fresh every morning ðŸ ² ₦1,500 a
plate. Free delivery in Port Harcourt. ðŸ‘‰ DM 'JOLLOF' or call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order. #TasteOfRiversKitchen
#PortHarcourtFood #PHJollof"
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two posts. Same business, same dish, same price. Post A was made without a plan — a quick snap, a
vague caption, random hashtags. Post B was made with a plan — a clear photo, a designed price graphic, a caption with
a hook, a value and a CTA. Which post would you order from? … Post B, obviously. Now here is the question: was Post B
made by someone more talented than the person who made Post A? No. It was made by someone who followed a process.
Today you learn that process. It is called a creative brief, and it turns 'post something about jollof' into a clear instruction
that anyone can follow."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at Post B and say "it looks professional," "it has the price," "it looks clean."
Accept all answers. Draw out the difference: process vs. no process.
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"Post B was not luck. Before anyone picked up a phone or opened Canva, someone wrote down five things: the goal of the
post, the audience, the message, the format and the CTA. That is a creative brief — and it takes ten minutes. Today you
will write one, then use it to create four posts from a single idea. That is the skill: one idea, many posts, all planned."
Why this opener works: It uses a side-by-side comparison that makes the abstract idea of "a creative brief" concrete. Learners
see immediately that planning beats luck — and they want to know the process.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core tasks of a social media manager?"
Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners say "posting" is the main task. Clarify: "Today we go deeper into the creating task."
Question 2: "What is a content pillar?"
Expected answer: A main topic a business posts about again and again — connected to the business, the audience and a
goal.
Common gap: learners say "a type of post." Clarify: "Close — but a pillar is a topic, not a format. 'Menu & prices' is a pillar.
'Image' is a format."
Question 3: "What is a content calendar?"
Expected answer: A plan that says what to post, when, on which platform, in which format, with which caption idea.
Common gap: learners say "a list of posts." Clarify: "It is more than a list — every row has a pillar, a format, a caption idea
and an asset."
Question 4: "What are the three parts of a caption?"
Expected answer: Hook, value, CTA — plus relevant hashtags.
Common gap: learners say "words and hashtags." Clarify: "The three parts are hook, value and CTA. Hashtags help
discovery — they are important, but they are not one of the three parts."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 3 we planned the content — pillars, cadence and a calendar. Today we make it. We will write a creative brief,
repurpose one idea into four post formats, design a clean Canva graphic, and learn to source photos and video honestly.
By the end of this session, you will have four finished posts from a single idea — and that is the skill that makes a
manager valuable."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 2, spend 3 extra minutes reviewing pillars with a quick example before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 From calendar row to creative brief
The idea. A calendar row says "Menu & prices / Image / 'This week's menu — jollof ₦1,500.'" That is a plan — but it is not yet
an instruction anyone can execute. A creative brief turns that row into a clear, step-by-step instruction.
Plain-English definition. A creative short document that says exactly what a post should achieve, who it is for, what it should
say, what it should look like and what action it should drive.
Why it exists. Without a brief, content is guesswork. The creator does not know the goal, the audience or the CTA — so they
make something that looks nice but does not work. With a brief, every decision is already made: the goal, the message, the
format, the look and the action. The creator just executes.
What a creative brief contains — the six parts:
What it says Example (Taste of Rivers Kitchen)
1. Goal
What the post should achieve. Get WhatsApp orders for jollof.
2. Audience
Who the post is talking to. Students and young professionals in Rumuola and GRA.
3. Message
The one thing the audience should
know.
Jollof is ₦1,500 a plate, cooked fresh every morning, delivered free in
Port Harcourt.
What it says Example (Taste of Rivers Kitchen)
4. Format
What the post looks like. A single image with a price graphic.
5. Look
The visual style — colours, fonts,
brand.
Warm colours (orange and brown), the brand font, the Taste of Rivers
logo.
6. CTA
What the audience should do. DM 'JOLLOF' or call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order.
Worked example — a complete creative brief for one post:
Creative Brief — Taste of Rivers Kitchen, Monday Menu Post
1. Goal: Get 10 WhatsApp orders for jollof this week.
2. Audience: Students and young professionals in Rumuola and GRA who want a fast, affordable lunch.
3. Message: Jollof is ₦1,500 a plate, cooked fresh every morning, delivered free in Port Harcourt.
4. Format: One Instagram image (1080 x 1080 px) with a price graphic.
5. Look: Warm orange and brown, the brand font (Montserrat), the Taste of Rivers logo in the top corner.
6. CTA: DM 'JOLLOF' or call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order.
The key lesson — a brief takes ten minutes and prevents ten mistakes. Without it, the creator might photograph the jollof
in dim light, forget the price, use the wrong colours and end the caption with "hope you like it." With it, every decision is already
made.
Real-life comparison. Think of a building plan. A builder who starts without a plan will build a wall in the wrong place, run out
of blocks and argue with the owner about the roof. A builder with a plan knows exactly how many blocks, where the walls go
and what the roof looks like. A creative brief is the content creator's building plan.
F.2 Repurposing — one idea, four posts
The idea. Repurposing is taking one piece of content — one idea, one photo shoot, one video — and turning it into several
posts for different formats and platforms. It is how a small business creates a week of content from one hour of work.
Plain-English definition. Repurposing is using one idea to make multiple posts, each adapted to a different format or platform,
instead of creating from scratch every time.
Why it exists. A small business cannot shoot and edit new content every day. Repurposing multiplies the value of one idea,
saves time and keeps the page consistent. It is the difference between a page that posts three times a week from one shoot, and
a page that needs three shoots to post three times.
The four repurposing formats — one idea, four posts:
Take one idea: "Jollof is ₦1,500 a plate, cooked fresh every morning."
Format What it is How the idea becomes this post
Reel A 15–30 second video.
Film the jollof being cooked — steam rising, being plated. Add on-screen text:
"Fresh jollof — ₦1,500." Add a CTA: "DM 'JOLLOF' to order."
Carousel A multi-image post
(3–5 slides).
Slide 1: "Our jollof — ₦1,500." Slide 2: "Cooked fresh every morning." Slide 3:
"Free delivery in Port Harcourt." Slide 4: "DM 'JOLLOF' to order."
Story A disappearing photo
or video.
A photo of the jollof with a "Order now" sticker and the WhatsApp link. Runs for
24 hours — creates urgency.
Caption set A caption written for
each platform.
Instagram: "Smoky jollof, cooked fresh every morning ðŸ ² ₦1,500 a plate..."
WhatsApp Status: "Fresh jollof — ₦1,500. Free delivery in PH. Call 0803-XXX-XXXX."
Worked example — one photo shoot, four posts:
Chef Nkechi plates a bowl of jollof. The manager:
1. Takes 5 photos of the jollof from different angles.
2. Films a 20-second video of the jollof being plated.
3. Uses one photo for the Instagram image post (with a price graphic).
4. Uses three photos for the carousel (menu, cooking, delivery).
5. Uses the video for the Reel.
6. Uses one photo for the Story (with an "Order now" sticker).
7. Writes a caption set — one for Instagram, one for WhatsApp Status.
One hour of work. Four posts. That is repurposing.
The key lesson — repurpose the idea, not the copy-paste. Repurposing does not mean posting the identical caption
everywhere. It means adapting the message to each format and platform. The Instagram caption can be longer; the WhatsApp
Status must be shorter. The Reel needs on-screen text; the image needs a price graphic. Same idea, different clothes.
Common misconception. "Repurposing is lazy." No — it is efficient. Creating four separate ideas for four posts is what is
exhausting. Repurposing is how professionals keep a page consistent without burning out.
F.3 Designing a clean Canva post
The idea. Canva is a free design tool that runs in a browser or on a phone. It is how a manager without design training creates
clean, on-brand graphics for a client.
Plain-English definition. Canva gives you templates, colours, fonts, images and simple editing tools — so anyone can make a
post that looks professional.
Why it exists. A small business cannot afford a designer for every post. Canva's free plan is enough to create a consistent,
readable brand look — and it is the tool this course uses from today onward.
The five rules of a clean Canva post:
1. Correct size. Instagram image: 1080 x 1080 px (square). Instagram Story: 1080 x 1920 px (vertical). Reel cover: 1080 x 1920 px.
Use the right size for the format.
2. Readable text. Big enough to read on a phone. A good rule: if you have to squint, it is too small. Use 2–3 colours
maximum for text.
3. Consistent brand. Use the same 2–3 colours, the same 2 fonts and the same logo on every post. Consistency is what
makes a page look professional.
4. One message per post. One price, one offer, one CTA. Do not cram five things into one graphic.
5. Clean background. A simple photo or a solid colour. Do not clutter the background — the message must stand out.
Worked example — designing the jollof price graphic in Canva:
1. Open Canva. Search "Instagram Post" — choose 1080 x 1080 px.
2. Choose a warm background — a soft orange or a photo of the jollof.
3. Add a text box: "Jollof Rice — ₦1,500." Make it big — at least 72 pt.
4. Add a second text box: "Free delivery in Port Harcourt." Smaller — 36 pt.
5. Add the Taste of Rivers logo in the top corner.
6. Add a CTA box: "DM 'JOLLOF' to order." At the bottom.
7. Save and download as a PNG.
The key lesson — a clean post is readable at phone size. Before finishing any design, zoom out and look at it on a phone. If
the price is hard to read, make it bigger. If the colours clash, simplify. If the message is unclear, cut words. A clean post is not
fancy — it is clear.
Real-life comparison. Think of a signboard. A signboard with ten colours, five fonts and three messages is ignored. A
signboard with one clear message, big readable text and the shop's colours is noticed. A Canva post is a signboard.
F.4 Sourcing content honestly — own shots, client photos, free stock
The idea. Every image and video a manager uses must be legally and ethically sourced. There are three honest sources: your
own shots, the client's photos (with permission), and free stock within licence.
Plain-English definition. Sourcing is where the content comes from. Honest sourcing means you have the right to use
everything you post.
The three honest sources:

Own shots Photos and videos you take
yourself with your phone.
The best source —
always prefer this.
You own the content. Make sure any person in
the photo has given permission for it to be
posted.

— of their products, shop or
team.
Useful when the client

What is NOT honest sourcing:
Taking images from Google — most are copyrighted. Using them without permission is theft.
Reposting another creator's content without credit and permission — this violates platform rules and copyright.
Using a customer's photo or message without asking — this violates privacy.
Editing a photo to misrepresent the product — making food look better than it is with filters that change the colour or
adding items that are not included.
Worked example — sourcing for the jollof post:
Own shots: The manager photographs the jollof with their phone. This is the main image. ✔
Client photos: Chef Nkechi sends a photo of her kitchen. The manager asks: "Can I use this on the page?" She says yes. ✔
Free stock: The manager needs a warm background texture for the Canva graphic. They use one from Canva's free library.
✔
Not okay: The manager finds a beautiful jollof photo on Google and uses it. ✘ (Copyrighted.)
The key lesson — when in doubt, shoot it yourself. The best content is the content you create from the client's real business.
It is honest, it is original, and it is what makes the page trustworthy.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who sells her own tomatoes is honest. A trader who takes tomatoes
from another stall and sells them as her own is a thief. A manager who uses their own shots is honest. A manager who steals
images from Google is a thief — and they will be caught.
F.5 Working with editors and photographers
The idea. Sometimes a client needs more than a phone photo — a professional video, a portrait, a product shoot. A manager
may hire an editor or a photographer. Doing that well is a skill.
Plain-English definition. Working with editors and photographers means giving them a clear brief, useful references, honest
feedback, a fair deadline, agreed payment and proper credit.
The six rules for working with creatives:
1. Brief. Tell them exactly what you need — the goal, the style, the format, the deadline. Use the creative brief from Section
F.2.
2. References. Show them 2–3 examples of the style you want. "Something like this, but with our colours."
3. Feedback. Be specific and kind. "The colours are too dark — can you brighten them?" not "I do not like it."
4. Deadline. Agree on a date and stick to it. Do not rush a creative with an impossible deadline.
5. Payment. Agree on a price before the work starts. Pay on time. A creative who is not paid will not work with you again.
6. Credit. If you publish their work, credit them — "Video by @name" or "Photo by @name." It is honest and it builds
relationships.
Worked example — hiring a videographer for a Reel:
Brief: "I need a 20-second vertical video of jollof being cooked and plated, for Instagram Reels. Warm colours, natural light,
the Taste of Rivers logo visible."
References: "Something like this Reel from a Lagos food page — but with our kitchen and our branding."
Feedback: "The steam shot is perfect. The plating shot is a little dark — can we brighten it?"
Deadline: "I need it by Friday evening for a Saturday post."
Payment: "₦5,000 for the edited video. I will pay 50% now, 50% on delivery."
Credit: "Video by @ChineduFilms" in the caption or on the video.
The key lesson — treat creatives as partners, not tools. A photographer or editor who is treated well will do great work for
you again. One who will not. The relationships a manager builds with creatives are part of their professional value.
F.6 Summary of the lecture
A creative brief turns a calendar row into a clear instruction — goal, audience, message, format, look and CTA.
Repurposing is using one idea to make multiple posts, each adapted to a different format or platform.
A clean Canva post is readable at phone size, uses consistent brand colours and fonts, and has one message.
Honest sourcing means own shots, client photos with permission, or free stock within licence — never copyrighted
images.
Working with editors and photographers means a clear brief, useful references, honest feedback, a fair deadline, agreed
payment and proper credit.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ada's Ankara is a small fashion business in Port Harcourt that sells ankara dresses and does custom sewing. The
owner, Ada, is a skilled tailor. She posts on Instagram — but the photos are inconsistent. Some are clear, well-lit shots of
dresses. Some are dark, blurry selfies. Some are photos she found on Google of other people's dresses. Her captions are
sometimes "New dress available" and sometimes a full description with prices. She has 1,500 followers but only gets about 5
orders a month. She is frustrated. She thinks "Instagram does not work for fashion."
Relevant facts.
Ada's dresses are genuinely beautiful — her customers love them.
Her photos are inconsistent — some professional, some not.
Some of her photos are taken from Google — they are not her dresses.
Her captions are inconsistent — sometimes no price, sometimes no CTA.
She has no creative brief, no plan and no way to know which posts bring orders.
Decision point. A social media manager has been hired. What should the manager do first — and what should they avoid?
Possible approaches.
1. Post more often — "let us post every day, that will fix it."
2. Buy followers — "let us get the page to 10,000 followers, then orders will come."
3. Build a content creation process — creative briefs, own shots, clean Canva designs, repurposing and honest sourcing.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — a content creation process. The manager should:
Write a creative brief for every post — goal, audience, message, format, look, CTA.
Take own shots of Ada's dresses — clear, well-lit photos with her phone.
Stop using Google photos — they are not her dresses, and they are copyrighted.
Design clean Canva graphics — consistent colours, fonts and logo.
Repurpose one shoot into four posts — image, carousel, Reel, story.
Adapt captions for each platform — Instagram, WhatsApp Status.
Measure results — which posts bring orders? Do more of that.
What the manager should avoid:
Posting more often without a process — seven inconsistent posts a week will not fix a process problem.
Buying followers — fake followers bring no orders.
Using copyrighted images — this is theft and it will damage Ada's page when she is caught.
Expecting instant results — a consistent process takes 30 days to show results.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is easy but ineffective — more inconsistent posting will not fix a process problem.
Approach 2 is cheap but destructive — fake followers make the page look big while bringing zero orders.
Approach 3 takes planning and discipline, but it builds a page that actually brings orders — and it gives the manager a
process they can use for every client.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional does not guess. A professional briefs, shoots, designs, repurposes and measures. That
is why a professional can charge ₦25,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Creating without a brief
Symptoms: Learner designs a post without writing a creative brief first. Likely cause: They think a brief is unnecessary — "I
know what I want to make." How to investigate: Ask: "What is the goal of this post? Who is it for? What is the CTA?"
Corrective steps: Write the brief first. Six lines. Ten minutes. Then design. How to confirm resolved: The learner writes a brief
before creating. Prevention: Always ask "what is the goal?" before "what should we make?"
K.2 Copy-pasting the same caption everywhere
Symptoms: Learner writes one caption and uses it for every format and platform. Likely cause: They think one caption fits all.
How to investigate: Ask: "Is this caption right for a Reel? For a WhatsApp Status? How is it different?" Corrective steps: Adapt
the caption for each format and platform. Instagram can be longer; WhatsApp Status must be shorter. Reels need on-screen
text; images need a price graphic. How to confirm resolved: The caption set has different versions for different platforms.
Prevention: Remind the class — repurpose the idea, not the copy-paste.
K.3 Using copyrighted images from Google
Symptoms: Learner uses a photo found on Google. Likely cause: They think "if it is on the internet, it is free to use." How to
investigate: Ask: "Did you take this photo? Do you have permission to use it?" Corrective steps: Use own shots, client photos
with permission, or free stock within licence. Never use a copyrighted image from Google. How to confirm resolved: Every
image is original, credited or properly licensed. Prevention: Remind the class — when in doubt, shoot it yourself.
K.4 Designing a cluttered post
Symptoms: Canva post has too many colours, fonts or messages. Likely cause: They think more = better. How to investigate:
Ask: "What is the one message of this post? Can you read the price on a phone?" Corrective steps: Two or three colours, one or
two fonts, one message, big readable text. How to confirm resolved: The post is readable at phone size with one clear
message. Prevention: A clean post is not fancy — it is clear.
K.5 Not repurposing
Symptoms: Learner creates one post from one idea and stops there. Likely cause: They do not yet see the value of repurposing.
How to investigate: Ask: "You have one photo and one video. How many posts can you make from them?" Corrective steps:
Plan four posts from one idea — image, carousel, Reel, story. How to confirm resolved: The repurposing worksheet has four
posts. Prevention: Remind the class — one hour of work, four posts.
K.6 Not crediting creatives
Symptoms: Learner uses an editor's or photographer's work without credit. Likely cause: They think credit is optional. How to
investigate: Ask: "Whose work is this? Did you agree on credit?" Corrective steps: Credit the creative — "Video by @name"
— unless they agreed otherwise. How to confirm resolved: Every creative's work is credited. Prevention: Credit is honest and
it builds relationships.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
