# Content Strategy and Pillars

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Strategy

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with content planning)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1 and 2 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, explain the ethics rules, research an audience, set SMART goals, choose platforms with reasons and write a positioning statement. Everyday smartphone use assumed. No prior content planning experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 2 we built the strategy — audience, goals, platforms, competitors and positioning. Today we turn that strategy into a content plan: content pillars, formats, posting cadence and a two-week content calendar. That calendar becomes the first half of the capstone's 30-day plan.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the five content pillars example (Section F.2) ready on screen; the two-week calendar template (Section H) printed per learner; the pillar-selection worksheet printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available; a whiteboard for the live calendar build; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This session is where planning becomes practical. Learners often think "content strategy" is something only agencies do — your job is to show them that it is simply deciding what to post, when to post it and why — and that a simple calendar is the tool that makes it real. Keep the mood energetic and practical. By the end of the session, every learner must have a two-week calendar in front of them.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why random posting fails and how content pillars fix it.
2. Select four to five content pillars for a client and explain how each one serves a goal.
3. Match content formats (Reel, carousel, image, story, text) to platforms and purposes.
4. Set a realistic posting cadence that one person can sustain.
5. Build a two-week content calendar with a pillar, format and caption idea for every post.

Why this opener works: It uses a visual, side-by-side comparison that makes the abstract idea of "content strategy" concrete.
Learners see immediately that a planned calendar beats random posting — and they want to know how to build one.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core tasks of a social media manager?"
Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners mix up the order. Clarify: "Today we go deeper into the planning task — specifically, planning the
content."
Question 2: "What is an audience profile, and what does it name?"
Expected answer: A profile of who the client's customers are — who they are, where they live, what they want, what stops
them and where they spend time online. It names a real obstacle.
Common gap: learners say "it is a list of followers." Clarify: "No — it is a description of the people most likely to buy, not
the people who already follow."
Question 3: "What does SMART stand for?"
Expected answer: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
Common gap: learners remember some letters but not all. Write them on the board and give a quick example of each.
Question 4: "What is a positioning statement?"
Expected answer: One sentence that says who the client serves, what they offer and why they are different.
Common gap: learners confuse it with a slogan. Clarify: "A slogan is 'Taste of Rivers — the best food in PH.' A positioning
statement is 'For students and young professionals in Port Harcourt, Taste of Rivers Kitchen is the local meals spot that
delivers fresh, hot jollof to your door in 45 minutes because we cook every morning and deliver ourselves.' One is a claim
— the other is a decision-making tool."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 2 we built the strategy — who we are talking to, what we are trying to achieve and where to post. Today we
turn that strategy into a content plan. We will choose our content pillars — the topics we post about again and again —
match formats to platforms, set a posting cadence we can actually sustain, and build a two-week content calendar. That
calendar is the first half of your capstone's 30-day plan. Let us start."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 3, spend 3 extra minutes writing SMART on the board with a one-line
example of each before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why random posting fails
The idea. Random posting is posting without a plan — whatever comes to mind, whenever you remember. It is the most
common reason small business pages fail.
Plain-English definition. Random posting is posting without a strategy — no pattern, no purpose, no connection to a goal.
Why it fails:
No focus — the page jumps from memes to food photos to news articles. Customers do not know what the business is.
No consistency — three posts in one day, silence for three weeks. The page looks abandoned.
No connection to goals — a meme might get likes, but it does not get orders. A "good morning" graphic does not build
trust.
Wasted effort — hours of work that bring no customers, no bookings, no calls.
Frustration — the owner sees no results, loses interest, and concludes "social media does not work."
What replaces random posting:
Content pillars — a small set of topics the business posts about again and again.
A cadence — a realistic posting schedule the business can sustain.
A calendar — a plan that says what to post, when and why.
Real-life comparison. Think of a radio station. A station that plays whatever the DJ feels like — rock, then gospel, then news,
then silence — will lose listeners. A station that plays music in the morning, news at noon, talk in the evening and keeps that
schedule every day — that station builds an audience. Content pillars are the station's schedule.
Important limitation / misconception. A content plan does not guarantee viral posts or instant results. It guarantees
consistency, focus and purpose — and those are what build trust and orders over time.
F.2 Content pillars — the topics you post about again and again
The idea. Content pillars are the three to five main topics a business posts about. They are the categories that give a page its
focus and personality.
Plain-English definition. A content pillar is a main topic or theme that a business returns to again and again — like the
columns that hold up a building.
Why they exist. Pillars solve the "what do I post?" problem forever. Instead of staring at a blank screen every day, a manager
with pillars knows: "This week I need one post from each pillar." The pillars keep the page focused, varied and connected to the
client's goals.
How to choose pillars — the four rules:
1. Connected to the client's business — every pillar must relate to what the client sells.
2. Connected to the audience's interests — every pillar must be something the audience cares about.
3. Connected to a goal — every pillar must serve at least one of the client's goals (orders, awareness, etc.).
4. Sustainable — the client must be able to create content for each pillar regularly.

F.4 Setting a realistic posting cadence
The idea. Cadence is how often the client posts — daily, three times a week, once a week. The key is choosing a cadence the
business can actually sustain.
Plain-English definition. Cadence is the posting schedule — how many posts per week, on which days, at what times.
Why it matters. A cadence that is too ambitious will collapse within two weeks. A cadence that is too sparse will make the page
look abandoned. The right cadence is the most the business can sustain without burning out.
The sustainability test — three questions:
1. Who is creating the content? One person? Two? A team?
2. How much time do they have? An hour a day? A few hours a week?
3. How much content can they realistically make? Photos are quick. Videos take longer. Carousels take design time.
Realistic cadences for a small Port Harcourt business:
Cadence Posts per week Who can sustain it Risk
Light 1–2 A very busy owner with no help. The page may look quiet.
Steady 3 One owner with a phone and a little time. Sustainable and professional.
Active 4–5 An owner with help or a manager. Good for growth, but needs planning.
Intense 6–7 A dedicated creator or team. High risk of burnout for a small business.
Worked example — cadence for Taste of Rivers Kitchen:
Who creates the content? Chef Nkechi (the owner) with help from the manager.
How much time? A few hours a week — filming while cooking, photographing while plating.
What can they realistically make? 3 posts a week — two photos and one short video.
Decision: Steady cadence — 3 posts per week, on Monday, Wednesday and Friday, at 12 p.m. or 5 p.m. (when people decide
what to eat).
The key lesson — start steady, grow later. A business that starts with 3 posts a week and keeps it up for a month is better
than a business that starts with 7 posts a week and collapses in two weeks. Consistency beats intensity.
Common misconception. "We need to post every day to grow." No — you need to post consistently to grow. Three posts a
week, every week, beats seven posts one week and nothing the next.
F.5 Building a two-week content calendar
The idea. A content calendar is a plan that says what to post, when, on which platform, in which format, with which caption
idea. It is the tool that turns pillars and cadence into actual posts.
Plain-English definition. A content calendar is a simple table — one row per post, with columns for the date, platform, pillar,
format, caption idea and asset needed.
Why it exists. A calendar removes the daily "what do I post?" panic. The manager knows on Monday morning exactly what to
post on Monday, Wednesday and Friday. The client can see the plan and approve it. Nothing is forgotten, nothing is rushed.

The key lesson — every row has a pillar, a format and a caption idea. A calendar row that says "post something about food"

How to build a calendar in 30 minutes:
1. Choose your pillars (from F.2).
2. Choose your cadence (from F.4).
3. Open the calendar template.
4. For each posting day, pick a pillar and a format.
5. Write a caption idea — just the hook, not the full caption.
6. Note the asset needed — a photo, a video, a graphic.
7. Repeat for two weeks.
Real-life comparison. Think of a school timetable. A teacher without a timetable teaches whatever comes to mind — chaos.
A teacher with a timetable knows exactly what to teach, when and in which classroom. A content calendar is the manager's
timetable.
Important limitation / misconception. A calendar is a plan, not a prison. If a post is not ready, move it — do not post
something rushed and bad. If a special event comes up, add it — the calendar is flexible. The point is to plan, not to be rigid.
F.6 Summary of the lecture
Random posting fails because it has no focus, no consistency and no connection to goals.
Content pillars are the three to five main topics a business posts about again and again — connected to the business, the
audience and the goals.
Formats (image, Reel, carousel, story, text) are matched to platforms and purposes — not to trends.
Cadence is how often the business posts — the most it can sustain without burning out.
A content calendar is a simple table — one row per post, with a pillar, format and caption idea — that turns the plan into
action.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. The Hungry Student is a small food business near the University of Port Harcourt. It sells jollof rice, fried rice, plantain
and fish — a full plate for ₦1,000. The owner, a young woman named Blessing, started the business six months ago. She posts
on Instagram — but randomly. Some days she posts three times, some days she does not post for a week. Her photos are
sometimes clear, sometimes blurry. Her captions are sometimes "Good morning" and sometimes a full description with prices.
She has 1,200 followers but only gets about 10 orders a week — and she cannot tell which posts bring orders. She is frustrated.
She thinks "social media does not work for food."
Relevant facts.
Blessing's food is genuinely good — students love her jollof.
Her posting is completely random — no pattern, no plan, no consistency.
Her photos and captions are inconsistent — sometimes professional, sometimes not.
She has no content pillars, no calendar and no way to measure results.
She posts from her phone, usually late at night when she is tired.
Decision point. A social media manager has been hired. What should the manager do first — and what should they avoid?
Possible approaches.
1. Post more often — "let us post every day, that will fix it."
2. Buy followers — "let us get the page to 5,000 followers, then orders will come."
3. Build a content strategy — choose pillars, set a cadence, build a calendar and post consistently for 30 days.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — a content strategy. The manager should:
Choose four pillars: Menu & prices, Behind the scenes, Customer love, Student specials.
Set a cadence: 3 posts a week — Monday, Wednesday, Friday — at 12 p.m. (when students decide lunch).
Build a two-week calendar: Every post has a pillar, format, caption idea and asset.
Post consistently for 30 days: No random posting, no gaps, no "good morning" graphics.
Measure results: At the end of 30 days, check which posts got the most orders — and do more of that.
What the manager should avoid:
Posting more often without a plan — seven random posts a week will not fix a planning problem.
Buying followers — fake followers bring no orders.
Using jargon with Blessing — she does not know "content pillars" or "cadence." The manager must explain in plain
language.
Expecting instant results — consistency takes 30 days to show results. The manager should set that expectation from day
one.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is easy but ineffective — more random posting will not fix a planning problem.
Approach 2 is cheap but destructive — fake followers make the page look big while bringing zero orders.
Approach 3 takes a few hours of planning, but it sets up a month of focused, effective content — and it gives the manager
a way to prove the results.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional does not guess. A professional plans, executes and measures. That is why a
professional can charge ₦15,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Choosing too many pillars
Symptoms: Learner chooses seven or eight content pillars. Likely cause: They think more pillars = more content = more results.
How to investigate: Ask: "Can the client realistically create content for every one of these pillars every week?" Corrective steps:
Limit to three to five pillars. Each must be sustainable — if the client cannot create content for it regularly, it does not belong.
How to confirm resolved: The learner has three to five pillars, each sustainable. Prevention: Remind the class — three clear
pillars beat ten unclear ones.
K.2 Choosing pillars that do not serve a goal
Symptoms: Learner chooses pillars like "memes" or "motivational quotes" that do not connect to the business or a goal. Likely
cause: They think entertaining content is the same as effective content. How to investigate: Ask: "Does this pillar help the
client get orders, bookings or awareness? How?" Corrective steps: Every pillar must serve at least one goal. If it does not,
remove it or replace it with one that does. How to confirm resolved: Each pillar is connected to a goal. Prevention: Always ask
"what goal does this pillar serve?" before adding it.
K.3 Setting an unsustainable cadence
Symptoms: Learner sets a cadence of 7 posts a week for a client who is one person with one phone. Likely cause: They think
more posting = faster growth. How to investigate: Ask: "Who is creating this content? How much time do they have? Can they
really do this every week?" Corrective steps: Use the sustainability test — who, how much time, how much content. Choose a
cadence the client can sustain. How to confirm resolved: The cadence is realistic for the client's capacity. Prevention: Start
steady, grow later. Three posts a week, every week, beats seven posts one week and nothing the next.
K.4 Leaving the calendar incomplete
Symptoms: Calendar rows are missing a pillar, format, caption idea or asset. Likely cause: The learner rushed or did not
understand the template. How to investigate: Ask: "What does this post look like? What is the caption? What do you need to
create it?" Corrective steps: Every row must have all five columns filled. A row that says "post something about food" is useless.
How to confirm resolved: Every row is complete. Prevention: Walk the room and check each row as learners fill it in.
K.5 Posting the same thing every week
Symptoms: Calendar repeats the exact same post every week — same pillar, same format, same caption. Likely cause: The
learner thinks repetition is consistency. How to investigate: Ask: "Is this post different from last week's? Why should someone
see the same thing again?" Corrective steps: Vary the pillars and formats across weeks. Consistency means showing up
regularly — not posting the identical thing. How to confirm resolved: Week two varies from week one. Prevention: Remind
the class — consistency is about regularity, not repetition.
K.6 Writing vague caption ideas
Symptoms: Caption ideas say "post about the menu" or "share a photo." Likely cause: The learner does not yet understand
hook–value–CTA. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the hook? What is the price? What is the CTA?" Corrective steps: Every
caption idea must have a hook and a CTA. "This week's menu — jollof ₦1,500. Order on WhatsApp." Not "post about the
menu." How to confirm resolved: Every caption idea has a hook and a CTA. Prevention: Remind the class — a caption idea is
not a topic, it is a plan for the words.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
