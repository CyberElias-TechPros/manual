## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a complete client proposal — services, scope, exclusions, posting schedule, response times, deliverables,
package price in naira, payment terms, revision policy, account ownership and confidentiality — that reads like a real client
document.
2. Build a 30-day content calendar — one row per post with date, platform, pillar, format, caption or hook idea, and asset
needed — that fits 30 days and one creator, and connects every post to a goal.
3. Create at least six written sample posts with captions, calls-to-action and hashtags, and at least four designed in Canva in
one consistent brand look.
4. Prepare a one-page reporting template — metrics, targets, actuals, what worked, what did not, next-month action —
ready to fill in after 30 days.
5. Write community-management guidelines — tone of voice, response-time target, moderation rules and an escalation
step for complaints — and a growth and promotion note that rejects fake growth.
6. Present and defend the package in a short talk — explaining the audience, platform choices, a sample post, the reporting
approach and how they would handle a bad month — and answer at least three questions.

Common gap: learners remember some letters. Write them on the board quickly.

Expected answer: (likes + comments + saves + shares) ÷ reach × 100.
Common gap: learners say "likes ÷ followers." Clarify: "Total interactions ÷ reach × 100. Not followers — reach."

F.1 What a client proposal is — and why it matters
The idea. A client proposal is a written document that tells a potential client exactly what you will do for them, how much it will
cost, and what they can expect. It is the document that turns a conversation into a contract.
Plain-English definition. A client proposal is a professional agreement written in plain language — it says who you are, what
you will deliver, when you will deliver it, how much you charge, and what the client must do to make it work.
Why it exists. A client who pays ₦20,000 or ₦35,000 a month for social media management is taking a risk. They are trusting
you with their brand, their account and their money. A written proposal protects both sides — it means there are no surprises,
no arguments later about "I thought you were going to post every day," and no confusion about what was agreed.

Real-life comparison. Think of a building contractor. A contractor who says "I will build your house, trust me" is a risk. A
contractor who hands you a written document — what they will build, the timeline, the cost, the materials, the payment
schedule — is a professional. A social media proposal is the same thing. It is how you look like a professional, not a guess.
Common misconception. "A proposal is just a price list." No — a price list says what things cost. A proposal says what you will
do, how you will do it, and what the client can expect. The price is one part of a much bigger document.
The key lesson — a proposal is a trust document. The proposal is not just paperwork. It is the thing that makes a client feel
safe enough to hand over their business. A clear, honest, complete proposal wins clients. A vague, incomplete proposal loses
them.
F.2 The strategy summary — audience, goals, platforms and positioning
The idea. The strategy summary is the part of the proposal that explains why you are doing what you are doing. It shows the
client that you understand their business, their audience and their goals — and that your plan is based on thinking, not
guessing.
Plain-English definition. A strategy summary is a short section — usually one to two pages — that states who the client's
audience is, what the goals are, which platforms you will use and why, and what makes the business different.

F.3 The 30-day content calendar — planning a full month
The idea. A 30-day content calendar is a day-by-day plan of what you will post for the next month. It is the most practical part
of the proposal — the client can see exactly what is coming.
Plain-English definition. A content calendar is a table with one row per post. Each row says the date, the platform, the content
pillar, the format, the caption or hook idea, and what asset (photo, video, graphic) is needed.
Why it exists. A client who can see a full month of planned posts feels safe. They know you are not going to disappear after one
week. They can also give feedback before you post — which saves everyone time. The calendar is the difference between "I will
post sometimes" and "I have a plan."

F.4 Sample posts — showing, not telling
The idea. Sample posts are finished posts — written captions and designed graphics — that you include in the proposal to
show the client what their content will look like. You are not just saying "I will make nice posts." You are showing them.
Plain-English definition. A sample post is a complete, ready-to-publish post — caption, hashtags and visual — created
specifically for the client, included in the proposal so the client can see the quality and style of the work.
Why it exists. Most small business owners are not visual people. They cannot imagine what "good content" looks like from a
description. But when they see a real post — a clear photo, a strong hook, a clear CTA, a consistent brand look — they
understand immediately. Sample posts are how you prove your skill before you are hired.
How many and what kind: The capstone requires at least six written sample posts and at least four designed in Canva in one
consistent brand look. The six posts should cover different pillars and formats — for example:
# Pillar Format Purpose
1 Menu spotlight Single image with caption Show a popular dish with a price and CTA
2 Behind the scenes Reel caption Show the cooking process — build trust
3 Customer love Testimonial graphic Share a real review — social proof
4 Educational tip Carousel (3 slides) Teach something useful — save value
5 Weekend special Flyer / event graphic Promote a time-limited offer
6 Brand story Single image with caption Tell the client's story — build connection
The consistent brand look. The four Canva designs must look like they come from the same business. This means:
The same colours — for example, warm orange and deep green, which suit a food brand.
The same fonts — one heading font, one body font, used every time.
The same logo — a simple text-based logo in the same corner of every design.
The same style — for example, always a clean photo on a solid colour background, with the price in a coloured box.

F.5 The reporting template — how you will prove results
The idea. A reporting template is a one-page table that shows the client exactly how you will measure and report results. It is
the part of the proposal that says: "After 30 days, I will come back to you with numbers, insights and next steps."
Plain-English definition. A reporting template is a reusable document with space for the key metrics — reach, engagement
rate, saves, shares, clicks, follower growth — with columns for the target, the actual result, what worked, what did not and what
you will do next.
Why it exists. A client who pays ₦20,000 or ₦35,000 a month wants to know: "Is this working?" The reporting template is how
you answer that question honestly. It shows the client that you will measure real numbers, report them clearly, and make
recommendations based on what you find.

F.8 Presenting and defending — the viva
The idea. The presentation — also called the viva — is a short spoken talk where you present your capstone package to the
class (or to a real client) and answer questions. It is the final proof that you understand what you have built.
Plain-English definition. The presentation is a 3–4 minute talk where you explain your proposal and plan, show at least one
sample post, describe how you would report a bad month, and answer questions from the audience.

What you say How long

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chef Nkechi has been running Taste of Rivers Kitchen for two years. Her Instagram page has 200 followers and has
been silent for three months. She gets about 10 WhatsApp orders a week — not enough to pay her two assistants and cover
food costs. She hires a social media manager who promises to "make the page active and bring more orders." The manager
sends her a proposal. Chef Nkechi reads it. The proposal is one page — it says "I will post regularly and grow your page" and
quotes ₦25,000 a month. There is no strategy summary, no calendar, no sample posts, no reporting template, no community
guidelines and no ethics statement. Chef Nkechi is not sure what she is buying, but she is desperate, so she agrees. The
manager posts twice in the first week, then once, then not at all. After 30 days, Chef Nkechi has 3 new followers and 2 extra
orders. She has paid ₦25,000 and has nothing to show for it. She feels robbed.
Relevant facts.
The proposal was one page — no detail, no proof, no plan.
The manager posted 4 times in 30 days.
The manager provided no report at the end of the month.
Chef Nkechi has 3 new followers and 2 extra orders after 30 days.
She paid ₦25,000.
The manager cannot explain why the page did not grow.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should a professional manager have done?
Possible approaches.
1. Blame the algorithm — "Instagram just did not show the posts."
2. Blame the client — "Your food is good but your photos are not professional enough."
3. Acknowledge the failures — no plan, no calendar, no sample posts, no report, no communication — and propose a
professional restart.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — acknowledge the failures. A professional manager would:
Apologise for the poor start and the lack of communication.
Explain what went wrong: no strategy, no calendar, no sample posts, no reporting template, no community guidelines.
Present a proper proposal — the full eight-component package with a strategy summary, a 30-day calendar, six sample
posts, a reporting template, community guidelines, a growth note and an ethics statement.
Offer a fair restart — a clear plan for the next 30 days, with honest reporting and regular communication.
What the manager should have done from the start:
Write a complete proposal — all eight sections, with a strategy summary, a calendar, sample posts, a reporting template,
community guidelines and a growth note.
Show sample posts — so Chef Nkechi could see the quality and style before agreeing.
Set measurable goals — 30 orders, 500 followers — so both sides know what success looks like.
Deliver a report — after 30 days, show the numbers, what worked, what did not and what will change.
Communicate — a weekly update, even if it is just a WhatsApp message: "This week we got 8 orders from the jollof post.
Next week we will try a new hook."
Never fake it — no bought followers, no inflated numbers, no excuses.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is a lie — the manager knows the plan was poor.
Approach 2 is deflection — the client's photos were fine; the manager's plan was not.
Approach 3 is hard but right — it rebuilds trust and sets up a professional relationship. A client who sees a manager own
their mistakes and present a proper plan is more likely to give them another chance.
Professional connection. This is exactly what separates a professional from someone who "just posts." A professional has a
plan, shows proof, reports honestly and communicates. A "poster" has none of these. That is why a professional can charge
₦25,000 a month and a "poster" cannot. The capstone you are building today is the document that makes you a professional.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Incomplete proposal — missing sections
Symptoms: The proposal is one or two pages — no strategy summary, no calendar, no sample posts, no reporting template.
Likely cause: The learner thinks a proposal is just a price list. How to investigate: Ask: "Can you show me your strategy
summary? Your calendar? Your sample posts?" Corrective steps: A proposal has eight sections. Every section must be present,
even if short. How to confirm resolved: The learner can point to all eight sections in their document. Prevention: Use the
assembly checklist — tick each component as it is completed.
K.2 Inconsistent calendar — posts that do not connect to goals
Symptoms: The calendar has posts that do not serve either goal — orders or followers. Likely cause: The learner is filling rows
without thinking about purpose. How to investigate: Pick a random row and ask: "What goal does this post serve?" Corrective
steps: Every post must connect to a goal. If it does not, either change it or remove it. How to confirm resolved: The learner can
explain the purpose of any post in the calendar. Prevention: Teach the "What is this post for?" test — apply it to every row.
K.3 No sample posts — promising quality without showing it
Symptoms: The proposal says "I will make nice posts" but shows no examples. Likely cause: The learner does not have finished
posts to show. How to investigate: Ask: "Can you show me one finished post — caption, hashtags, visual?" Corrective steps:
Create at least one sample post — hook, value, CTA, hashtags — and describe the visual. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can show at least one complete sample post. Prevention: Remind learners that sample posts are proof — a client
cannot buy what they cannot see.
K.4 No reporting template — no way to prove results
Symptoms: The proposal has no plan for measuring or reporting. Likely cause: The learner thinks reporting is something that
happens later, not something to plan now. How to investigate: Ask: "After 30 days, how will Chef Nkechi know if the work is
paying off?" Corrective steps: Include the six-column reporting template — metrics, targets, actuals, what worked, what did
not, next steps. How to confirm resolved: The proposal includes a reporting template with targets. Prevention: The reporting
template is a promise — it commits the manager to honest measurement.
K.5 No ethics statement — missing the trust signal
Symptoms: The proposal does not mention fake followers, fake reviews or honest growth. Likely cause: The learner thinks
ethics is assumed. How to investigate: Ask: "Chef Nkechi has been burned before. Where do you promise her honesty?"
Corrective steps: Include a clear ethics statement — no bought followers, no fake reviews, no inflated numbers. How to
confirm resolved: The ethics statement is present and explicit. Prevention: The ethics statement is not optional — it is a core
part of the proposal.
K.6 Presentation without ownership — reading instead of explaining
Symptoms: The learner reads from their document instead of explaining their decisions. Likely cause: The learner has
memorised the document but does not understand the reasons behind it. How to investigate: Ask: "Why did you choose
Instagram? Why 30 orders? Why that CTA?" Corrective steps: The learner must be able to explain every decision — the
audience, the platforms, the goals, the posts, the reporting, the bad-month plan. How to confirm resolved: The learner can
answer three questions without reading from the document. Prevention: Rehearse the presentation out loud — if you cannot
explain it, you do not own it.
K.7 Unrealistic posting schedule — seven posts a week
Symptoms: The calendar has seven posts a week — one per day. Likely cause: The learner thinks more is better. How to
investigate: Ask: "Can one person make seven quality posts a week for 30 days?" Corrective steps: Three posts per week is
realistic and sustainable for one creator. More leads to burnout and declining quality. How to confirm resolved: The calendar
has 12–13 posts over 30 days. Prevention: Teach the "one creator" rule — plan for what you can sustain, not what looks
impressive on paper.
K.8 Sample posts that do not match the client
Symptoms: The sample posts are generic — they could be for any business. Likely cause: The learner wrote generic posts
instead of client-specific posts. How to investigate: Ask: "Could this post be for any food business? What makes it specific to
Taste of Rivers Kitchen?" Corrective steps: Every sample post should reference the client's dishes, prices, location and brand.
How to confirm resolved: A reader can tell which business the post is for. Prevention: Write every sample post with the client's
name, dishes and prices in mind.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"A proposal is just a price list." → No — it has eight sections and every section builds trust.
"More posts = better." → Three sustainable posts per week beats seven posts that burn out in two weeks.
"The calendar is just a list of dates." → Every post must connect to a goal — otherwise it is noise.
"Sample posts are optional." → They are proof — a client cannot buy what they cannot see.
"The reporting template is for later." → It is a promise you make in the proposal — it commits you to honesty.
"The ethics statement is not important." → It is critical — especially for a client who has been burned.
"Reading the presentation is presenting." → No — you must explain and defend every decision out loud.
"A good product sells itself online." → No — without a plan, even a good product stays invisible.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Proposal section drill: give the learner a one-page proposal and ask them to identify which of the eight sections are
missing. Then ask them to write the missing ones.
2. Calendar connection drill: give the learner a calendar with five posts and ask them to state which goal each post serves. If a
post serves no goal, ask them to rewrite it or remove it.
3. Sample post writing drill: give the learner a client brief and ask them to write one sample post — hook, value, CTA,
hashtags — in 10 minutes.
4. Presentation rehearsal drill: ask the learner to present their package to a partner in 4 minutes. The partner asks three
questions. The learner must answer without reading.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed proposal — services, price and exclusions already filled in — and ask
the learner to complete the strategy summary, calendar and one sample post. Provide the reporting template with targets
already filled in.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full assembly of all eight components plus presentation outline.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a client-facing summary — a one-page document that explains the entire
plan in plain language a non-technical client can understand, with no jargon. This tests whether the learner truly
understands what they have built and can communicate it to the person who matters most — the client.
