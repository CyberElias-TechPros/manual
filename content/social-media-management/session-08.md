# Paid Promotion Basics and Growth

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Grow and Report

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with paid promotion)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–7 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, build a strategy, design a content calendar, create content, manage a community, schedule posts, draft a working agreement and produce an honest monthly report. Everyday smartphone use assumed. No prior advertising or media buying experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 7 we learned to measure and report honestly. Today we learn to grow — both organically and with paid promotion. We cover what boosting is, when it makes sense, how to set basic targeting, how to plan a small naira budget with a stop rule, and why bought followers and engagement pods destroy trust. The practical is a paper boost plan — no real money is spent.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the sample post options for the boost decision exercise (Section D) ready on screen; the boost-plan worksheet (Section H) printed per learner; the budget planner printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available; a whiteboard for the live budget build; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This session has two messages, and both must land. First: paid promotion is a useful tool when used wisely — it can help a good business reach more of the right people. Second: fake growth is fraud, and a manager who buys followers or uses engagement pods is a cheat. Keep the mood balanced and confident. Many learners will have seen "buy followers" offered online — your job is to show them clearly why it is worthless and risky, and to teach them the honest alternative.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what boosting is, how it differs from organic growth, and when it makes sense for a small business.
2. Plan a boost with an audience, a duration, a naira budget and a success measure — on paper, without spending real
money.
3. Calculate basic results in naira — cost per result, return on a small budget — and decide whether to continue, adjust or
stop.
4. Describe honest organic growth methods that build a real audience over time.
5. Argue why bought followers, likes and engagement pods are rejected — with clear reasoning.
6. Apply the rule: never spend a client's money on ads without written approval.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show three fictional posts from the same business — Taste of Rivers Kitchen. All three are
real posts from the past month. Do not explain which one is best yet.
Post A — "The Strong Post." A clear, well-lit photo of jollof with a price graphic. It reached 2,400 people naturally, got 180
likes, 45 comments, 80 saves and 30 shares. 25 people clicked the WhatsApp link.
Post B — "The Weak Post." A dark, blurry photo of plantain with no price. It reached 150 people, got 8 likes, 1 comment, 0
saves and 0 shares. 1 person clicked the link.
Post C — "The Event Post." A flyer for a weekend special — small-chops tray, ₦8,000, Saturday only. It reached 350 people,
got 20 likes, 5 comments, 10 saves and 8 shares. 4 people clicked the link.
What the instructor says:
"Look at these three posts. Post A is strong — clear photo, visible price, people saved it and shared it. Post B is weak —
dark, no price, nobody cared. Post C is an event post — a time-limited offer. Now, hands up: which of these three posts
would you spend money to boost? … Post A, obviously. Why? Because it is already working — boosting a strong post
makes it work harder. Would you boost Post B? No — because boosting a bad post just shows more people a bad post.
Would you boost Post C? Maybe — if the event is this week and you want to fill it. Today you learn to make that decision
— when to boost, when not to, and how to plan a small naira budget. And you learn why buying followers is not growth
— it is fraud."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at Post A and say "it is already working," "it has good numbers," "it is clear."
Accept all answers. Draw out the logic: boost what works, fix what does not.
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"Boosting is simple: you pay the platform to show your post to more of the right people. But the skill is not pressing the
button — the skill is deciding what to boost, who to show it to, how much to spend, and when to stop. A manager who
boosts blindly will burn a client's money in a day. A manager who plans a boost will get real results. Today you learn to
plan — on paper, without spending a single naira."
Why this opener works: It uses three concrete posts with real numbers to make the abstract decision of "what to boost"
tangible. Learners see immediately that boosting a strong post makes sense and boosting a weak post does not — and they
want to learn the full process.

<!-- kind:prereq_review -->

Expected answer: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
Common gap: learners remember some letters. Write them on the board quickly.
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 7 we learned to measure honestly. Today we learn to grow — with and without money. We will cover what

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Organic growth vs paid promotion — what money can and cannot buy
The idea. Organic growth is growing a page without paying — through good content, consistency and engagement. Paid
promotion is paying a platform to show content to more people. Both have a place — but they do different jobs.
Plain-English definition. Organic growth is earning attention by being worth following. Paid promotion is buying attention to
reach more of the right people faster.
What money can buy:
Reach — more people see the post.
Speed — results in days, not months.
Specific targeting — showing the post to people in a certain area, age or interest.
Support for something that works — boosting a strong post to make it work harder.
What money cannot buy:
Loyalty — a boosted follower who does not care about the business will never buy.
Trust — people trust recommendations and quality, not ads.
Engagement rate — boosting a post with a low engagement rate will not suddenly make people care.
A good product — no amount of money will make a bad product sell.
Real results — money can bring eyes; only quality converts eyes into orders.
The key lesson — organic is the foundation, paid is the amplifier. A page that grows organically has real followers, real
engagement and real trust. Paid promotion on top of that foundation can accelerate growth. But paid promotion on a page
with no organic foundation is like building a house on sand — it will not last.
Real-life comparison. Think of a musician. A musician who builds a fanbase by playing gigs, sharing songs and connecting
with listeners is growing organically. A musician who pays to have their song played on the radio is using paid promotion. The
radio play can help — but only if the song is good. A bad song on the radio is just expensive noise.
F.2 What boosting is — and when it makes sense
The idea. Boosting is paying a platform (Instagram, Facebook) to show one of your existing posts to more people than it would
reach naturally. It is the simplest form of paid promotion.
Plain-English definition. Boosting is paying to give one post a bigger audience — so more of the right people see it.
Why it exists. A post that works naturally deserves a bigger audience. Boosting lets a business put money behind their best
content instead of hoping the algorithm shows it to enough people.
When boosting makes sense — the four situations:
1. A strong post — a post that is already performing well naturally. Boosting it makes it work harder.
2. An event — a time-limited offer, a weekend special, a launch. Boosting fills the event.
3. A goal that needs reach — when the client's goal is awareness (for example, "reach 5,000 people this month") and organic
reach is not enough.
4. A proven offer — a discount or package that has converted before. Boosting it to a targeted audience can bring orders.
When boosting does NOT make sense:
A weak post — boosting a post with low engagement just shows more people a post they will ignore.
A bad product or offer — no amount of money will fix a weak offer.
No clear goal — boosting without a goal is just spending money.
No budget agreement — never boost without the client's written approval and a clear budget.
Worked example — the boost decision for the three posts:
Post Decision Why
Post A (strong jollof) Boost — ₦2,000 for 3 days Already working; boost to reach more of the right people.
Post B (weak plantain) Do not boost — fix the post first Low engagement; boosting a weak post wastes money.
Post C (event flyer) Boost — ₦3,000 for 2 days Time-limited event; boost to fill it.
The key lesson — boost what works, fix what does not. A manager who boosts every post will waste a client's money. A
manager who boosts selectively — strong posts, events and proven offers — will get real results.
F.3 Planning a boost — audience, duration, budget and success measure
The idea. A boost without a plan is a gamble. A boost with a plan is an investment. The plan has four parts: audience, duration,
budget and success measure.
Plain-English definition. A boost plan is a simple document that says who the post will reach, how long it will run, how much it
will cost and what result it will achieve.
The four parts:
What it says Example
Audience Who will see the post — location, age,
interests.
Women and men, 18–40, within 15 km of Port Harcourt,
interested in food and restaurants.
What it says Example
Duration How many days the boost will run. 3 days (Friday to Sunday — when people decide weekend
food).
Budget How much money will be spent, in naira. ₦2,000 total — about ₦667 per day.
Success measure What result the boost will achieve — and
how it will be judged.
20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders. If we get fewer than 10
clicks after 2 days, we stop.
Worked example — a complete boost plan for Post A:
Boost Plan — Taste of Rivers Kitchen, Jollof Post
Post: The strong jollof photo (2,400 natural reach, 180 likes, 25 WhatsApp clicks).
Goal: Get 20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders.
Audience: People aged 18–40, within 15 km of Port Harcourt, interested in food, restaurants and cooking.
Duration: 3 days — Friday to Sunday.
Budget: ₦2,000 total (about ₦667 per day).
Success measure: 20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders. If fewer than 10 clicks after 2 days, stop the boost.
Approval: Chef Nkechi has approved this plan in writing (WhatsApp message, 10 a.m. Monday).
The key lesson — the stop rule is the most important part. A manager without a stop rule will keep spending money on a
boost that is not working. A manager with a stop rule — "if fewer than 10 clicks after 2 days, stop" — protects the client's
money.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader who pays for a stall at a busy market. A trader who pays for the stall, watches
the sales for two hours, and leaves if nobody is buying — that trader is using a stop rule. A trader who pays for the stall and
stays all day even when nobody is buying — that trader is wasting money.
F.4 Reading results in naira — cost per result and the stop rule
The idea. A boost produces numbers — clicks, orders, reach. A manager must read those numbers in naira and decide whether
to continue, adjust or stop.
Plain-English definition. Cost per result is how much each click, order or result cost in naira. The stop rule is the point at which
a boost is not working and must be stopped.
The two calculations:
1. Cost per click: Budget ÷ number of clicks.
Example: ₦2,000 ÷ 25 clicks = ₦80 per click.
2. Cost per order: Budget ÷ number of orders.
Example: ₦2,000 ÷ 5 orders = ₦400 per order.
Worked example — reading the boost results:
Day Budget spent Clicks Orders Cost per click Decision
Day 1 ₦667 6 1 ₦111 Below target — watch.
Day 2 ₦667 5 1 ₦133 Below target — stop rule triggered.
Day 3 ₦0 0 0 — Boost stopped.
Say: "After two days, we had 11 clicks and 2 orders. The stop rule said: if fewer than 10 clicks after 2 days, stop. We had 11 —
but the trend was down (6 then 5). A good manager would stop here and try a different post or audience. That is how you
protect the client's money."
The key lesson — judge the trend, not just the total. A boost that gets 11 clicks in two days is not terrible — but if the daily
trend is down, it will not reach 20 clicks in three days. A manager who watches the daily numbers can stop early and save the
rest of the budget.
Common misconception. "More money = more results." Not necessarily. A boost with the wrong audience or a weak post will
waste any budget. A boost with the right audience and a strong post can work with ₦500 a day.
F.5 Honest organic growth — building a real audience
The idea. Organic growth is growing a page without paying — through good content, consistency and genuine engagement.
It is slower than paid promotion, but it builds something real.
Plain-English definition. Organic growth is earning followers and engagement by being worth following — posting
consistently, using the right hashtags, engaging with the community and collaborating with others.
The five honest organic growth methods:
1. Post consistently — three times a week, every week. The algorithm rewards consistency.
2. Use relevant hashtags — 3–10 focused tags (location + niche + brand), not 30 random ones.
3. Engage with the community — reply to every comment, comment on other local pages, join local conversations.
4. Collaborate — partner with a complementary business (a zobo seller for a food page) to reach each other's audiences.
5. Create shareable content — behind-the-scenes videos, customer photos and useful tips that people want to pass on.
Worked example — an organic growth plan for Taste of Rivers Kitchen:
Week Action
Week 1 Post 3 times with 5 relevant hashtags each. Reply to every comment within 2 hours.
Week 2 Comment on 5 local food pages (genuine comments, not spam). Post a customer photo.
Week 3 Partner with a local zobo seller — cross-promote each other's pages.
Week 4 Post a "behind the kitchen" Reel and ask viewers to share it.
The key lesson — organic growth is a marathon, not a sprint. A page that grows honestly over three months will have real
followers, real engagement and real trust. That is worth more than 10,000 bought followers who never buy.
Real-life comparison. Think of a farmer. A farmer who plants seeds, waters them and waits for the harvest is growing
organically. A farmer who buys fake fruit and puts it on the tree is faking it — and everyone can tell. A manager who grows a
page honestly is the first farmer.
F.6 Why fake growth is rejected — the case against bought followers and pods
The idea. Bought followers, likes, views and engagement pods are fraud. They inflate numbers without bringing real customers
— and they destroy trust when discovered.
Plain-English definition. Fake growth is paying for followers, likes, views or comments that are not from real people who care
about the business.
Why fake growth destroys value:
1. Fake followers never buy. A bought follower is a bot or a fake account. They will never order, book or call.
2. Fake engagement destroys credibility. A page with 10,000 followers and 3 likes per post looks fake — and customers
notice.
3. Platforms punish fake growth. Instagram and Facebook regularly remove fake followers and engagement — a page that
bought followers can lose them overnight, or be banned.
4. Fake growth inflates expectations. A client who believes they have 10,000 real followers will be confused when they get 3
orders a month. The manager looks incompetent — or dishonest.
5. Fake growth is fraud. A manager who buys followers for a client is taking money for something worthless. That is fraud,
not marketing.
Engagement pods — what they are and why they fail. An engagement pod is a group of accounts that agree to like and
comment on each other's posts to trick the algorithm. They fail because: the engagement is fake, the algorithm eventually
detects it, and the followers gained from it are not real.
Say this to the class:
"Let me be very clear. In this course, and in this profession, there is no such thing as a harmless fake follower. Every bought
follower, every fake like, every engagement pod — they are all fraud. They make the page look big while bringing zero
customers. They get the page banned. And they destroy the trust between the manager and the client. A manager who
buys followers is not a shortcut — they are a cheat. And a cheat will be caught. Real growth is slower and worth more.
Always."
Real-life comparison. Think of a student who cheats on an exam. A student who cheats might pass one exam — but they
have not learned anything, and they will fail the real test when it comes. A manager who buys followers might inflate one report
— but they have not built anything real, and they will fail when the client checks their own insights.
F.7 The written approval rule — never spend without permission
The idea. A manager must never spend a client's money on ads without written approval. This is both an ethics rule and a
professional standard.
Plain-English definition. The written approval rule means the client must agree — in writing — to the boost plan before any
money is spent.
Why it exists: Spending someone's money without permission is a serious breach of trust. Even if the boost works, the client
may feel violated. Even if the boost fails, the client may feel robbed. Written approval protects both sides.
What the written approval includes:
The post that will be boosted.
The audience, duration and budget.
The success measure and stop rule.
The client's explicit agreement — a WhatsApp message, email or signed note is enough.
Worked example — the approval message:
Manager to Chef Nkechi: "Chef Nkechi, I would like to boost Friday's jollof post. Plan: ₦2,000 for 3 days, targeting people
within 15 km of Port Harcourt interested in food. Goal: 20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders. If we get fewer than 10 clicks after
2 days, I will stop. Do you approve?"
Chef Nkechi: "Approved. ₦2,000 for 3 days. Let me know the results."
The key lesson — approval is not a formality; it is protection. A manager who gets written approval before spending is
professional. A manager who spends without approval is gambling with the client's trust — and their own reputation.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Organic growth is earning attention by being worth following. Paid promotion is buying attention to reach more of the
right people faster.
Boosting is paying to show one existing post to more people. It makes sense for strong posts, events, awareness goals and
proven offers — never for weak posts or without a clear goal.
A boost plan has four parts: audience, duration, budget and success measure — plus a stop rule.
Cost per result is calculated in naira — cost per click and cost per order. The manager judges the daily trend, not just the
total.
Honest organic growth is a marathon — consistency, relevant hashtags, community engagement, collaborations and
shareable content.
Fake growth is fraud — bought followers, likes, views and engagement pods destroy value and trust.
The written approval rule — never spend a client's money on ads without written approval.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Walk the class through a complete boost planning process — choosing what to boost, setting the audience and budget,
calculating cost per result and deciding whether to continue or stop — for the running client, Taste of Rivers Kitchen.
Setup: On the projector, show the three sample posts from the opening activity. Have the boost-plan worksheet and the budget
planner visible.

Post Natural performance Decision
Jollof photo 2,400 reach, 180 likes, 25 clicks Boost — strong post.
Plantain photo 150 reach, 8 likes, 1 click Do not boost — weak post. Fix it first.
Event flyer 350 reach, 20 likes, 4 clicks Maybe — if the event is this week.
Say: "We boost the jollof post. It is already working — clear photo, visible price, people clicking. We do not boost the plantain

G.2 Demonstration 2 — Write the boost plan (4 minutes)

Boost Plan — Taste of Rivers Kitchen, Jollof Post

Goal: Get 20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders.
Audience: People aged 18–40, within 15 km of Port Harcourt, interested in food and restaurants.

Budget: ₦2,000 total (about ₦667 per day).

G.3 Demonstration 3 — Read the results (5 minutes)
Say: "Now let us say the boost ran for two days. Here are the results."
Show the results on the projector:
Day Budget spent Clicks Orders Cost per click Decision
Day 1 ₦667 6 1 ₦111 Below target — watch.
Day 2 ₦667 5 1 ₦133 Trend is down — stop rule triggered.
Walk through the decision:
"After two days, we spent ₦1,334 and got 11 clicks and 2 orders. The stop rule said: if fewer than 10 clicks after 2 days,
stop. We had 11 — but the trend was down (6 then 5). A good manager stops here. We saved ₦666 of the budget. We do
not spend the third day on a boost that is declining."
Calculate the cost per result:
Cost per click so far: ₦1,334 ÷ 11 = ₦121 per click. Cost per order: ₦1,334 ÷ 2 = ₦667 per order.
Say: "₦121 per click and ₦667 per order. Is that good? For a small food business, a ₦1,500 plate of jollof — ₦667 per order is
high but not terrible if the customer becomes a regular. But the trend was down, so we stop. We try a different audience or a
different post next time."
G.4 Demonstration 4 — Get written approval (3 minutes)
Say: "Last — before we spend a single naira, we get approval."
Show the approval message on the projector:
Manager to Chef Nkechi: "Chef Nkechi, I would like to boost Friday's jollof post. Plan: ₦2,000 for 3 days, targeting people
within 15 km of Port Harcourt interested in food. Goal: 20 WhatsApp clicks and 5 orders. If we get fewer than 10 clicks after
2 days, I will stop. Do you approve?"
Chef Nkechi: "Approved. ₦2,000 for 3 days. Let me know the results."
Say: "Written approval. A WhatsApp message is enough. The client knows exactly what we will spend, who we will target and
what we will achieve. That is professional."
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Which post did we boost, and why?" (The jollof post — it was already working.)
"What is the stop rule?" (If fewer than 10 clicks after 2 days, stop.)
"What was the cost per click after two days?" (₦121.)
"Why did we stop even though we had 11 clicks?" (The trend was down — 6 then 5.)
"What do we need before we spend?" (Written approval.)
Expected result: Learners see a complete boost planning process — choose, plan, execute, read, stop, approve — and they
see that it is a disciplined process, not a gamble.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes a boost plan for a new fictional client — Glamour Touch Salon —
and reads the results to decide whether to continue, adjust or stop.
Starting materials: The printed boost-plan worksheet, the printed budget planner, a pen, a calculator. No phone needed for the
first part — it is a planning exercise.
CLIENT BRIEF — GLAMOUR TOUCH SALON (fictional). Glamour Touch Salon is a hair and beauty salon in Port Harcourt. It
offers braids, weaves, natural hair care and make-up. Prices run from ₦1,000 for a basic braid to ₦15,000 for a full weave
installation. The salon has 800 Instagram followers and posts rarely. The owner, "Aunty Funke," wants more bookings — people
should see her work, know her prices, and call to book. She can pay ₦20,000 a month for management. She has ₦5,000
available for a one-time boost. She does not want fake followers.
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners work; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your name at the top of the boost-plan worksheet. Read the client brief once.
2. Choose a post to boost. Look at the three sample posts below. Which one would you boost — and why?
3. Write the boost plan. Fill in all four parts — audience, duration, budget and success measure — plus a stop rule.
4. Get written approval. Write the approval message you would send to Aunty Funke.
5. Read the results. Use the sample results below. Calculate the cost per click and cost per order. Decide: continue, adjust or
stop — and explain why.
6. Pause and predict: Cover your worksheets and ask yourself: "What is the most important part of a boost plan?" Write your
answer at the bottom.
7. Compare your plan with the person beside you. If you chose different audiences or budgets, explain your choices.

Post Natural performance
Post A — Box braid photo: Clear photo of a fresh box braid style with a price. 1,800 reach, 120 likes, 35
comments, 60 saves, 20 shares,
18 phone clicks.
Post B — Dark selfie: A blurry selfie of Aunty Funke with no price. 120 reach, 6 likes, 1 comment, 0

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama Put's Rice and Beans hired a social media manager who promised to "grow the page fast." The manager spent
₦20,000 of Mama Put's money on a boost — without asking. The boost targeted "everyone in Nigeria" and ran for 10 days.
The results: 5,000 reach, 30 likes, 2 comments and 0 orders. The manager also bought 2,000 fake followers for ₦5,000 — the
page now has 2,500 followers but still gets only 3 likes per post. Mama Put is angry. She feels robbed. She does not know what
was real and what was fake.
Relevant facts.
The manager spent ₦20,000 on a boost without written approval.
The boost targeted "everyone in Nigeria" — not a specific audience.
The boost ran for 10 days with no stop rule.
The results: 5,000 reach, 30 likes, 2 comments, 0 orders.
The manager bought 2,000 fake followers for ₦5,000.
The page now has 2,500 followers but only 3 likes per post.
Mama Put feels robbed and does not trust the manager.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should a professional manager have done?
Possible approaches.
1. Defend the spending — "boosting is always a gamble."
2. Blame the platform — "Instagram did not show the post."
3. Acknowledge the mistakes — no written approval, no targeting, no stop rule, fake followers — and propose a
professional plan going forward.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — acknowledge the mistakes. A professional manager would:
Apologise for spending without approval and for buying fake followers.
Explain what went wrong: no targeting, no stop rule, no clear goal, fake followers.
Propose a professional plan: a small budget (₦5,000), a targeted audience, a 3-day duration, a clear success measure and a
stop rule — with written approval before spending.
Remove the fake followers (if possible) and commit to honest growth only.
What the manager should have done from the start:
Get written approval before spending any money.
Target a specific audience — not "everyone in Nigeria."
Set a clear goal and stop rule — and stop when the rule is triggered.
Never buy followers — fake growth is fraud.
Report honestly — real numbers, even when they are bad.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is defensive and dishonest — Mama Put will end the relationship.
Approach 2 is a lie — the manager knows what went wrong.
Approach 3 is hard but right — it rebuilds trust and sets up a professional relationship going forward.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional never spends without approval, never targets vaguely, never skips the stop rule and
never buys followers. A professional plans, executes, measures and reports honestly. That is why a professional can charge
₦20,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Spending without written approval
Symptoms: The manager spends the client's money on ads without asking. Likely cause: They think "I know what is best — I
will just do it." How to investigate: Ask: "Did the client approve this boost in writing? Can you show me the message?"
Corrective steps: Never spend without written approval. Send the boost plan and get explicit agreement before spending. How
to confirm resolved: The manager can show an approval message for every boost. Prevention: Written approval is non-
negotiable — make it a habit.
K.2 Boosting a weak post
Symptoms: The manager boosts a post with low natural engagement. Likely cause: They think boosting will fix a weak post.
How to investigate: Ask: "What was the natural reach and engagement of this post? Is it strong or weak?" Corrective steps:
Boost strong posts only. Fix weak posts first — better photo, clearer price, stronger hook. How to confirm resolved: The
manager can explain why a post is worth boosting. Prevention: Boost what works — never boost what does not.
K.3 No stop rule
Symptoms: The manager runs a boost with no trigger to stop. Likely cause: They think "more days = more results." How to
investigate: Ask: "When do you stop? What number tells you the boost is not working?" Corrective steps: Set a stop rule
before the boost — "if fewer than X clicks after 2 days, stop." How to confirm resolved: The plan includes a stop rule.
Prevention: The stop rule is the most important part of the plan — never skip it.
K.4 Targeting too broadly
Symptoms: The boost targets "everyone in Nigeria" or "all interests." Likely cause: They think a bigger audience = more results.
How to investigate: Ask: "Who is the client's customer? Where do they live? What are their interests?" Corrective steps: Target
a specific audience — location, age, interests — tied to the client's customer. How to confirm resolved: The audience is
specific and justified. Prevention: A targeted audience of 5,000 right people beats a broad audience of 500,000 wrong people.
K.5 Buying followers or using pods
Symptoms: The manager buys followers or joins an engagement pod. Likely cause: They think it is a shortcut to growth. How
to investigate: Ask: "Are these followers real? Will they ever buy from the client?" Corrective steps: Never buy followers or use
pods. Grow organically — consistency, hashtags, engagement, collaborations, shareable content. How to confirm resolved:
The manager can explain why fake growth is fraud. Prevention: Repeat the ethics line — real growth is slower and worth more.
K.6 Not judging the trend
Symptoms: The manager looks only at the total results, not the daily trend. Likely cause: They think the total is what matters.
How to investigate: Ask: "What were the daily numbers? Was the trend up or down?" Corrective steps: Judge the daily trend
— if clicks are declining day by day, the boost is fading. Stop early. How to confirm resolved: The manager can explain the
trend and its implications. Prevention: A declining trend is an early warning — teach learners to watch it.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
