# Audience, Goals and Platform Strategy

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Strategy

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with strategy)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks and explain the ethics rules. Everyday smartphone use assumed. No prior marketing or research experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 we defined the job: planning, creating, posting, engaging and reporting. Today we do the planning properly — before anyone posts a single thing. We research the audience, set goals, choose platforms with reasons, scan competitors and write a positioning statement for our running client, Taste of Rivers Kitchen.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the three fictional competitor pages (Section G) prepared as printed sheets or screenshots; the audience-profile worksheet (Section H) printed per learner; the goal-setting worksheet printed; the positioning-statement template printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available (from Session 1); a whiteboard for the live audience-profile build; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This session is where learners discover that strategy is not a big-word mystery — it is asking the right questions before posting. Keep the mood practical and confident. Many learners will want to skip to "what do I post?" — your job is to show them that the answers to who, why and where come first, and that skipping this step is why most small business pages fail.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why strategy comes before posting and what happens when a business skips it.
2. Research an audience using free methods (questions, observation, platform search) and write a simple audience profile
that names a real obstacle — not "everyone."
3. Set two measurable goals using the SMART format and connect each goal to a specific action.
4. Choose one or two platforms for a client with written reasons tied to the audience and the goal.
5. Run a basic three-competitor scan and extract two useful lessons from it.
6. Write a one-sentence positioning statement that says who the client serves, what they offer and why they are different.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two fictional Instagram pages side by side. Both are food businesses in Port Harcourt.
Do not explain which is "better" yet.
Page A — "Random Posting Kitchen." The page has 1,200 followers. The last ten posts are: a random meme, a photo of the
owner's car, a blurry photo of rice with no caption, a reposted news article, another meme, a photo of a wedding that has
nothing to do with food, a blurry selfie, a "good morning" graphic, a photo of plantain with no price, and another meme.
Posting times are random — 3 a.m., 11 p.m., 4 p.m. The bio says "We sell food."
Page B — "Planned Kitchen." The page has 800 followers. The last ten posts are: a clear jollof photo with price and order
number, a 20-second reel of small chops being plated, a carousel of "3 reasons our jollof is different," a customer testimonial
photo, a story asking "What should we cook this weekend?", a behind-the-scenes video of the kitchen, a fried rice photo with a
weekend special price, a reel of plantain frying, a "how to order" graphic, and a customer repost with permission. Posting times
are consistent — mostly 5 p.m. and 12 p.m. The bio says "Fresh local meals, delivered in Port Harcourt. Order on WhatsApp:
0803-XXX-XXXX."
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two pages. Page A has 1,200 followers. Page B has 800. Now tell me — which page would you order from?
… Page B, by a mile. Why? Page A has more followers, but it looks like nobody is home. Page B looks like a real business
that knows what it is doing. Here is the secret: Page B has fewer followers because it started later and has no money for
ads. But it is winning — because someone planned it. Someone decided who they are talking to, what to post, where to
post and why. That someone is a social media manager. Today you become that person."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at Page B and say "it looks professional," "it has prices," "it looks like a real
business," "Page A is just posting anything." Accept all answers. Draw out the difference: planning vs. random posting.
Then reveal the numbers: Say:
"Page A gets about 30 likes per post. Page B gets about 120 likes and 15 comments — and 5 of those comments become
orders every week. Page B has half the followers and ten times the results. That is the power of strategy. And strategy is
not a gift — it is a set of questions you can learn to ask. Let us learn them now."
Why this opener works: It uses a visual, side-by-side comparison that makes the abstract idea of "strategy" concrete. Learners
see immediately that planning beats random posting — and they want to know how to do it.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core tasks of a social media manager?"
Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners mix up the order. Clarify: "Planning comes first — before you create anything."
Question 2: "What is the difference between a personal account and a business account?"
Expected answer: A business account shows a category, a contact button and insights (numbers about who saw your post).
Common gap: some learners have never switched. Say: "We will do that in Session 6. For now, just know: business accounts
are free and they give you the numbers you need."
Question 3: "Name two things a client expects from a manager."
Expected answer: Any two of — consistency, prompt replies, professionalism, confidentiality, honest numbers.
Common gap: learners say "good content" or "more followers." Clarify: "Those are results. The expectations are about how
you work — consistency, replies, professionalism, confidentiality, honest numbers."
Question 4: "Why must a manager never buy followers?"
Expected answer: Fake followers never buy from the client, they inflate expectations, and they get the page flagged or
banned.
Common gap: learners think "a few fake followers won't hurt." Say: "Even a few — because the moment the client checks
their insights and sees 2,000 followers but 3 likes, they know. Trust is gone."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 1 we learned the job. Today we learn the first task — planning. And planning starts with three questions: Who
are we talking to? What are we trying to achieve? And where do these people spend time online? If you cannot answer
those three questions, you are not ready to post. Let us learn to answer them properly."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 3 extra minutes writing the five tasks on the board with a one-
line example of each before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why strategy comes before posting
The idea. Strategy is the plan that decides who you are talking to, what you are trying to achieve, where you will post and why
each post exists. It comes before any content is created.
Plain-English definition. Strategy is thinking before posting. It is the difference between a page that looks like a real business
and a page that looks like somebody's personal diary.
Why it exists. Most small business pages fail not because the food is bad or the product is poor — they fail because the owner
posts randomly. A meme today, a blurry photo next week, a "good morning" graphic at 3 a.m. The page has no focus, no
consistency and no connection to a goal. Customers scroll past because nothing speaks to them. Strategy fixes this by giving
every post a reason to exist.
What happens without strategy:
Random posting — no pattern, no focus, no goal.
Inconsistency — three posts in one day, silence for three weeks.
Wrong audience — posting student food content to a page followed by parents and professionals.
No measurement — the owner cannot tell if the page is working because there is nothing to measure against.
Wasted effort — hours of work that bring no orders, no bookings, no calls.
What happens with strategy:
Every post has a purpose — it serves a goal and speaks to a specific audience.
Consistency — a realistic cadence the business can sustain.
Right audience — content that speaks to the people most likely to buy.
Clear measurement — goals give you something to check against.
Efficient work — no more guessing, no more wasted hours.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who sets up her stall anywhere, sells anything, and changes her prices
every hour will struggle. A trader who chooses the right corner, sells what the people passing want, keeps her prices clear and
her stall open at the same time every day — that trader eats. Strategy is choosing the right corner and the right product before
you open the stall.
Important limitation / misconception. Strategy is not a guarantee of success. A good strategy does not make a bad product
sell. But a good strategy makes a good product visible to the right people — and that is the manager's job.
F.2 Researching the audience — who are we talking to?
The idea. The audience is the specific group of people most likely to buy from the client. Strategy starts by understanding them
— not guessing, not assuming "everyone."
Plain-English definition. Audience research is finding out who your client's real customers are, what they want, what stops
them from buying, and where they spend time online.
Why it exists. A page that tries to speak to everyone speaks to nobody. A jollof video aimed at "everyone" will not connect with
a student looking for a cheap lunch, a mother ordering for a family, or a professional catering an event. But a page that knows
its audience can speak directly to each group — and that is what gets orders.
Free research methods (no tools, no budget):
1. Ask the client. The owner knows their customers. Ask: "Who buys most often? What do they usually order? How old are
they? Where do they live? What do they complain about?" Write down the answers.
2. Ask the customers. If the client has a WhatsApp chat list or regulars, ask a few: "Why do you order from us? What do you
wish we did better? Where do you usually find food online?"
3. Observe the platform. Search the client's category on Instagram or Facebook. Look at the pages that are doing well. Who
comments? What do they ask for? What do they complain about?
4. Check the client's WhatsApp. The order history is a goldmine. What is ordered most? At what times? From which areas?
5. Use free platform search. Type "Port Harcourt food" or "Rumuola restaurant" into Instagram or Facebook search. See what
comes up, who posts it, and who engages.
The audience profile — a simple template. Once you have answers, write a profile. It does not need to be long — one page
is enough. The key is that it names a real obstacle, not "everyone."

cannot find our menu online." The obstacle — they do not know we deliver — is what the content will solve.
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor's waiting room. A doctor who treats "everyone" without asking what is wrong will give
the same pill to every patient. A good doctor asks questions first — where does it hurt, how long, what have you tried — and
then treats the actual problem. Audience research is the manager asking questions before prescribing content.
F.3 Setting measurable goals — what are we trying to achieve?
The idea. A goal is a specific, measurable result the client wants to achieve. Goals give the manager something to aim at and
something to measure against.
Plain-English definition. A goal is a clear target — not "get more followers" but "get 30 WhatsApp orders in 30 days."
Why it exists. Without a goal, a manager cannot know if their work is working. "Post more" is not a goal — it is an activity.
"Get 30 WhatsApp orders in 30 days" is a goal — it is specific, measurable and time-bound. Goals turn social media from a
guessing game into a job with results.
The SMART goal format. A good goal is SMART:
Letter Meaning Example
S — Specific Clear and precise, not vague "Get WhatsApp orders" not "get more customers"
M — Measurable You can count it "30 orders" not "more orders"
A — Achievable Realistic for a small business 30 orders in 30 days is realistic for a busy kitchen
R — Relevant Connected to the real business goal Orders are the business goal — not followers
T — Time-bound Has a deadline "In 30 days" not "someday"
Worked example — two goals for Taste of Rivers Kitchen:
1. Goal 1 (orders): "Get 30 WhatsApp orders from social media in the next 30 days."
Specific: WhatsApp orders from social media.
Measurable: 30 orders.
Achievable: The kitchen already gets word-of-mouth orders; 30 from social media in a month is realistic.
Relevant: Orders are the business.
Time-bound: 30 days.
2. Goal 2 (awareness): "Reach 5,000 unique people on Instagram and Facebook in the next 30 days."
Specific: unique people reached on Instagram and Facebook.
Measurable: 5,000.
Achievable: With 3 posts a week and consistent engagement, 5,000 reach is realistic for a small page.
Relevant: Awareness supports orders — people cannot order from a page they have never seen.
Time-bound: 30 days.
The key lesson — connect every post to a goal. A post that does not serve a goal is a waste. Before posting, ask: "Does this
post move us toward Goal 1 or Goal 2?" If the answer is no, do not post it — or change it so it does.
Common misconception. "Followers are a goal." No — followers are a metric, not a goal. A goal is a business result: orders,
bookings, calls, visits. Followers matter only if they lead to the business result. A page with 10,000 followers and no orders is
failing; a page with 500 followers and 30 orders a month is succeeding.
F.4 Choosing platforms — where do these people spend time?
The idea. A manager does not need to be on every platform. They need to choose one or two platforms where the client's
audience actually spends time — and they need to be able to explain why.
Plain-English definition. Platform choice is deciding which social media platform(s) to use for a client, based on where the
audience is and what the goal is — not on what is trendy.
Why it exists. A small business cannot be everywhere at once. A manager who tries to run Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, X and
LinkedIn for a small client will do all of them badly. A manager who chooses one or two and does them well will get better
results with less stress.
The platform choice process — three questions:
1. Where does the audience spend time? If the client's customers are students and young professionals, Instagram and
TikTok. If they are parents and older customers, Facebook. If they are professionals and recruiters, LinkedIn.
2. What format does the content need? If the business is visual — food, fashion, beauty — Instagram or TikTok. If it is
community and events — Facebook. If it is text and conversation — X.
3. What can the business realistically sustain? A kitchen with one phone and one person cannot make TikTok videos every
day. It can post photos and short reels three times a week. Choose what is sustainable.

Common misconception. "We need to be on TikTok because it is trendy." Maybe — but only if the audience is there and the
business can sustain it. A trendy platform that does not fit the audience or the capacity is a waste of time.
F.5 Scanning competitors — what can we learn?
The idea. A competitor scan is a quick, honest look at three businesses similar to the client's — what they post, how often,
what works and what does not. The goal is to learn, not to copy.
Plain-English definition. A competitor scan is studying three similar businesses to see what they do well, where they are weak,
and how the client can do better.
Why it exists. Competitors are free research. They show you what the audience responds to, what content formats work, and
where the gaps are — the things they do not do that the client can do better.
The three-competitor scan process:
1. Choose three competitors. Pick businesses that are similar to the client — same category, same area, same audience. Not
the biggest brand in Nigeria — a realistic local competitor.
2. Look at their last 10–15 posts. What do they post? How often? What format — photos, videos, text?
3. Note what works. Which posts get the most likes, comments and shares? What do people say in the comments?
4. Note what does not work. Which posts get little response? What do they never post? What do customers complain about?
5. Extract two lessons. What can the client do better? What gap can the client fill?

F.6 Writing a positioning statement — who are we, and why us?
The idea. A positioning statement is one sentence that says who the client serves, what they offer and why they are different. It
is the clearest possible answer to "why should someone choose this business?"
Plain-English definition. A positioning statement is a single sentence that captures what makes the client the right choice for
their specific audience.
Why it exists. A positioning statement keeps the manager focused. Every post, every caption, every decision can be checked
against it: "Does this fit who we are and why we are different?" If not, it does not belong on the page.
The positioning-statement template:
"For [audience], [business] is the [category] that [benefit] because [reason]."
Worked example — Taste of Rivers Kitchen:
"For students and young professionals in Port Harcourt, Taste of Rivers Kitchen is the local meals spot that delivers fresh,
hot jollof and small chops to your door in 45 minutes because we cook every morning and deliver ourselves."
Break it down:
Audience: students and young professionals in Port Harcourt.
Category: local meals spot.
Benefit: delivers fresh, hot jollof and small chops to your door in 45 minutes.
Reason: we cook every morning and deliver ourselves.
The key lesson — be specific. A weak positioning statement says "we sell good food." A strong one says "we deliver fresh, hot
jollof to your door in 45 minutes because we cook every morning." The specific version tells the customer exactly why to choose
you — and it gives the manager something to post about.
Real-life comparison. Think of a bus conductor who calls out "Ojuelegba! Ojuelegba! Ojuelegba!" — that is not positioning. A
conductor who calls out "Ojuelegba! Last bus! Air-conditioned! Leaving now!" — that is positioning. He tells you the
destination, the benefit and the urgency. A positioning statement does the same for the client.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Strategy comes before posting — thinking before posting is the difference between a real business page and a personal
diary.
Audience research finds out who the real customers are, what they want, what stops them and where they spend time
online — using free methods: asking the client, asking customers, observing the platform, checking WhatsApp and using
free search.
A good audience profile names a real obstacle — not "everyone."

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama Put's Rice and Beans is a small food business in Diobu, Port Harcourt. It sells rice, beans, plantain and fish —
a full plate for ₦1,200. The owner, Mama Put, is a excellent cook but she is not online. Her daughter created an Instagram page
two years ago and posted a few times, then stopped. The page has 200 followers — mostly family. Mama Put gets orders by
word of mouth and a regular customer who calls to place bulk orders for events. She wants more customers — especially
students and office workers near Diobu. She can pay ₦15,000 a month. She has never heard of "strategy" and does not know
what a "content pillar" is.
Relevant facts.
Mama Put's food is genuinely good — her rice and beans sell out by 2 p.m. most days.
The Instagram page has 200 followers, no posts in eight months, and the bio says "We sell food."
Mama Put's daughter posted a few blurry photos with no prices and no order information.
The business is near a busy market and several offices — students and office workers pass by every day.
Mama Put does not have a smartphone — her daughter has one and helps with the page.
Decision point. A new social media manager has been hired. What should the manager do first — and what should they avoid?
Possible approaches.
1. Post immediately — "let us start posting today, we will figure it out as we go."
2. Buy followers first — "let us get the page to look popular, then post."
3. Build a strategy first — research the audience, set goals, choose platforms, scan competitors and write a positioning
statement — then post.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — strategy first. The manager should:
Research the audience: Who buys from Mama Put? Students and office workers near Diobu. What do they want? A fast,
affordable, filling lunch. What stops them? They do not know the menu, the price or that the business is there.
Set two SMART goals: Get 20 new customers from social media in 30 days; reach 2,000 unique people on Instagram in 30
days.
Choose platforms: Instagram (primary) — because students and office workers are there, and food is visual. Facebook
(secondary) — because some office workers and older customers are there.
Scan competitors: Look at three other food businesses near Diobu. What do they post? What works? What can Mama Put
do differently?
Write a positioning statement: "For students and office workers in Diobu, Mama Put's Rice and Beans is the food spot that
serves a full, hot plate of rice and beans for ₦1,200 because we cook fresh every morning and we are right here in Diobu."
Then post: Three times a week — a clear photo of the rice and beans with the price, a short video of the food being served,
a "how to order" graphic with the phone number.
What the manager should avoid:
Posting randomly — no plan, no goal, no consistency.
Buying followers — fake followers bring no customers and break trust.
Using jargon with Mama Put — she does not know "content pillars" or "engagement rate." The manager must explain in
plain language.
Promising results — "I will get you 1,000 followers" is a lie. "I will post consistently, engage daily, and show you real
numbers" is honest.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is fast but wastes time — posting without a plan brings no results and frustrates the client.
Approach 2 is cheap but destructive — fake followers make the page look big while bringing zero customers.
Approach 3 takes a few hours of work before the first post, but it sets up a month of focused, effective content — and it
gives the manager a way to prove the results.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional does not guess. A professional asks questions, writes a plan, and then executes. That
is why a professional can charge ₦15,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Skipping strategy and posting immediately
Symptoms: Learner wants to post before researching the audience or setting goals. Likely cause: They think strategy is a waste
of time — "just post and see what happens." How to investigate: Ask: "Who are you posting for? What goal does this post
serve?" Corrective steps: Walk through the strategy process — audience, goals, platforms, competitors, positioning. Show that
a few hours of planning saves days of wasted posting. How to confirm resolved: Learner can explain why strategy comes
before posting. Prevention: Always ask "what is the goal?" before "what should we post?"
K.2 Writing "everyone" as the audience
Symptoms: Audience profile says "everyone in Port Harcourt" or "all Nigerians." Likely cause: The learner has not thought
about who actually buys from the client. How to investigate: Ask: "Who walks into the shop? Who calls to order? What do they
have in common?" Corrective steps: Use the five-row profile template — who, where, what they want, what stops them,
where they spend time online. Name a real obstacle. How to confirm resolved: The profile names a specific group and a
specific obstacle. Prevention: Remind the class — a page that speaks to everyone speaks to nobody.
K.3 Setting vague goals
Symptoms: Goals say "get more followers" or "increase engagement." Likely cause: The learner does not know how to make a
goal measurable. How to investigate: Ask: "How will you know if you achieved it? What number? By when?" Corrective steps:
Use the SMART format — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. Turn "get more followers" into "reach 5,000
unique people in 30 days." How to confirm resolved: Each goal has a number and a deadline. Prevention: Check every goal
against SMART before accepting it.
K.4 Choosing platforms by trend, not by audience
Symptoms: Learner chooses TikTok because "it is trendy" even when the audience is not there. Likely cause: They think more
platforms = more results. How to investigate: Ask: "Where does the client's customer spend time? Can the business sustain this
platform?" Corrective steps: Use the three-question process — where is the audience, what format does the content need,
what can the business sustain. How to confirm resolved: The platform choice has written reasons tied to the audience and the
goal. Prevention: Always ask "why this platform?" before "should we be on it?"
K.5 Copying competitors instead of learning from them
Symptoms: Learner copies a competitor's caption, photo style or content idea. Likely cause: They think copying is the same as
learning. How to investigate: Ask: "Did you change anything? Is this original content?" Corrective steps: Explain the difference
— we scan to learn what works and where the gaps are, but the client's content must be original. How to confirm resolved:
The learner can explain what they learned and what they will do differently. Prevention: Remind the class — we observe, we
learn, we improve our own work. We never copy.
K.6 Writing a vague positioning statement
Symptoms: Positioning statement says "we sell good food" or "we are the best." Likely cause: The learner has not thought
about what makes the client different. How to investigate: Ask: "Why should someone choose this business over the one next
door?" Corrective steps: Use the template — "For [audience], [business] is the [category] that [benefit] because [reason]."
Make it specific. How to confirm resolved: The statement names the audience, the benefit and the reason. Prevention: A weak
positioning statement is a sign the learner does not yet understand the client — go back to the audience profile.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

A7. False. A competitor scan means studying what competitors do to learn what works and where the gaps are — never to
copy. The client's content must be original.
A8. "For [audience], [business] is the [category] that [benefit] because [reason]."

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner explains why strategy comes before posting — not just "it is important."
Learner writes an audience profile that names a real obstacle — not "everyone."
Learner sets SMART goals and checks them against all five letters.
Learner chooses platforms with written reasons tied to the audience and the goal.
Learner runs a competitor scan and extracts lessons — not copies.
Learner writes a positioning statement with all four parts.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Strategy is a waste of time — just post." → No — strategy gives every post a reason to exist.
"Everyone is the audience." → A page that speaks to everyone speaks to nobody.
"Followers are a goal." → Followers are a metric; orders, bookings and calls are goals.
"More platforms = more results." → Choose one or two with reasons.
"Copying competitors is learning." → We scan to learn what works — never to copy.
"A positioning statement is just a slogan." → It is a decision-making tool — every post is checked against it.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Audience obstacle drill: give five weak audience profiles ("everyone," "all Nigerians," "people who love food") and ask the
learner to rewrite each with a specific group and a real obstacle.
2. SMART goal drill: give five vague goals ("get more followers," "increase engagement," "be the best") and ask the learner to
rewrite each in SMART format.
3. Platform reason drill: give five platform choices with no reasons and ask the learner to write a reason for each — or
explain why the platform does not fit.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed strategy sheet — audience profile and goals already filled in — and ask
the learner to complete the platform choice, competitor scan and positioning statement.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written — a complete strategy sheet for a new client.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a short "strategy defence" — three sentences explaining to a skeptical client
why each part of the strategy (audience, goals, platforms, competitors, positioning) matters and how it will bring results.
The defence must be in plain language, not jargon.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners completing a full strategy sheet (Sections H and the assignment). If the room is slow,
shorten the competitor scan demonstration (G.4) and protect the guided practical — the ability to build a strategy is the core
skill. If the room is fast, extend the case study discussion and let learners present their strategy sheets to the class.
