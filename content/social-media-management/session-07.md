# Analytics and Client Reporting

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Grow and Report

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with analytics)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, build a strategy, design a content calendar, create content, manage a community, schedule posts and draft a working agreement. Basic smartphone and spreadsheet use assumed. No prior analytics or reporting experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 6 we learned to schedule posts and run the workflow. Today we learn to measure whether any of it is working. We cover the key metrics — reach, engagement rate, saves, shares, clicks and growth — and learn to produce a one-page monthly report with real numbers, screenshots, insights and a recommendation. That report becomes the capstone's reporting template.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the sample metrics data (Section G) prepared as a printed sheet or on screen; the one-page report template (Section F.4) printed per learner; the metrics worksheet printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This is the session where learners discover that numbers are not scary — they are the proof of their work. Many learners will say "I am not good with numbers" — your job is to show them that reading six simple metrics and writing a one-page report is a skill anyone can learn. Keep the mood practical and confident. Emphasise from the start: honest reporting is non-negotiable. A manager who fakes numbers will be caught and fired.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Identify the key metrics — reach, engagement rate, saves, shares, clicks and follower growth — and explain what each
one means.
2. Read basic analytics from Instagram and Facebook and pull the right numbers for a monthly report.
3. Calculate an engagement rate correctly (engagement ÷ reach × 100).
4. Produce a one-page monthly report with numbers against goals, top content, what worked, what did not, and a next-
month recommendation.
5. Present results honestly — explaining both good and bad months — and never inflate numbers.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two fictional monthly reports for the same client — Taste of Rivers Kitchen. Both
cover the same month. Do not explain which is "better" yet.
Report A — "The Inflated Report."
"This month was amazing! We reached 50,000 people, got 5,000 likes, and the page grew by 2,000 followers. The jollof video
went viral! Overall: excellent month!"
Report B — "The Honest Report."
"This month we reached 8,400 people (goal: 5,000 — we exceeded it). We got 340 likes, 85 comments, 120 saves and 45
shares. 28 people clicked the WhatsApp link, and 12 became orders (goal: 30 — we did not reach it). The jollof video
worked best — 1,200 views and 18 DMs. The plantain graphic did not work — 150 views and 2 DMs. Next month: post
more jollof content, try a different graphic style for the plantain, and add a 'how to order' graphic to every post. What we
will keep: the Friday posting time, the warm reply tone, and the customer photo pillar."
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two reports. Report A sounds exciting — 'amazing,' 'viral,' 'excellent.' But ask yourself: can Chef Nkechi
check those numbers? Can she verify that you reached 50,000 people? Can she see the 2,000 new followers? No — because
those numbers are made up. Report B is less exciting — but every number is real, every claim can be checked, and there is
a clear plan for next month. Report B is what a professional manager sends. Report B is what builds trust. Today you learn
to write Report B."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at Report B and say "it has real numbers," "it explains what worked," "it tells you
what to do next." Accept all answers. Draw out the difference: honest and useful vs. inflated and vague.
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"Report B is not luck. It is built from six simple metrics — reach, engagement rate, saves, shares, clicks and growth. You do
not need to be a mathematician to read them. You need to know what each one means, how to find it, and how to write it
down honestly. Today you learn to do that. By the end of this session, you will be able to produce a one-page report that
makes a client trust you — and that proves your work."
Why this opener works: It uses a side-by-side comparison that makes the value of honest reporting immediate and emotional.
Learners see that inflated numbers are dangerous and honest numbers are powerful — and they want to learn the skill.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core tasks of a social media manager?"
Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners forget "reporting." Clarify: "Today is all about the reporting task — analytics and the monthly
report."
Question 2: "What is a SMART goal?"
Expected answer: A goal that is Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.
Common gap: learners remember some letters but not all. Write them on the board quickly.
Question 3: "What is a content calendar?"
Expected answer: A plan that says what to post, when, on which platform, in which format, with which caption idea.
Common gap: learners say "a list of posts." Clarify: "It is a table — one row per post, with a pillar, format, caption idea and
asset."
Question 4: "What is a working agreement?"
Expected answer: A one-page document covering scope, deliverables, payment, revisions, account ownership,
communication and termination.
Common gap: learners say "a contract." Clarify: "It is simpler than a contract — it is a professional understanding in
writing."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In Session 6 we learned to schedule and run the workflow. Today we learn to measure whether any of it is working. We will
cover the six key metrics, learn to read them from Instagram and Facebook, calculate an engagement rate, and produce a
one-page monthly report. That report is the most important document a manager produces — because it is the proof that
the work is worth paying for."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 2, spend 3 extra minutes reviewing SMART with a quick example before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why measurement matters — and why honesty is non-negotiable
The idea. Measurement is the process of tracking numbers to see if the work is achieving the goals. Reporting is telling the
client what the numbers say — honestly.
Plain-English definition. Measurement is checking the numbers. Reporting is writing them down and explaining what they
mean.
Why it exists. A manager who does not measure is working blind. They cannot tell if a post worked, if a strategy is right, or if
the client is getting value. A manager who does not report is invisible — the client pays ₦35,000 a month and has no idea what
they are getting. Measurement and reporting are how a manager proves their value.
Why honesty is non-negotiable:
The client can check. Instagram and Facebook show the owner their own insights. If the manager says "50,000 reach" and
the owner sees 8,400, the trust is destroyed.
A bad month is information, not failure. A manager who hides a bad month cannot fix it. A manager who reports it
honestly — and proposes a plan — builds trust.
Inflated numbers are fraud. A manager who fakes a report is not just unprofessional — they are dishonest. That is a
character issue, not a skill issue.
Say this to the class:
"Let me be very clear about something. In this course, and in this profession, honest reporting is not optional. If the month
was bad, you say so — and you explain what you will do differently. If the engagement rate is 3%, you write 3% — you
do not round it up to 30%. If a post got 10 views, you write 10 — you do not screenshot someone else's results. A client who
discovers you have lied about numbers will fire you, and they will tell other business owners. One fake report can end a
career. Real numbers, honest insights — that is the job."
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor's report. A doctor who tells a patient "you are perfectly healthy" when the blood test
shows a problem is dangerous. A doctor who says "your cholesterol is a little high — here is what we will do" is trustworthy. A
manager is the doctor, the numbers are the blood test, and the report is the diagnosis.

saves, shares, clicks and follower growth.

what is not.

the ones connected to the business goal.
F.3 Calculating an engagement rate correctly
The idea. The engagement rate is the percentage of people who saw a post and then interacted with it. It is the single most
important metric for judging content quality.
Plain-English definition. Engagement rate = (likes + comments + saves + shares) ÷ reach × 100.
Why it exists. Reach tells you how many people saw the content. Engagement rate tells you how many of them cared. A post
with 10,000 reach and 10 likes is failing. A post with 1,000 reach and 100 likes is succeeding.
Worked example — calculating engagement rate:
Reach: 8,400
Likes: 340
Comments: 85
Saves: 120
Shares: 45
Engagement rate = (340 + 85 + 120 + 45) ÷ 8,400 × 100 = 590 ÷ 8,400 × 100 = 7.02%
Say: "7%. That is a healthy engagement rate for a small page. It means 7 out of every 100 people who saw our content interacted
with it. That is good — and it tells Chef Nkechi that the content is connecting."
The key lesson — always calculate, never guess. A manager who says "engagement is good" without calculating it is
guessing. A manager who writes "engagement rate: 7%" is reporting. The calculation takes 30 seconds and makes the report
professional.
Common misconception. "Engagement rate = likes ÷ followers." No — it is total interactions ÷ reach. Followers are not the
same as reach. A post can reach more people than follow it has (through shares and explore) or fewer (if the algorithm shows it
to a small percentage of followers).
F.4 The one-page monthly report — structure and content
The idea. A monthly report is a one-page document that tells the client what happened, what worked, what did not and what
happens next. It is the manager's most important deliverable.
Plain-English definition. A monthly report is a simple one-page summary with real numbers, honest insights and a clear
recommendation.
Why it exists. The client pays for results. The report is the proof — or the honest explanation. It is what separates a professional
from a poster.
The six parts of a one-page report:
What it says Example
1. Numbers against goals
The goal, the actual number,
and whether it was met.
"Goal: 30 WhatsApp orders. Actual: 12. Not met."
2. Top content
The best-performing posts and
why they worked.
"The jollof video worked best — 1,200 views and 18
DMs."
What it says Example
3. What worked
The content, formats and
strategies that succeeded.
"Behind-the-scenes videos and menu photos with
prices."
4. What did not work
The content and strategies that
failed — honestly.
"The plantain graphic did not work — 150 views
and 2 DMs."
5. Insights
Why things worked or did not
— the story behind the
numbers.
"People want to see the food being cooked and the
price clearly. The plantain graphic was dark and had
no price."
6. Next-month recommendation
What the manager will do
differently next month.
"Post more jollof content, brighten the plantain
graphic, add a 'how to order' graphic to every post."
Worked example — a complete one-page report for Taste of Rivers Kitchen:
Monthly Report — Taste of Rivers Kitchen, Month 1
1. Numbers against goals:
Goal: 30 WhatsApp orders. Actual: 12. Not met.
Goal: Reach 5,000 people. Actual: 8,400. Exceeded.
Engagement rate: 7% (healthy).
New followers: +85 (real, not bought).
2. Top content:
Jollof cooking video: 1,200 views, 18 DMs, 3 shares.
Menu photo with prices: 800 views, 12 DMs, 45 saves.
Customer photo: 600 views, 8 DMs, 12 shares.
3. What worked:
Behind-the-scenes videos — people want to see the food being cooked.
Menu photos with clear prices — people save them and order from them.
Customer photos — they build trust.
4. What did not work:
Plantain graphic — dark photo, no price visible. 150 views, 2 DMs.
"Good morning" text posts — no image, no value. 80 views, 0 DMs.
5. Insights:
People respond to clear food photos with visible prices and a way to order.
People ignore text-only posts and dark images.
The WhatsApp order link works, but only 12 of 28 clickers became orders — the ordering process may be unclear.
6. Next-month recommendation:
Post more jollof and menu content (what works).
Brighten all food photos and always include the price.
Add a "how to order" graphic to every post.
Stop text-only "good morning" posts.
Review the WhatsApp ordering process with Chef Nkechi to make it easier.
The key lesson — insights explain why, not just what. "The plantain graphic did not work" is what. "The plantain graphic was
dark and had no price visible" is why. The why is what makes the report useful — it tells the client what to change.
Real-life comparison. Think of a school report card. A report card that says "Mathematics: C" is not useful. A report card that
says "Mathematics: C — needs to practise fractions and show working" is useful. A monthly report is the manager's report card
for the client's business.
F.5 Presenting results honestly — the good, the bad and the plan
The idea. A manager presents results every month — the good months and the bad months. The skill is presenting both with
the same honesty and professionalism.
Plain-English definition. Presenting results means explaining what happened, owning what did not, and proposing what comes
next — calmly and clearly.
Why it exists. A manager who only celebrates good months looks like they are hiding something. A manager who only reports
bad months looks like they are failing. A manager who reports both honestly — with insights and a plan — looks like a
professional.
The presentation formula — the three parts:
1. The good. "Here is what worked and why." — Celebrate the wins, but explain them.
2. The bad. "Here is what did not work and why." — Own the failures, but explain them.
3. The plan. "Here is what we will do differently next month." — Propose a clear, specific action.
Worked example — presenting a mixed month:
"This month was mixed. We exceeded our reach goal — 8,400 people against a target of 5,000 — and engagement was
healthy at 7%. The jollof video and the menu photos worked well. But we did not reach our orders goal — 12 against a
target of 30 — and the plantain graphic did not work. The issue is that people are clicking but not ordering, so next month
we will make the ordering process clearer on every post and review the WhatsApp process with you. I am confident we will
hit the orders target next month."
The key lesson — a bad month is not a disaster; it is information. A manager who reports a bad month honestly — with
insights and a plan — builds more trust than a manager who reports a good month with inflated numbers. The client is not
paying for perfection; they are paying for honesty and improvement.
Real-life comparison. Think of a football coach. A coach whose team loses and says "the referee was bad, the pitch was bad,
the players were tired" loses the team's respect. A coach whose team loses and says "we lost because we defended badly —
here is what we will practise this week" earns respect. A manager is the coach, the numbers are the scoreline, and the report is
the post-match analysis.
F.6 Screenshots and Sheets — the tools of reporting
The idea. A manager uses two simple tools for reporting: screenshots (to show the client the actual numbers) and Google
Sheets (to track the numbers over time).
Plain-English definition. Screenshots are photos of the analytics page — proof that the numbers are real. Google Sheets is a
free spreadsheet where the manager tracks the numbers month by month.
Why they exist. A report with numbers but no screenshots is a claim. A report with numbers and screenshots is proof. A Google
Sheet that tracks the numbers over time shows trends — is the page growing, stagnating or declining?
Worked example — the reporting toolkit:
1. Screenshots. At the end of each month, take screenshots of the Instagram and Facebook insights pages. Attach them to
the report or share them in a Google Drive folder.
2. Google Sheet. Create a simple table with one row per month and one column per metric: Month / Reach / Engagement
rate / Saves / Shares / Clicks / Followers / Orders.
3. The trend. After three months, the Sheet shows the trend — is reach growing? Is engagement rising? Is the orders goal
getting closer?
The key lesson — screenshots prove, Sheets show trends. A one-page report with screenshots is professional. A Google
Sheet that tracks the numbers over time is invaluable — it shows the client that the work is building something.
Real-life comparison. Think of a bank statement. A bank statement with one month's balance is a snapshot. A bank statement
with six months of balances is a story — it shows whether you are saving or spending. A Google Sheet is the manager's bank
statement for the client's social media.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama J's Small Chops hired a social media manager three months ago. The manager posts regularly and replies to
comments — but the monthly reports are always the same: "This month was good. Lots of engagement. Keep going." Mama J
asks: "How many people did we reach? How many orders did we get? Which posts worked?" The manager cannot answer. They
do not take screenshots, do not track numbers and do not know how to calculate an engagement rate. Last month, Mama J
checked her own Instagram insights and found that the page had reached only 1,200 people — but the manager's report said
"great reach." Mama J feels misled. She is thinking of ending the relationship.
Relevant facts.
The manager posts regularly — that is genuine.
The manager replies to comments — that is genuine.
But the reports are vague and inflated — "good," "great," "lots of engagement" with no numbers.
The manager does not take screenshots, does not track numbers and cannot calculate an engagement rate.
Mama J checked her own insights and found the reach was 1,200 — not the "great reach" the manager reported.
Mama J feels misled and is thinking of ending the relationship.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should the manager do now?
Possible approaches.
1. Continue as before — "Mama J does not understand social media anyway."
2. Blame the platform — "Instagram is not showing our posts."
3. Fix the reporting — start tracking real numbers, take screenshots, calculate engagement rates and produce honest one-
page reports.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — fix the reporting. The manager should:
Start tracking the numbers — reach, engagement, saves, shares, clicks, orders — every month.
Take screenshots of the insights pages — proof that the numbers are real.
Calculate the engagement rate — and include it in every report.
Produce a one-page report — numbers against goals, top content, what worked, what did not, insights, next-month
recommendation.
Present honestly — the good, the bad and the plan.
What the manager should have done from the start:
Used Meta Business Suite — to see the numbers easily.
Created a Google Sheet — to track the numbers month by month.
Produced a real report — not a vague "good month" message.
Been honest — when the reach was 1,200, say 1,200 — and explain what will change.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is passive and dishonest — the manager will lose Mama J.
Approach 2 is defensive and unprofessional — Mama J knows the difference between a bad month and a lie.
Approach 3 takes a few hours of work, but it rebuilds trust — and it turns the manager from a "poster" into a professional.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional does not guess. A professional tracks, calculates, reports and presents honestly. That
is why a professional can charge ₦15,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Inflating numbers
Symptoms: The manager writes "great reach" or "amazing engagement" with no numbers. Likely cause: They do not track the
numbers — or they are embarrassed by the real ones. How to investigate: Ask: "What was the exact reach? What was the
engagement rate? Can you show me the screenshot?" Corrective steps: Track real numbers. Take screenshots. Calculate the
engagement rate. Report the actual figures. How to confirm resolved: The report has real numbers and screenshots.
Prevention: Honest reporting is non-negotiable — repeat this every session.
K.2 Not calculating the engagement rate
Symptoms: The manager says "engagement is good" without calculating it. Likely cause: They do not know the formula — or
they think it is too complicated. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the formula? Can you calculate it for this post?" Corrective
steps: Teach the formula — (likes + comments + saves + shares) ÷ reach × 100. Practise it. How to confirm resolved: The
manager can calculate the engagement rate correctly. Prevention: The calculation takes 30 seconds — make it a habit.
K.3 Hiding a bad month
Symptoms: The manager only reports good months — or makes excuses for bad ones. Likely cause: They think a bad month
is a failure. How to investigate: Ask: "What did not work this month? Why?" Corrective steps: A bad month is information, not
failure. Report it honestly — with insights and a plan. How to confirm resolved: The report includes what did not work and a
next-month recommendation. Prevention: Honesty builds trust — a bad month reported well is better than a good month
reported falsely.
K.4 Writing vague reports
Symptoms: The report says "post more" or "engagement is good" with no specifics. Likely cause: The manager does not know
what a good report looks like. How to investigate: Ask: "What specifically will you do differently next month? Why did that post
work?" Corrective steps: Use the six-part structure — numbers against goals, top content, what worked, what did not, insights,
next-month recommendation. How to confirm resolved: The report has all six parts with specific details. Prevention: Insights
explain why — not just what.
K.5 Not taking screenshots
Symptoms: The report has numbers but no proof. Likely cause: The manager does not know that screenshots are standard
practice. How to investigate: Ask: "Can you show me the insights page? Where is the screenshot?" Corrective steps: Take
screenshots of the insights pages every month. Attach them to the report or share them in a Google Drive folder. How to
confirm resolved: The report includes screenshots. Prevention: Screenshots prove — make them a habit.
K.6 Focusing on followers instead of results
Symptoms: The manager celebrates follower growth but ignores orders, bookings or calls. Likely cause: They think followers
are the goal. How to investigate: Ask: "How many orders did we get? How many booking enquiries? Did we hit the goal?"
Corrective steps: Followers are a metric, not a goal. The goal is the business result — orders, bookings, calls. How to confirm
resolved: The report focuses on the business goal. Prevention: Always connect the metrics to the client's goal.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
