# Scheduling, Tools and Client Workflow

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Create and Manage

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first hands-on tool setup)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, build a strategy, design a content calendar, create content, manage a community and build a reply bank. Basic smartphone and computer use assumed. No prior tool setup or client management experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 5 we learned to manage the community — replying, moderating and building a reply bank. Today we learn the tools and the workflow that make everything run smoothly: connecting Instagram and Facebook in Meta Business Suite, scheduling posts, using free schedulers for TikTok, X and LinkedIn, running a draft–review–approve workflow, onboarding a client and drafting a working agreement.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers with Meta Business Suite loaded and a test Instagram + Facebook connection ready; projector connected and tested; the working agreement template (Section F.5) printed per learner; the scheduling worksheet printed; the onboarding checklist printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available; academy Wi-Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This session is where the course becomes operational. Learners have been building skills for five sessions — today they learn the tools and the process that tie it all together. Many learners will be nervous about the tools — your job is to show them that Meta Business Suite, Google tools and free schedulers are simple once you know where to click. Walk slowly, step by step, and let learners follow on their own devices. The goal is not perfection today — it is confidence that they can do this on their own.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Connect Instagram and Facebook in Meta Business Suite and explain why a manager uses it.
2. Schedule three posts in Meta Business Suite or another free tool, with the correct format, caption and time.
3. Run a draft → review → approve → schedule workflow that keeps the client involved and mistakes out.
4. Onboard a client professionally — collecting access, information and expectations safely and honestly.
5. Draft a one-page working agreement covering scope, deliverables, payment, revisions and account ownership.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two fictional "week in the life" scenarios for the same manager — one using tools
and a workflow, one doing everything manually. Do not explain which is "better" yet.
Scenario A — "The Organised Manager."
Monday: Opens Meta Business Suite. Sees three posts already scheduled for the week — Monday, Wednesday, Friday, with
captions and images ready. Checks the inbox — five comments from last week, all already replied to. Spends 15 minutes
reviewing the week.
Wednesday: Checks the scheduled post — it goes live at 12 p.m. automatically. Replies to three new comments within an
hour.
Friday: Pulls the week's insights — reach, engagement, DMs. Fills in the monthly report. Sends it to the client with a
WhatsApp message: "Here is this week's report. The jollof video did best — 500 views and 12 DMs. Next week we post more
of that."
Scenario B — "The Chaotic Manager."
Monday: Realises no posts are scheduled. Rushes to write a caption and find a photo. Posts at 3 p.m. — no plan, no
approval. Forgets to post on Wednesday. Posts twice on Friday.
Wednesday: Opens Instagram — 15 unanswered comments. Replies to three, forgets the rest. Does not know how to check
insights.
Friday: Realises the client asked for a report two weeks ago. Rushes to find screenshots. Cannot find the numbers. Sends a
message: "The page is doing well. Lots of engagement." No specifics.
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two managers. Same job, same client, same week. Manager A is calm, consistent and professional —
because they use tools and a workflow. Manager B is stressed, inconsistent and unprofessional — because they do
everything manually and hope. Here is the secret: Manager A is not smarter or more talented. Manager A just knows three
things — what tools to use, how to schedule posts, and how to run a workflow that keeps the client involved. Today you
learn those three things. By the end of this session, you will be Manager A."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at Scenario A and say "they are organised," "they have a system," "they are not
stressed." Accept all answers. Draw out the difference: tools + workflow vs. chaos.
Then reveal the hook: Say:
"Manager A uses Meta Business Suite — a free tool that connects Instagram and Facebook, schedules posts and shows
the inbox in one place. They also use a simple workflow — draft, review, approve, schedule — so the client sees
everything before it goes live. These are not expensive tools or complicated systems. They are free and simple. But using
them is the difference between a professional and an amateur. Let us learn to use them."
Why this opener works: It uses a side-by-side comparison that makes the value of tools and workflow immediate and
emotional. Learners see that organisation is not a personality trait — it is a set of tools and a process they can learn.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core tasks of a social media manager?"
Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners forget "posting." Clarify: "Today we go deeper into the posting task — specifically, scheduling posts
in tools."
Question 2: "What is a content calendar?"
Expected answer: A plan that says what to post, when, on which platform, in which format, with which caption idea.
Common gap: learners say "a list of posts." Clarify: "It is a table — one row per post, with a pillar, format, caption idea and
asset."
Question 3: "What is a reply bank?"
Expected answer: A collection of prepared replies for the ten most common scenarios.
Common gap: learners say "a list of comments." Clarify: "It is a list of replies — for questions, orders, compliments,
complaints and spam."
Question 4: "What are the four rules of a good reply?"
Expected answer: Quick, warm, correct, action-oriented.
Common gap: learners say "be nice." Clarify: "Be specific — quick, warm, correct, action-oriented."
Bridge to today (say this):
"In the last three sessions we planned content, created it and managed the community. Today we learn the tools and the
workflow that make it all run smoothly. We will connect Instagram and Facebook in Meta Business Suite, schedule three
posts, run a draft–review–approve workflow, onboard a client and draft a working agreement. By the end of this
session, you will have the operational skills to run a client's social media professionally."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 2, spend 3 extra minutes reviewing the content calendar structure
before continuing.

F.1 Why tools matter — the manager's toolkit
The idea. A professional manager does not work from memory and hope. They use a small set of free tools that handle
scheduling, communication, storage and reporting. Knowing these tools is part of the job.
Plain-English definition. The manager's toolkit is the set of free tools a social media manager uses to schedule posts,
communicate with the client, store assets and produce reports.
Why it exists. A manager who tries to run a client's social media from their personal Instagram app will miss messages, forget
to post and lose track of what was agreed. The tools solve these problems — and they are all free.

Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who keeps all their stock in their head will forget what they have, lose

manager's tools are the notebook and the scale.
Important limitation / misconception. Tools do not replace skill. A manager who does not know what to post will schedule the
wrong content just as fast as they post it manually. The tools make the workflow efficient — the strategy and the content are
still the manager's job.
F.2 Meta Business Suite — the core tool
The idea. Meta Business Suite is Meta's free tool for managing Instagram and Facebook from one place. It is the most
important tool a manager learns in this course.
Plain-English definition. Meta Business Suite connects a client's Instagram and Facebook accounts so the manager can post to
both, reply to messages from both and see the numbers for both — from one dashboard.
Why it exists. Without it, the manager has to switch between the Instagram app and the Facebook app, post twice, reply twice
and check insights twice. With it, everything is in one place — faster, more consistent and more professional.
The four main features:
1. Connect accounts. Link the client's Instagram and Facebook pages to Meta Business Suite. (The client must give permission
— see Section F.4.)
2. Schedule posts. Write a caption, upload a photo or video, choose a date and time, and the post goes live automatically —
on both platforms if needed.
3. Unified inbox. See and reply to comments and DMs from Instagram and Facebook in one place.
4. Insights. See reach, engagement, clicks and follower growth — the numbers needed for the monthly report.

The key lesson — scheduling is setting it and forgetting it. A manager who schedules a week of posts on Monday does not
have to think about posting for the rest of the week. They can focus on engaging, creating and reporting.
Real-life comparison. Think of a timer on a rice cooker. A cook who sets the timer does not have to watch the pot — they
can do other things. Scheduling posts is setting the timer for the content.
F.3 Free schedulers for TikTok, X and LinkedIn
The idea. Meta Business Suite covers Instagram and Facebook — but not TikTok, X and LinkedIn. For those platforms, a
manager uses a free scheduler like Buffer or Later.
Plain-English definition. Buffer and Later are free tools that let a manager schedule posts for TikTok, X and LinkedIn — so all
platforms are covered.
Why it exists. A client who wants to be on four platforms needs a way to schedule all of them. Meta Business Suite covers two;
Buffer or Later covers the rest.
The free tier limits — and how to work within them:
Buffer free tier: Up to 3 channels, 10 scheduled posts per channel. Enough for one client on TikTok, X and LinkedIn.
Later free tier: Up to 1 social set, 30 scheduled posts per platform. Enough for one client on the three platforms.
Worked example — scheduling a TikTok post in Buffer:
1. Open Buffer.
2. Connect the TikTok account.
3. Click "Create Post."
4. Upload the 20-second jollof video.
5. Write the caption: "Fresh jollof — ₦1,500 a plate ðŸ ² #TasteOfRiversKitchen #PortHarcourtFood"
6. Choose the date and time: Wednesday, 5 p.m.
7. Click "Schedule."
8. The post goes live automatically at Wednesday 5 p.m.
The key lesson — free tiers are enough for one client. A manager with one client does not need a paid plan. The free tiers of
Meta Business Suite, Buffer and Later are sufficient. A manager with multiple clients may eventually upgrade — but not on day
one.
Common misconception. "I need a paid scheduler." No — the free tiers are enough for one or two clients. Start free, upgrade
only when the workload demands it.
F.4 The draft–review–approve–schedule workflow
The idea. A workflow is the process a manager follows to get a post from idea to live — with the client involved at the right
moment. The standard workflow is: draft → review → approve → schedule.
Plain-English definition. The approval workflow is a four-step process: the manager drafts the post, the client reviews it, the
client approves it (or requests changes), and the manager schedules it.
Why it exists. A manager who posts without approval risks publishing wrong prices, off-brand content or mistakes the client
did not see. A manager who waits for approval on everything is slow. The workflow balances speed and safety — the client sees
everything, but the manager keeps moving.

Worked example — the workflow for a Friday post:
Monday: The manager drafts the Friday post — caption, photo, Canva graphic. Sends it to Chef Nkechi on WhatsApp:
"This is Friday's post. Please check the price and wording. I'll schedule it for Friday 12 p.m. unless you tell me to change

Tuesday: Chef Nkechi replies: "Change ₦1,500 to ₦1,600 — I changed the price this week." The manager updates the

Friday 12 p.m.: The post goes live automatically. The manager checks that it posted correctly and replies to the first
comments.
The key lesson — the workflow protects both sides. The client is protected because they see everything before it goes live.
The manager is protected because they have written approval — if the client later says "I never saw that," the manager has the
message.
Real-life comparison. Think of a building inspector. A builder who builds without inspection may make expensive mistakes. An
inspection before the wall is closed catches problems early. The approval workflow is the content inspection.
Important limitation / misconception. The workflow does not mean the client controls every word. The manager is the
professional — they write the captions, design the graphics and make the decisions. The client reviews and approves — they
do not rewrite. A manager who lets the client rewrite everything is not managing; they are typing.
F.5 Client onboarding — the first week
The idea. Onboarding is the process of starting work with a new client — collecting access, information and expectations so
the manager can do the job properly. It sets the tone for the whole relationship.
Plain-English definition. Onboarding is the first week of a client relationship — where the manager collects what they need,
sets expectations and establishes how the two will work together.
Why it exists. A manager who starts without onboarding will miss passwords, post the wrong content, misunderstand the
client's goals and frustrate the client within a month. Onboarding prevents that — it is the foundation of a professional
relationship.

The key lesson — onboarding is a trust exercise. A client is handing over their business's online presence. The manager must

access will lose the client — and their reputation.
Worked example — the first week with a new client:
Day 1: Call or meet the client. Explain the process. Collect the onboarding checklist items.
Day 2: Connect the accounts in Meta Business Suite. Set up the brand assets in Canva.
Day 3: Write the strategy (audience, goals, platforms, pillars — from Sessions 2 and 3). Send it to the client for feedback.
Day 4: Build the first week's content calendar. Send it to the client for approval.
Day 5: Draft the first three posts. Send them for review. Schedule once approved.
Real-life comparison. Think of a new employee's first week. A good employer does not hand a new employee a desk and say
"get to work." They show them around, introduce the team, explain the rules and set expectations. Onboarding is the manager
doing that for themselves — professionally and clearly.
F.6 The working agreement — the rules of the relationship
The idea. A working agreement is a one-page document that says exactly what the manager will do, what the client will do,
how much it costs and how the two will work together. It is not a legal contract — it is a professional understanding in writing.
Plain-English definition. A working agreement is a simple document that covers scope, deliverables, payment, revisions and
account ownership — so both sides know what to expect.
Why it exists. A manager and client who work from a verbal agreement will disagree within a month — about prices, about
how many posts, about who owns the account. A working agreement prevents that by putting everything in writing before the
work starts.

Real-life comparison. Think of a rent agreement. A tenant and landlord who shake hands on a rent will disagree about repairs,
increases and notice periods. A written agreement prevents that. A working agreement is the rent agreement for social media
management.
Important limitation / misconception. A working agreement is not a substitute for trust — it is a foundation for trust. The
manager still has to deliver, communicate and be honest. The agreement just makes the expectations clear so both sides can
succeed.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ada's Ankara is a small fashion business in Port Harcourt. The owner, Ada, hired a social media manager three
months ago. The manager posts regularly — but there is no workflow. The manager writes captions and posts them
immediately, without Ada seeing them. The manager uses a personal Instagram account to post — Ada's account is not
connected to any business tool. There is no written agreement — they agreed on "₦20,000 a month" verbally. Last week, Ada
saw a post with the wrong price — ₦5,000 instead of ₦8,000. A customer ordered at the wrong price and demanded a refund.
Ada is frustrated. She does not know if she can trust the manager anymore.
Relevant facts.
The manager posts regularly — but without approval.
The manager uses a personal account — Ada's account is not connected to Meta Business Suite.
There is no written agreement — just a verbal understanding.
A wrong price was posted — a customer ordered at ₦5,000 instead of ₦8,000.
Ada had to refund the customer and apologise.
Ada does not know how many posts were scheduled, what the manager is paid, or who owns the content.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should Ada do now?
Possible approaches.
1. Fire the manager immediately — "they are unprofessional."
2. Continue as before — "it was just one mistake."
3. Fix the workflow — connect accounts, run an approval process and sign a written agreement.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — fix the workflow. Ada should:
Connect the accounts in Meta Business Suite — so the manager posts from the right account.
Run the approval workflow — the manager drafts, Ada reviews, Ada approves, the manager schedules.
Sign a written working agreement — scope, deliverables, payment, revisions, account ownership, communication,
termination.
Set clear expectations — what the manager will do, what they will not do, and how they will communicate.
What Ada should have done from the start:
Onboarded properly — collected access, information and expectations in the first week.
Used tools — connected accounts in Meta Business Suite.
Run the approval workflow — no post goes live without Ada seeing it.
Signed a working agreement — everything in writing before the work started.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is understandable but wasteful — the manager posts regularly, which is valuable. The problem is the process,
not the person.
Approach 2 is passive and risky — without a workflow, the same mistake will happen again.
Approach 3 takes a few hours of work, but it sets up a professional relationship where both sides know what to expect —
and it prevents the next mistake.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional does not rely on memory and hope. A professional uses tools, runs a workflow and
puts everything in writing. That is why a professional can charge ₦20,000 a month and a "poster" cannot.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Posting without approval
Symptoms: The manager posts without the client seeing it first. Likely cause: They think approval slows them down — or they
do not know the workflow. How to investigate: Ask: "When did the client last see a post before it went live?" Corrective steps:
Run the draft–review–approve–schedule workflow. No post goes live without written approval. How to confirm resolved:
The manager can describe the four steps and show an approval message. Prevention: The workflow protects both sides —
make it a habit from day one.
K.2 Using a personal account instead of the client's
Symptoms: The manager posts from their personal Instagram instead of the client's account. Likely cause: They have not
connected the client's accounts to Meta Business Suite. How to investigate: Ask: "Which account are you posting from? Is it
connected to Meta Business Suite?" Corrective steps: Connect the client's accounts in Meta Business Suite. Post from the
client's accounts only. How to confirm resolved: The manager can show the connected accounts. Prevention: The client's
accounts are the business — never post from a personal account.
K.3 Working without a written agreement
Symptoms: The manager and client have only a verbal understanding. Likely cause: They think a written agreement is too
formal — or they do not know what it should contain. How to investigate: Ask: "What was agreed? Can you both describe it
the same way?" Corrective steps: Draft a one-page working agreement — scope, deliverables, payment, revisions, account
ownership, communication, termination. How to confirm resolved: Both sides have signed or confirmed the agreement.
Prevention: A verbal agreement is a memory — a written agreement is a record.
K.4 Handling account access carelessly
Symptoms: The manager shares login details over text, stores them in an unsecured place, or does not return them when the
relationship ends. Likely cause: They do not understand that account access is a trust issue. How to investigate: Ask: "How did
the client share their password? How do you store it? What happens if the agreement ends?" Corrective steps: Handle access
securely — use a password manager or a secure note. Never share access with anyone else. Return all access promptly if the
agreement ends. How to confirm resolved: The manager can explain how they handle access securely. Prevention: Account
access is a privilege — treat it with care.
K.5 Not using a scheduler
Symptoms: The manager posts manually every day — opening the app, writing the caption, uploading the photo. Likely
cause: They do not know about scheduling — or they think it is complicated. How to investigate: Ask: "How long does it take
you to post each day? Have you tried Meta Business Suite's scheduling?" Corrective steps: Schedule posts in advance — a
week at a time. Use Meta Business Suite for Instagram and Facebook, Buffer or Later for other platforms. How to confirm
resolved: The manager has scheduled at least three posts in advance. Prevention: Scheduling saves time and keeps the page
consistent.
K.6 Not keeping records
Symptoms: The manager does not keep records of what was posted, what was agreed or what was paid. Likely cause: They
think memory is enough. How to investigate: Ask: "Can you show me what was posted last week? What was agreed? What was
paid?" Corrective steps: Keep a simple record — a Google Sheet with posts, approvals and payments. How to confirm
resolved: The manager can show a record of posts and payments. Prevention: Records protect both sides — make them a
habit.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
