# Community Management and Engagement

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Create and Manage

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with community management)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–4 — the learner can define social media management, list the five core tasks, build a strategy, design a content calendar, write a creative brief and create content. Everyday social media use assumed. No prior customer service or moderation experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 we created content — briefs, Canva posts, repurposing sets. Today we do the work that happens after the post goes live. We reply to comments and DMs quickly and warmly, moderate a community, handle negative comments without arguing, and build a ten reply bank with a moderation policy. That reply bank becomes part of the capstone's community-management guidelines.

**Preparation before class:** Projector connected and tested; the ten comment and DM scenarios (Section H) printed per learner; the moderation-policy template printed; the tone-of-voice worksheet printed; the Taste of Rivers Kitchen client brief available; a whiteboard for the live reply build; academy Wi Fi confirmed. Running client reminder. Taste of Rivers Kitchen — fictional small food business in Rumuola, Port Harcourt. Sells local meals (jollof, fried rice, small chops, plantain, fish) for pickup and delivery. Prices ₦1,500–₦8,000. Owner "Chef Nkechi" cooks with two assistants, takes orders on one WhatsApp line. Instagram and Facebook pages silent for three months. Wants consistent posting for 30 days and more WhatsApp orders — no advertising budget. Has been burned by someone who bought her fake followers. Instructor note on tone. This session is where learners discover that community management is not "just replying" — it is the skill that turns a page into a business. Many learners will say "I already know how to reply to comments" — your job is to show them that professional replies are different from personal replies: they are warm, correct, on-brand, prompt and never defensive. Keep the mood practical and role-play friendly. The scenario exercise is where learners will do their best work — protect that time.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why community management is a paid service and what happens when a business ignores its comments and DMs.
2. Define a brand's tone of voice and write replies that match it consistently.
3. Reply to comments and DMs quickly, warmly and usefully — using the brand's tone.
4. Apply a moderation rule — delete spam and abuse, keep legitimate criticism, block only real troublemakers.
5. Respond to a negative comment without arguing, insulting or deleting legitimate criticism.
6. Build a ten-reply bank covering common scenarios — questions, orders, compliments, complaints and spam.

care — no learner should feel attacked or humiliated.

Expected answer: Planning, creating, posting, engaging, reporting.
Common gap: learners forget "engaging." Clarify: "Today is all about the engaging task — community management."
Question 2: "What is a creative brief?"
Expected answer: A short document that says what a post should achieve — goal, audience, message, format, look, CTA.

Expected answer: Hook, value, CTA — plus relevant hashtags.
Common gap: learners say "words and hashtags." Clarify: "The three parts are hook, value and CTA."

Expected answer: Using one idea to make multiple posts for different formats and platforms.
Common gap: learners say "posting the same thing everywhere." Clarify: "No — repurposing is adapting the same idea to

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why community management is a paid service
The idea. Community management is the work of replying to comments and DMs, moderating conversations and keeping a
page's comment section healthy. It is one of the five core tasks — and one of the most valuable services a manager sells.
Plain-English definition. Community management is being present and responsive on a client's page — answering questions,
thanking customers, handling complaints and keeping the conversation professional.
Why it exists. A post is not the end of the job — it is the beginning. When a post goes live, customers respond. They ask
questions, place orders, give feedback and sometimes complain. A business that replies quickly and well turns those responses
into orders and loyalty. A business that does not reply loses customers to competitors who do.
What happens without community management:
Questions go unanswered — "Do you deliver to GRA?" sits for days. The customer orders from somewhere else.
Complaints go unhandled — a frustrated customer posts publicly, sees no response, and tells their friends the business
does not care.
Spam accumulates — scammers and bots fill the comment section with fake links and offers.
The page looks abandoned — even regular followers stop commenting because they know no one will reply.
The competitor wins — the business next door that replies within the hour gets the orders.
What happens with good community management:
Questions become orders — a quick reply turns "Do you deliver?" into "Yes — send us a DM."
Complaints become loyalty — a customer who is handled well often becomes a regular.
The comment section stays clean — spam is removed, abuse is blocked, and legitimate criticism is kept and answered.
The page looks alive — followers comment more because they know they will be heard.
The business grows — word of mouth and repeat customers are built one reply at a time.
Real-life comparison. Think of a shop attendant. A shop with no attendant — customers walk in, look around, ask questions
to no one, and leave. A shop with a good attendant — greets customers, answers questions, handles complaints, keeps the
shop tidy — that shop builds a loyal clientele. A social media manager is the shop attendant for the online shop.
Important limitation / misconception. Community management cannot fix a bad product or a dishonest business. If the food
is genuinely bad, no reply will save it. But for a good business, community management is the difference between a page that
looks alive and a page that looks dead.
F.2 Defining the brand's tone of voice
The idea. A brand's tone of voice is how it sounds when it speaks online — friendly, formal, playful, warm, professional. A
manager must define it and use it consistently, so every reply sounds like it comes from the same business.
Plain-English definition. Tone of voice is the personality of the brand's words. Is it warm and casual? Formal and respectful? Fun
and playful? The tone must match the business and its audience.
Why it exists. A brand that sounds different every day is confusing. One day formal, one day slangy, one day angry —
customers do not know what to expect. A consistent tone builds trust. Customers feel they are talking to the same business
every time.
How to define the tone — the three questions:
1. What is the business like in person? A warm, friendly kitchen has a warm, friendly tone. A luxury salon has a polished,
professional tone.
2. Who is the audience? Students respond to casual warmth. Professionals may expect more polish.
3. What does the brand never sound like? No brand sounds angry, sarcastic or dismissive.

F.3 Replying to comments and DMs
The idea. A reply is a mini-conversation — the manager's chance to answer a question, thank a customer, handle a complaint
or turn a comment into an order. Good replies are quick, warm, correct and on-brand.
Plain-English definition. A reply is a response to a comment or DM that matches the brand's tone, answers the question and
moves the conversation toward a result.
Why it exists. Every comment is a customer raising their hand. A good reply makes them feel heard and guides them to the
next step — an order, a DM, a visit. A bad reply — or no reply — makes them feel ignored.
The four rules of a good reply:
1. Quick. Reply within a few hours — same day if possible. A reply after three days feels like no reply.
2. Warm. Thank the customer, use their name if you know it, match the brand's tone.
3. Correct. Answer the actual question — the price, the delivery area, the opening hours. No guessing, no wrong
information.
4. Action-oriented. Move the conversation to the next step — "Send us a DM," "Call to order," "Visit us today."

F.4 Moderation — keeping the comment section healthy
The idea. Moderation is managing the comment section — removing what should not be there, keeping what should, and
blocking only the people who truly need to be blocked.
Plain-English definition. Moderation is keeping a page's comment section clean, respectful and useful — by removing spam
and abuse, keeping legitimate criticism and blocking real troublemakers.
Why it exists. A comment section with spam, abuse and fake links looks unprofessional and can drive customers away. A clean
comment section builds trust. Moderation is what keeps it healthy.

F.5 Handling negative comments without arguing
The idea. A negative comment is not an attack — it is a customer with a problem. A manager who argues publicly loses the
argument in front of everyone. A manager who responds calmly and professionally wins the room.
Plain-English definition. Handling a negative comment means responding without arguing, insulting or deleting legitimate
criticism — and moving the problem to a private channel where it can be solved.
Why it exists. A negative comment is public. Everyone can see it — not just the complainer, but every other customer reading
the comments. A bad reply (arguing, insulting, being dismissive) convinces everyone that the business is difficult. A good reply
(calm, apologetic, solution-focused) convinces everyone that the business cares.
The four-step response to a negative comment:
1. Acknowledge. "I'm really sorry to hear that." — Show that you have read and understood the problem.
2. Apologise (if it is warranted). "That is not the standard we want." — Do not make excuses.
3. Move to private. "Please send us a DM with your order details." — Take the problem off the public stage.
4. Fix it. Resolve the issue in the DM — a refund, a replacement, a discount. Then (with the customer's permission) update
the public thread: "We've sorted this out — thank you for letting us make it right."
Worked example — the cold food complaint:
Customer: "I ordered last week and the food was cold. Very disappointed. I won't order again." Bad reply: "That's not true.
Our food is always hot. You probably waited too long to eat it." (Arguing, defensive, dismissive — everyone sees this.)
Good reply: "I'm really sorry about that — cold food is not what we want for you. Please send us a DM with your order
details and we'll make it right. Thank you for telling us." (Calm, apologetic, solution-focused.)
The key lesson — you are not defending yourself; you are protecting the brand. A negative comment is not about you
personally. It is about the customer's experience. Your job is to solve the problem, not to win an argument. The moment you
argue, you have lost — even if you are right.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A customer says "this fish is not fresh." A bad trader argues: "Of course it is
fresh, are you blind?" A good trader says: "I'm sorry, let me get you a fresh one — and I'll add an extra piece for the trouble."
The good trader keeps the customer and wins everyone watching.
Important limitation / misconception. Not every complaint is legitimate. Some people are abusive, some are trolls, some are
scammers. The rule is: legitimate criticism gets a calm, solution-focused reply; abuse gets deleted and blocked. The difference is
whether the comment is raising a real problem or just trying to cause trouble.

compliments, complaints and spam — so the manager can respond quickly and consistently.
Why it exists. A manager who writes every reply from scratch will be slow and inconsistent. A reply bank means the manager
can find the right reply in seconds, adapt it to the specific comment and send it — quickly, warmly and on-brand.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama J's Small Chops is a small food business in Port Harcourt that sells small chops, meat pies and doughnuts. The
owner, Mama J, posts on Instagram — but she does not reply to comments. She thinks "the food will speak for itself." Her
comment section is full of unanswered questions: "Do you deliver to GRA?" "How much is the tray?" "What are your opening
hours?" She also has several spam comments — "Follow my page for cheap followers!" — that she does not know how to
remove. One frustrated customer posted: "I ordered and nobody even replied to my message. I went somewhere else." That
comment is still there, unanswered, three weeks later.
Relevant facts.
Mama J's small chops are genuinely good — they sell out every Saturday.
She has 1,500 Instagram followers but only gets about 10 orders a week.
Her comment section has 20+ unanswered questions.
She has 5 spam comments she does not know how to remove.
She lost at least 3 customers who posted complaints and got no reply.
She is willing to pay ₦15,000 a month for someone to manage her comments.
Decision point. A social media manager has been hired. What should the manager do first — and what should they avoid?
Possible approaches.
1. Post more often — "let us post every day, that will fix it."
2. Buy followers — "let us get the page to 5,000 followers, then orders will come."
3. Manage the community — reply to every comment, moderate the spam, handle complaints professionally and build a
reply bank.
Recommended solution. Approach 3 — manage the community. The manager should:
Reply to every comment — questions, orders, compliments — within a few hours.
Delete the spam — remove the "follow my page for cheap followers" comments.
Handle the complaints — acknowledge, apologise, move to DM, fix.
Build a reply bank — ten ready replies for the most common scenarios.
Write a moderation policy — clear rules for spam, abuse, criticism and trolling.
Set a response-time target — reply within 2 hours during business hours.
What the manager should avoid:
Arguing with the complaint — "I ordered and nobody replied" is a real problem. Arguing will make it worse.
Deleting the complaint — legitimate criticism should be kept and answered.
Ignoring the spam — spam makes the page look unprofessional.
Promising to reply instantly, 24/7 — a realistic target is a few hours during business hours.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is easy but ineffective — more posts will not fix a reply problem.
Approach 2 is cheap but destructive — fake followers bring no orders.
Approach 3 takes time and discipline, but it turns the comment section into a sales channel — and it builds the kind of
loyalty that word of mouth cannot buy.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a professional manager does — and it is what separates a professional from
someone who "just posts." A professional understands that the comment section is not a noticeboard — it is a shop floor.
Every comment is a customer standing in front of you. Reply well, and they buy. Ignore them, or reply badly, and they leave.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Replying slowly or not at all
Symptoms: Comments sit for days without a reply. Likely cause: The manager does not check the page regularly, or does not
understand that speed matters. How to investigate: Ask: "When did you last check the comments? How long did it take you to
reply?" Corrective steps: Set a daily routine — check comments at least twice a day (morning and evening). Reply within a few
hours. Use a reply bank to be faster. How to confirm resolved: The manager can explain the daily routine and the response-
time target. Prevention: Remind the class — a reply after three days feels like no reply.
K.2 Arguing with a negative comment
Symptoms: The manager writes "That's not true" or "You are wrong" in response to a complaint. Likely cause: They take the
complaint personally. How to investigate: Ask: "Are you defending yourself or protecting the brand? Who can see this reply?"
Corrective steps: Use the four-step response — acknowledge, apologise, move to private, fix. Never argue publicly. How to
confirm resolved: The reply is calm, apologetic and solution-focused. Prevention: The moment you argue, you have lost —
even if you are right.
K.3 Deleting legitimate criticism
Symptoms: The manager deletes every negative comment. Likely cause: They think negative comments are bad for the page.
How to investigate: Ask: "Is this spam or abuse? No — it is a real problem. What should we do with it?" Corrective steps:
Keep legitimate criticism. Respond to it professionally. Use it to improve the business. How to confirm resolved: The complaint
is kept and answered calmly. Prevention: Delete spam and abuse only — never honest feedback.
K.4 Replying to spam
Symptoms: The manager replies to spam comments — "Stop spamming!" or "Who are you?" Likely cause: They want to
confront the spammer. How to investigate: Ask: "What happens when you reply to spam? Does it stop — or does it encourage
more?" Corrective steps: Delete spam. Do not reply. Replying to spam signals that the page is active and encourages more
spam. How to confirm resolved: Spam is deleted silently. Prevention: Spam is removed, not engaged with.
K.5 Using an inconsistent tone
Symptoms: One reply is formal, the next is slangy, the next is angry. Likely cause: The manager has not defined the brand's
tone of voice. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the brand's voice? Read your last three replies — do they sound like the same
business?" Corrective steps: Define the tone of voice — three questions: what is the business like, who is the audience, what
does the brand never sound like. Apply it to every reply. How to confirm resolved: All replies sound like the same brand.
Prevention: The tone of voice is the brand's personality, not the manager's mood.
K.6 Sharing private details publicly
Symptoms: The manager posts a customer's order details or phone number in the comments. Likely cause: They are trying to
be helpful — "Look, this customer ordered 4 plates." How to investigate: Ask: "Did the customer give permission for this
information to be public?" Corrective steps: Never share private details publicly. Move order details and personal information
to DM. How to confirm resolved: No private details in public comments. Prevention: Privacy is a trust rule — break it once
and the client loses confidence.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it — ask the learner to explain
their reasoning, not just their answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner explains why community management is a paid service — not just "replying."
Learner defines a tone of voice and writes replies that match it consistently.
Learner replies quickly, warmly and correctly to all scenario types.
Learner handles a negative comment without arguing.
Learner deletes spam and abuse but keeps legitimate criticism.
Learner builds a complete ten-reply bank and a moderation policy.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"The food will speak for itself — no need to reply." → Silence loses customers; good replies win them.
"I'll reply however I feel." → A professional reply is planned, on-brand and consistent.
"Delete anything negative." → Keep and answer legitimate criticism; delete spam and abuse only.
"Arguing with a complaint shows strength." → Arguing publicly loses the room — calm professionalism wins it.
"A reply bank is a script to copy-paste blindly." → Adapt the reply to the specific comment; the structure stays, the details
change.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Tone check: give five replies with inconsistent tones and ask the learner to rewrite them in one defined tone.
2. Complaint response drill: give three complaint scenarios and ask the learner to write a reply using the four-step response.
3. Moderation sort: give ten comments (spam, abuse, criticism, trolling, questions) and ask the learner to categorise each and
state the action.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed reply bank — five replies already written — and ask the learner to write
the remaining five and the moderation policy.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written — a complete ten-reply bank and moderation policy for a new client.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a "community management guide" — a one-page document explaining the
tone of voice, the reply rules, the moderation policy and the response-time target to a new manager who has never
managed a community before. The guide must be in plain language, not jargon.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners completing a ten-reply bank and a moderation policy (Sections H and the
assignment). If the room is slow, shorten the tone-of-voice section (F.2) and protect the guided practical — the ability to write
professional replies is the core skill. If the room is fast, extend the case study discussion and let learners role-play the difficult
complaint scenarios in pairs.
