# Capstone: Security Assessment Report and Presentation

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Assess and Improve (capstone build and present)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes (build, finalise, present, viva)

**Difficulty:** Beginner (final integration of all skills)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–8. Learners must have produced a findings table (S7) and an improvement plan plus incident response card (S8). They must be able to explain the permission/scope rule, the eight rooms, risk = likelihood × impact, and the six incident steps.

**Connection to previous work:** Everything in this course has been building toward this single deliverable: a complete defensive security assessment report and prioritised improvement plan for an authorised, fictional business. The capstone is the certificate project; presenting it also completes LO10.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on; projector ready for presentations; a timer visible; printed Capstone Rubric (Section L) for each presenter; printed Viva Question List (Section M) for the instructor; the Adaeze Fashion running example complete report skeleton on the board; a class folder CEA-Sec Name for submissions; refreshments arrangement for the brief closing (optional).

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a complete defensive security assessment report for a fictional, authorised business, containing all required
sections.
2. Justify the scope and authorisation statement, and demonstrate the ethics of assessing only permitted systems.
3. Present their findings table, risk ratings, and prioritised improvement plan clearly to a non-technical audience.
4. Defend their decisions in a short viva, answering questions about risk ratings and recommendations.
5. Demonstrate one hardened, defensive action live (e.g., showing MFA switched on, or a backup completed) on their own
academy account/device.
6. Explain honestly what this qualification does and does not make them: a safer user and a junior IT-support beginner with
defensive awareness — not a hacker or penetration tester.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One computer per learner for finalising and presenting.
Instructor computer connected to a projector or large screen for presentations.
A visible timer (projector clock or phone).
Software
A word processor for the report and slides/notes for the presentation.
No attack tools, no scanning tools, no live systems. All targets are fictional and paper-based.
Printed materials
The Capstone Rubric (Section L) — one per presenter and one for the instructor.
The Viva Question List (Section M) — instructor copy.
The learner's own Session 7 findings table and Session 8 improvement plan and incident card.
Any vignette card / own invented business profile.
Accounts
Learners' own academy email account (for the live demonstration of MFA/backup only). No client accounts, no real
business data.
Internet
Optional, only for the live demonstration of turning on MFA on the learner's own email, or uploading a dummy file to their
own Drive. If the internet is down, the learner describes the action and shows the settings on Windows instead.
Safety and ethics note for the instructor: Repeat at the start and at the demo: all targets are fictional; the only live system
touched is the learner's own academy account, with their consent; nothing is done to any other person's system.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a finished-looking one-page report cover:
DEFENSIVE SECURITY ASSESSMENT Prepared for: Adaeze Fashion, Port Harcourt Prepared by: [Your name], Cyber Elias
Academy Date: October 2026 Authorised assessment of a fictional business — training use only.
What the instructor says:
"For eight weeks we have been building one skill at a time: spotting scams, strong passwords and MFA, safe browsing,
phishing, hardening devices, Wi-Fi and backups, then assessing a business, then writing a plan and an incident card.
Today all of it comes together into one document that a real business would pay for — and then you stand up and
present it.
This is your certificate project. It is not about being clever or fast. It is about being careful, honest, and clear. A business
owner reading your report should feel calmer, not more confused. That is the standard."
Ask the class: "Which single document would you want a new business to have ready before anything bad happens?"
Expected learner response: Learners will suggest the improvement plan, the incident response card, backups, MFA. Accept all,
then connect:
"Right — and your capstone report bundles all of these together: what is wrong (findings), what to do (plan), and what to
do if it happens anyway (incident card). Plus the executive summary that a busy owner reads first. Today you finish it and
present it."
Why this opener works: It frames the capstone as a real, saleable professional document and sets a clear quality standard ("the
owner should feel calmer"), which reduces anxiety about presenting.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a fast oral round-robin. These prompts restate the exact ingredients the learner must put into the report.
Question 1: "What must come before any assessment, and what is it called?"
Expected answer: written permission and a defined scope (scope and authorisation).
Common gap: saying "the owner's trust." Clarify: trust is good but it must be written and scoped.
Question 2: "Name four of the eight rooms again."
Expected answer: any four of accounts/passwords, MFA, updates, antivirus/firewall, Wi-Fi, backups, staff awareness, data
handling.
Common gap: forgetting backups or data handling. Prompt them.
Question 3: "How do you rate risk, and what are the three levels?"
Expected answer: likelihood × impact → Low/Medium/High. Reason required.
Common gap: leaving out the reason. Repeat: no reason, no rating.
Question 4: "In an incident, what is the very first action and why?"
Expected answer: disconnect from the network, to stop it spreading.
Common gap: "call the owner" — acceptable but containment comes first.
Question 5: "What is the difference between a findings table and an improvement plan?"
Expected answer: findings = what is wrong; plan = what to do, in order, with who/when/cost.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already have every ingredient. Today you simply put them into one neat report, add the executive summary at the
front and the conclusion at the end, and take the stage. The viva is not a trap — it is a conversation to check that the
report is truly yours and that you understand it."
If a learner has not completed the Session 7 and 8 assignments, use the first 15 minutes of the practical to help them finish the
findings table and plan using the Adaeze Fashion running example (Appendix 9A), so they can still build a report.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What the capstone is (restated)
Plain-English definition. The capstone is a complete defensive security assessment report and prioritised improvement
plan for an authorised, fictional business — for example, Adaeze Fashion, Port Harcourt. It gathers everything you learned into
one professional document, which you then present and defend in a short viva (a spoken question-and-answer).
Why it exists. Employers and clients do not pay for isolated skills; they pay for a clear deliverable. A report proves you can take
a messy real-world situation and produce something useful and trustworthy. It also proves you understand the ethics: the report
itself states its scope and authorisation, showing you know you may only assess permitted systems.
What it contains. Six core parts plus a plan and a card:
1. Executive summary
2. Scope and authorisation
3. Method
4. Findings table with risk ratings
5. Prioritised improvement plan
6. Incident response card
7. Conclusion
Familiar analogy. The capstone is like a final-year project or a building inspection report. The inspector visits, measures, writes
a clear report with photos and a list of repairs in priority order, and signs it. The owner can act on it. That is exactly what you
produce.
Important limitation / misconception. "The capstone means I can now work as a hacker or penetration tester." No. This course
is defensive only. The capstone proves you can assess and advise — a safer user and a junior IT-support beginner with
defensive awareness. It does not make you a penetration tester, and it does not authorise you to test anyone's system. See
Section O and the course wrap-up.

F.3 The required report structure
Write the report in this order. Each part has a clear job.
1. Title page.
Title: Defensive Security Assessment Report.
Prepared for: the fictional business name.
Prepared by: your name, Cyber Elias Academy.
Date.
A clear line: "Fictional business — authorised training assessment only."
2. Executive summary (about half a page).
The single most important part for a busy owner.
Plain English, no jargon. States: what was assessed, the overall state of security, the top three risks, and the top three
recommended actions. An owner should be able to read only this page and still know what to do first.
Example sentence: "Overall, the business's security is weak in three areas. The most urgent issue is that one weak password
protects the bank, email and social media at once. We recommend switching on MFA today (free) as the first step."
3. Scope and authorisation.
State the business (fictional), the person authorising (the owner), what is included, what is excluded, the date, and that it is
for training only. This proves you follow the ethics rule.
4. Method.
How you assessed: "The eight-room checklist; observation and interview; no system was changed; no other system was
tested." Keep it short and honest. Do not claim screenshots or scans you did not do.
5. Findings table.
Columns: Issue | Risk Level | Reason | Priority. Every row has a reason mentioning likelihood and impact.
6. Prioritised improvement plan.
Columns: Recommendation | Why | How | Who | When | Cost | Priority. Quick wins first; free fixes before paid.
7. Incident response card.
The one-page card: Prepare note + the six first-hour actions.
8. Conclusion.
A short, honest closing: what the biggest risks are, what should happen next, and a note that with the recommended free
fixes started today, the business's risk is meaningfully reduced. Thank the owner and offer the next review date.
Say this to the class: "Notice the report has a front an owner reads (executive summary), a middle that is the evidence, and a
back that is the action. Neat, ordered, professional."
Misconception. "More pages = better." No. A clear, well-ordered report of a few pages beats a long, confusing one. Clarity is
the mark of competence.
F.4 The live demonstration (what it adds)
The capstone includes one live demonstration of a defensive action on your own academy account or device. This proves you
can do, not just describe.
Choose one:
Show MFA switched on for your own academy email (walk through the settings).
Show a backup completed: upload a dummy file to your own Google Drive and show the copy exists.
Show Windows Defender and the firewall switched on, and updates set to automatic.
Show your own file saved into a password manager (if the demo is available).
Rules for the demo:
Only your own academy account/device. Never anyone else's.
Explain why the action reduces risk (one sentence).
Expected result: the setting visibly shows it is on, or the backup copy visibly exists.
What to watch: if internet fails, describe the action and show the setting on Windows instead. Do not claim a screenshot
you do not have.
F.5 Summary of the session
The capstone is a complete defensive assessment report for a fictional, authorised business.
Structure: executive summary, scope/authorisation, method, findings, plan, incident card, conclusion.
Meet the milestones (M1–M8); most of the work is already done from S7 and S8.
Present clearly to a non-technical owner; defend your reasoning in the viva.
Demonstrate one hardened action live on your own system.
Be honest about what the certificate means — a safer user / junior IT-support beginner, not a hacker.

Speak to the owner, not to the screen.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes) — Capstone build
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner assembles their capstone report from their S7 and S8 work and prepares a
short presentation.
Materials: Learner's S7 findings table, S8 improvement plan, S8 incident card, the report structure (F.3), the rubric (L), a word
processor.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. (2 min) Create the file CEA-Sec-YourName-Capstone in the class folder. Add the title page (who, what, when, "fictional —
training only").
2. (3 min) Pause and plan: On paper, write your top three risks and your top three actions. These will become your executive
summary.
3. (4 min) Write the executive summary (half a page, no jargon). Check: does it name the top three risks and top three
actions?
4. (2 min) Paste your scope and authorisation statement and your method paragraph (from Session 7/8).
5. (3 min) Paste your findings table and improvement plan, and incident card. Make sure the plan has each finding turned
into an action, quick wins first.
6. (2 min) Write the conclusion (three or four sentences).
7. (2 min) Pause and rehearse: read your executive summary aloud to your neighbour in under one minute. Ask them:
"Would a busy shop owner understand this?"
Expected result: A complete report containing the title page, executive summary, scope/authorisation, method, findings table,
improvement plan, incident card, and conclusion — in order. A short spoken summary ready.
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Executive summary full of jargon → rewrite: "weak password", "backup", not "credential hygiene" or "risk surface."
Missing scope/authorisation → add it now; it is a marked item.
Plan rows missing who/when → fix before submitting.
Learner wants to assess a real shop → stop and redirect to the fictional example.
Learner's demo would touch someone else's account → redirect to their own academy account.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Title page with "fictional — training only"
Executive summary names top 3 risks and top 3 actions
Scope and authorisation present
Method present
Findings table with reasons
Improvement plan with quick wins first, who/when/cost
Incident card included
Conclusion present
Report saved in CEA-Sec-YourName

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes) — Present and viva
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task: Each learner presents their capstone report (3 minutes) and answers viva questions (2 minutes). The instructor and class
use the rubric.
Objective: Demonstrate clear presentation and defend the reasoning behind findings, risk ratings and recommendations.
Materials: Projector; timer; rubric (L); viva list (M).
Procedure:
1. (1 min) Instructor explains the format: 3 minutes present + 2 minutes viva, per learner. While one presents, others listen and
note one strength and one question.
2. (2 min) First presenter sets up; instructor starts the timer.
3. (12 min) Each learner presents their report and demonstration, then answers 2–3 viva questions from Section M. The
instructor scores live using the rubric.
Presentation expectations (restate to the class):
Greeting and one-line summary.
Top three findings, each with a reason.
Top three actions, quick wins first.
One sentence on the incident card.
One live defensive demonstration on your own account.
Honest close and thanks.
Viva expectations: Answer in plain English; if you do not know, say so honestly and explain your reasoning — this is
acceptable and even expected of a beginner.
Instructor answer key / model viva answers: see Section M (the full viva key). The instructor listens for the ideas, not exact
words.
Success criteria (per learner):
Report is complete and in order.
Presentation clear, in time, with reasons given.
Viva answers show understanding, not memorisation.
Live demonstration is on the learner's own system and reduces risk.
Hints available if stuck (during presentation):
Hint 1: If you forget a finding, read it from your table — that is allowed.
Hint 2: If a viva question is hard, restate the question and answer the part you know.
Hint 3: Always name your top risk and its reason — that is the heart.
Note to instructor: This is the graded practical of the course (simulation 15 + capstone 30 + viva 5 in the assessable mark
distribution). Pace strictly so every learner presents.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze Fashion is a fictional but realistic Port Harcourt fashion business (the course's running example). Adaeze runs
the shop on one Android phone used for banking, POS and WhatsApp Business; she shares it with her assistant; she reuses one
weak password; MFA is off; the Wi-Fi password is on a card at the counter; there is no backup of her customer list; her two staff
have never been trained on scams; and the back-office Windows laptop has Defender off and is months behind on updates.
Relevant facts.
The most dangerous single issue is the reused weak password: one leak would expose her bank, email and social media
together.
Several High-risk issues are free to fix today (screen lock, MFA, password change, Wi-Fi password, Defender on).
The backup and the staff training are small projects.
No incident has happened yet — but the risk is real.
Decision point. If the business can only act on one thing today, what should it be, and why — and what comes second and
third?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy an expensive security suite and hope it solves everything.
2. Do the free quick wins first, then plan the projects.
3. Wait until there is a real problem.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Today: switch on MFA and change the reused password (free, protects money), put a
screen lock on the phone (free), change the Wi-Fi password and remove the card (free), switch Defender on and update the
laptop (free). This week: start a backup to Google Drive + a USB. This month: train the two staff. This mirrors the prioritised plan
and the capstone report exactly.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 spends money before addressing the free, highest-impact risks — poor value.
Approach 2 removes most of the immediate danger at little or no cost, then invests sensibly.
Approach 3 leaves the business exposed for no benefit; risk depends on likelihood and impact, not on whether anything has
happened yet.
Professional connection. This is precisely the judgement a junior IT-support person or consultant must exercise: protect the
money and the accounts first, spend nothing where free fixes suffice, and set a realistic plan for the rest. The capstone report is
the professional expression of this judgement.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
These are the most common failure points in the capstone and how to fix each.
K.1 Missing scope and authorisation statement
Symptoms: The report jumps straight into findings. Why it fails: It fails the scope/ethics criterion and shows the learner did not
follow the course's central rule. Corrective steps: Add a short section stating: fictional business, owner authorising, included,
excluded, date, "training only." How to confirm resolved: The reports opens with a clear, written permission statement.
Prevention: Write scope first, before any findings.
K.2 Jargon-filled executive summary
Symptoms: The summary uses words like "threat surface," "credential hygiene," "attack vector." Why it fails: The owner cannot
act on it; it fails the clarity criterion. Corrective steps: Rewrite in plain words: "weak password," "backup," "not trained." Aim for
a busy owner to understand it in one reading. Prevention: Write the summary as if speaking to Adaeze herself.
K.3 Risk ratings with no reasons
Symptoms: A column of "High/High/High." Why it fails: Fails the risk-rating criterion; looks like guessing. Corrective steps:
Add a likelihood-and-impact reason to every row. Re-check for at least one satisfactory/Low finding. Prevention: Rule of the
course — no reason, no rating.
K.4 Improvement plan lists problems, not actions
Symptoms: Rows read "Wi-Fi weak" instead of "Change the Wi-Fi password." Why it fails: The plan cannot be acted on.
Corrective steps: Rewrite each row starting with a verb; ensure who/when/cost are filled. Prevention: For each finding ask,
"What is the doing word?"
K.5 Assessing or demonstrating on a real/third-party system
Symptoms: Learner wants to "check" a real shop's Wi-Fi or someone else's phone during the demo. Why it fails: Serious ethics
breach; must never happen. Corrective steps: Stop immediately; redirect to the fictional report and the learner's own academy
account. Prevention: Repeat the permission rule before the demo; the only live system is your own.
K.6 Overrunning time in the presentation
Symptoms: Presenter runs past 3 minutes; later learners get squeezed. Corrective steps: Instructor signals time; presenter
jumps to top actions and close. Prevention: Rehearse; lead with the top three.
K.7 Claiming screenshots/scans not done
Symptoms: Report says "screenshots attached" or "port scan performed" when none was. Why it fails: Dishonest; a professional
report must be accurate. Corrective steps: Remove the claim; describe only what was actually observed/recorded. Prevention:
Write only what you did. Honesty is marked.
K.8 Not demonstrating a live action
Symptoms: No demo during the presentation. Corrective steps: Show one setting (MFA, backup, Defender) on your own
account and explain why it reduces risk. Prevention: Prepare the demo before the session.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: In your report, say what you observed and how you know it (who showed you / where you
looked). Do not present a guess as a confirmed fact.

## M. Knowledge Check — Viva Question List with Model Answers
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Instructor copy. Ask 2–3 per learner. Mark on understanding, not exact words. Accept any answer that shows the right idea.)
Q1. What is a defensive security assessment, and how is it different from hacking?
Model answer: A security assessment is a permission-based, organised look at a business's devices, accounts and habits to
find weaknesses, rate risk and recommend fixes. Hacking is breaking in without permission. The difference is written
permission: an assessment has it, hacking does not; a penetration test also has permission but this course does not do that.
Q2. Why does your report begin with scope and authorisation?
Model answer: Because you may only assess your own systems or systems you have written permission to assess. Scope
says exactly what is included and excluded. Stating it proves the assessment is ethical and legal. It is a fictional business used
for training only.
Q3. How did you rate risk, and why is one of your findings High?
Model answer: Risk = likelihood × impact, mapped to Low/Medium/High. (Then a specific example): "The reused weak
password is High because the likelihood is High (short, reused, guessable) and the impact is High (one leak reaches the
bank, email and social media)."
Q4. Why did you put the free fixes first in your improvement plan?
Model answer: Because they remove the most serious risks at no cost and can be done today, which a small business can
actually afford and act on. Expensive items are planned later.
Q5. What are the six incident response steps, and what is the first thing to do in a suspected breach?
Model answer: Prepare, Identify, Contain, Eradicate, Recover, Learn. First action: disconnect the affected device from the
network to stop it spreading.
Q6. Why should passwords be changed from a clean device during an incident?
Model answer: So the compromised device cannot capture the new passwords as you type them.
Q7. Why should the business not pay a ransomware demand?
Model answer: Paying does not guarantee the files return, it may fund criminals, and it can mark the business as a willing
payer. It is better to contain, preserve evidence, protect accounts, inform the right people, and restore from a clean backup.
Q8. What does your certificate actually qualify you to do?
Model answer (key honesty question): It shows I am a safer user and a junior IT-support beginner with defensive
awareness — I can protect accounts and devices, assess a small business's security with permission, and write a plan. It
does not make me a hacker or a penetration tester, and it does not authorise me to test anyone's system.
Q9. Which finding in your report did you rank highest, and would the owner agree? Why?
Model answer: (Any reasoned answer.) Typically the reused weak password, because it exposes money and multiple
accounts at once; the owner would likely agree because it directly touches her money, which she cares about most.
Q10. What would you do differently if this were a real paying client?
Model answer: Get a signed authorisation and a written scope; agree start/end times; keep the client's information
confidential; and, as a beginner, bring in a more experienced professional for anything beyond a basic assessment.
