# Wi-Fi Security, Mobile Safety and Backups

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Protecting People and Devices (theme: Harden) — final session of Week 2

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (no prior security knowledge assumed)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5. In particular: strong passwords + MFA (S2), safe browsing and HTTPS (S3), phishing (S4), and device hardening — updates, Defender, firewall (S5).

**Connection to previous work:** We have protected accounts, people and the computer itself. This session closes the loop in three ways: secure the network the computer uses (Wi-Fi/router), secure the phone in every pocket (mobile safety), and build the safety net so that even if a device is lost, stolen or infected, the important files survive (backups).

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on. Instructor machine for demonstrations. An external USB drive/flash drive or a spare folder for the backup demonstration. If demonstrating router settings, use a practice/fictional router interface or the instructor's own home router screenshots described verbally — never change a live router the class depends on, and never change a router the learner does not own. Learners need a USB drive or access to an external drive/folder for the practical. Safety / permission note (read aloud) "We only change settings on networks and devices we own or have written permission to change. We never touch someone else's router, and we never try to access anyone else's Wi-Fi. All practical backup work uses your own harmless practice files — never other people's private data. This is defensive work: we protect, we do not intrude."

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why Wi-Fi encryption (WPA2/WPA3) matters and describe what happens to data on an open network.
2. List and explain the key router hardening steps: change the default admin password, set a strong Wi-Fi password, turn off
WPS, and enable a guest network.
3. Describe the risks of public Wi-Fi and state what they should avoid doing on it.
4. Apply mobile safety basics: screen lock, reviewing app permissions, updating apps, using only official app stores, enabling
find-my-device, and avoiding sideloaded/cracked apps.
5. Explain the 3-2-1 backup rule in simple words: 3 copies, 2 different media, 1 offsite.
6. Decide what is worth backing up and compare cloud, external drive and USB options with their trade-offs.
7. Explain why a backup must be tested ("a backup you have never restored is not a backup") and describe how to test a
restore.
8. Build a backup plan for a small business and perform a folder backup to an external drive/USB and restore one file.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two Wi-Fi networks in the list: one named "ShopWiFi" (open, no lock icon) and one
named "ShopWiFi-Secure" (with a padlock). Do not connect to either. Just point.
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two Wi-Fi names. This one, 'ShopWiFi', has no padlock — it is open. Anyone nearby can join, and anything
sent on an open network can be seen or changed by someone on the same network. This other one has a padlock and a
password — that is encryption.
Now think of your phone. Your whole life is in it — bank app, WhatsApp, photos, contacts. And now think of the folder on
your computer with your shop records. If this laptop is stolen tonight, or a ransom attack locks your files, what happens to
that folder? ... For many people, it is gone forever.
Today we close three gaps: the network you connect to, the phone you carry, and the backup that saves you when
everything else fails."
Expected learner response: Learners may say they have used open Wi-Fi before ("at the market", "in a café"), or admit they
have never made a backup. Some may not know their router password. Accept all; make it clear this is common and fixable.
Then demonstrate the hook: On the projector, open a folder of harmless practice files (e.g., "CEA Practice Files") on the
Desktop. Copy it to a USB drive, then delete one file from the original folder and show how it can be restored from the USB
copy in seconds.
"Watch: I made a copy, then 'lost' a file, then got it back from the backup in one step. That is the whole idea. Simple —
but most people never do it, and pay a heavy price later."
Why this opener works: It uses the familiar Wi-Fi list to make "encryption" visible, connects the phone and the shop records to
a concrete loss the learner can imagine, and ends with a live, satisfying restore that proves the value of backups in seconds.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as an oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "From Session 5, name two device-hardening layers and say what each does."
Expected answer: e.g., updates fix known holes; Defender antivirus finds malware; firewall filters network connections;
standard account limits damage.
Common gap: mixing up firewall and antivirus. Re-clarify: firewall = network gate keeper; antivirus = file scanner.
Question 2: "Why is a unique strong password important, and what adds a second lock?"
Expected answer: so one leaked site does not unlock your other accounts; MFA adds a second factor.
Common gap: password reuse. Re-state: reuse is the chain that breaks everything.
Question 3: "From Session 3, what does a padlock in the browser mean, and what does it not mean?"
Expected answer: the connection is encrypted/private; it does not mean the site owner is honest.
Common gap: equating padlock with trust. This links directly to today's Wi-Fi encryption idea.
Bridge to today (say this):
"We have hardened the computer. But three things remain: the network it joins, the phone we carry everywhere, and our
last line of defence — the backup. Remember the school case from last time? Prevention is best, but backups are what
let you recover even when prevention fails. Today we protect all three."
If more than a third of the class cannot name two hardening layers, spend 3 extra minutes re-teaching them before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Wi-Fi security basics — encrypting the air
Plain-English definition. Wi-Fi security means protecting the data that travels wirelessly between your device and the router so
that other people nearby cannot read or change it.
Why it exists. Wi-Fi travels through the air as radio waves. On an open network (no password), the data is like a postcard —
anyone nearby with the right tool can read it. With encryption, the data is like a sealed, coded letter — even if someone
intercepts it, they cannot read it.
The standards (just enough):
WPA2 — strong encryption; the common secure standard for years. Uses AES encryption.
WPA3 — the newer, stronger standard; better protection, especially against guessing the Wi-Fi password. If your router
supports it, prefer it.
WEP — very old and weak; should not be used. If your router only offers WEP, it is old and should be replaced or
configured carefully.
Open / no security — no encryption; avoid for anything private.
The analogy. WPA2/WPA3 are like a strong safe for the messages in the air; WEP is like a paper envelope someone can easily
open; an open network is a postcard anyone can read.
A realistic Nigerian scenario. A POS operator runs an open Wi-Fi "to help customers". A nearby attacker on the same network
watches traffic and redirects a customer to a fake page. Had the Wi-Fi used WPA2/WPA3 with a strong password, the traffic
would have been encrypted and far harder to read or redirect.
Misconception. "My Wi-Fi has a password, so it is fully safe." A password protects joining the network and encrypts traffic only
if the security type is WPA2/WPA3. Also, a weak Wi-Fi password can be guessed. The type of security matters, not just the
presence of a password.
Limitation. Wi-Fi encryption protects the air link between your device and the router. It does not protect you from a malicious
website you choose to visit, or from malware. It is one layer.
F.2 Router hardening — locking the front gate
The router is the device that brings the internet to your home/shop and shares it out. It is the front gate. Hardening it is one of
the highest-value, lowest-cost security steps — and it is done once, then mostly forgotten.
Step 1 — Change the default router admin password. Every router ships with a default admin username/password (often
"admin/admin"), printed on a sticker or in the manual, and known to attackers. If you never change it, anyone on your network
(or even remotely, in some cases) can log in and change your settings. Change it to a strong, unique password and store it
safely.
Step 2 — Set a strong Wi-Fi password. The Wi-Fi password (the one you give guests) must be long and not guessable. A
short password like "12345678" or the shop name can be guessed. Use a long passphrase (several words) — remember the idea
from Session 2.
Step 3 — Turn off WPS. WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup) is a feature meant to make connecting easy, often via a button or a
PIN. Its PIN method has known weaknesses and can be abused. Turn WPS off unless you truly need it.
Step 4 — Use a guest network. Most modern routers let you create a guest network — a separate Wi-Fi for visitors,
customers and smart devices. Guests get internet but cannot reach your main devices (your shop computer, your files). This is
like keeping visitors in the sitting room instead of letting them roam the whole house. If your router supports it, enable it; use
the guest network for customers and unknown devices.
Optional good practices: keep the router firmware updated (check the manufacturer's app/admin page), and change the
default network name (SSID) so it does not reveal the router model. (Renaming is a small benefit; the password and WPS
matter more.)
The analogy. The router is the gate to your compound: the Wi-Fi password is the lock on the gate; the admin password is the
lock on the control room; WPS-off removes a spare hidden key under the mat; the guest network is a separate visitors' area.
How to access router settings (general guidance only): You usually open a browser and go to the router's admin address
(often printed on the sticker, e.g., 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1 ), then log in. Wording and layout differ by brand — the
instructor should tell learners: "the exact menu names vary, look for Security / Wireless / WPS / Guest Network options." Do not
change a router you do not own or are not authorised to change.
Realistic Nigerian scenario. Many homes and small shops never change the router admin password. When the router is
replaced or returned after repair, or when a neighbour or a visitor knows the default, settings can be changed without the
owner's knowledge. Changing the admin password and disabling WPS takes ten minutes and closes this.
Misconceptions.
"The default is fine, nobody knows it." Attackers and manuals know it; it is published.
"WPS is convenient, I will leave it on for guests." Convenience for you is also convenience for an intruder; turn it off.
"Guests only need internet, they won't touch my files." Without a guest network, guests are on the same network as your
devices.
Limitation. Router hardening protects your home/shop network. It does not protect you when you join someone else's network
(public Wi-Fi), which is why Section F.3 matters.
F.3 Public Wi-Fi risks — and what to avoid
Plain-English definition. Public Wi-Fi is a wireless network you do not control, such as in a café, hotel, airport, or at an event.
You cannot see who else is on it or how it is configured.
The risks.
Snooping — on an open network, others may be able to observe unencrypted traffic.
Fake hotspots — an attacker sets up a network with a tempting name ("Free Airport WiFi") so devices connect to their
network instead.
Rogue redirects — being sent to a fake login page to steal details.
The analogy. Using public Wi-Fi is like making a phone call in a crowded market — someone may be listening. Using mobile
data (your GSM network) or a trusted network is like a private line.
What to avoid on public Wi-Fi (teach this list):
Banking and mobile-money transactions — avoid, if possible, unless you are on mobile data or a trusted network.
Entering passwords or card details on pages you do not absolutely trust.
Accessing sensitive work systems without an approved secure method (e.g., a company VPN).
Connecting automatically to unknown networks — turn off "auto-connect to open networks" on your phone.
What to do instead (defensive):
Use mobile data (your SIM data) for anything sensitive.
If you must use public Wi-Fi, prefer networks you or your organisation control, and use HTTPS sites (padlock) — though
remember from Session 3 that a padlock is only one clue.
If your workplace provides a VPN, use it, and follow your organisation's rules.
Forget the network after use, and avoid saving auto-connect.
Misconception. "Public Wi-Fi is safe if it asks for a password." A password you are given for a café network does not make it
private or trustworthy; everyone in the café has it.
Limitation. You can reduce public-Wi-Fi risk but never fully trust it. The safest habit is to keep sensitive activity on mobile data
or trusted networks.
F.4 Mobile security — protecting the phone in your pocket
The phone is often the most important and least-protected device we own. It holds bank apps, WhatsApp, photos and contacts.
The same Stop–Think–Verify mindset applies, plus a few mobile-specific controls.
1. Screen lock. Set a PIN, password, pattern, or biometric (fingerprint/face) lock with a short auto-lock time (e.g., 30
seconds). This is the first barrier if the phone is lost or stolen. Avoid obvious PINs (birthdates, 0000 , 1234 ).
2. App permissions. When you install an app, it asks for permissions (camera, microphone, location, contacts, files, SMS). Give
only what the app truly needs for its job. A torch app does not need your contacts; a simple game does not need your SMS.
Review permissions in your phone's settings and revoke anything unnecessary.
3. Update apps and the phone. Keep both the operating system and the apps updated — the same "broken window" logic as
Session 5. Updates fix known security holes in apps.
4. Only official app stores. Install apps from the official store for your phone (Google Play on Android, App Store on iPhone).
Avoid downloading app files (APKs) from random sites or links, and avoid "cracked" or "modded" apps — these are a
common source of malware and of stolen data. A cracked app is like buying a cheap phone from a stranger in the market with
the case already opened.
5. Find-my-device. Enable Find My Device (Android) or Find My (iPhone). If the phone is lost or stolen, you can locate, lock, or
erase it remotely. Test that it is on before you need it.
6. Screen privacy and sharing. Be careful with screen sharing and shoulder-surfing; use a screen lock and do not leave the
phone unlocked around strangers.
7. Be careful with links and OTPs on mobile. Phishing arrives by SMS and WhatsApp constantly. Apply Session 4's rules:
hover/long-press before tapping, never share OTPs, verify by a separate known channel.
8. Back up the phone too. Contacts, photos and messages matter. Enable the phone's built-in backup (to your account/cloud)
so a lost phone does not mean lost memories.
The analogy. Your phone is a wallet that also holds your house keys. Lock it, do not hand out copies, only install trusted apps,
and enable the "lost wallet" finder.
Realistic Nigerian scenario. A trader installs a "free" modded version of a popular app from a link on WhatsApp. The app asks
for SMS permission, which the trader grants. It then reads incoming OTPs and the attacker drains the account. A screen lock
alone would not stop this — the real fix was official app stores only and minimal permissions.
Misconceptions.
"iPhone/Android is secure, so I do not need to do anything." Both need updates, a screen lock and safe app habits.
"Permissions are harmless." Permissions are exactly what let a bad app steal data.
"Cracked apps are just cheaper." They often carry malware or spyware.
Limitation. Mobile security reduces risk but cannot stop every trick; the habits from Sessions 1–5 remain essential.
F.5 Backups — the safety net
Plain-English definition. A backup is an extra copy of your important data kept somewhere else, so that if the original is lost,
stolen, corrupted or locked by ransomware, you still have the data.
Why it exists. Prevention can fail. Devices get stolen (a laptop in traffic), damaged (water, fire), infected (ransomware encrypts
files), or files get deleted by mistake. A backup is the only guaranteed recovery for data — this is why security professionals
treat it as essential, not optional.
The 3-2-1 rule — explained simply:
3 — keep three copies of your important data: the original plus two backups.
2 — keep them on two different types of media (for example, an external drive and the cloud), so a single failure cannot
destroy all copies.
1 — keep one copy offsite (not in the same building), so a fire, flood or theft at one place cannot destroy every copy.
Example for a small shop. Copy 1: the shop computer (original). Copy 2: an external hard drive kept in the shop. Copy 3: a
cloud backup (offsite). If the shop burns or is robbed, the cloud copy survives; if the internet is down, the external drive works.
What to back up. The data you cannot recreate:
Business records, invoices, customer lists, spreadsheets.
Photos and videos, important documents (certificates, ID copies, contracts).
Phone contacts and messages (via phone backup). What not to bother with: the operating system and installed programs
(they can be reinstalled) — though a full system image is an option for advanced setups.

Best practice: combine at least two methods — e.g., a USB/external drive for a local copy and a cloud copy for offsite. This
satisfies 3-2-1 without much cost.
The golden rule (teach this phrase): "A backup you have never restored is not a backup." Always test that you can actually
restore a file. An untested backup may be incomplete, corrupted, or encrypted with a forgotten password — and you only
discover that when you desperately need it.
How to test a restore: pick one unimportant file from your backup, delete the original (or use a copy), and restore it from the
backup. If it opens correctly, your backup works.
Realistic Nigerian scenario. A shop owner "backed up" every month to a USB drive — but the drive was always left plugged
into the same computer. When the computer was stolen, the backup was stolen with it. 3-2-1 would have kept an offsite cloud
copy. Another owner never tested a restore; when needed, the "backup" folder was empty.
Misconceptions.
"I have a backup, so I am safe." Only if it is separate, current and tested.
"The cloud is enough." Cloud is excellent for offsite, but you also want a local copy for speed and when offline. (3-2-1
covers this.)
"I will do it later." Data loss does not wait; set backups to run automatically where possible.
Limitation. Backups protect data, not devices. You still need the other layers to prevent the loss in the first place — but when
prevention fails, backups are what let you recover.
F.6 Summary of the session
Wi-Fi encryption: prefer WPA2/WPA3; an open network is a postcard anyone can read.
Router hardening: change the admin password, set a strong Wi-Fi password, turn off WPS, enable a guest network.
Public Wi-Fi: avoid banking/sensitive logins; prefer mobile data or trusted networks; turn off auto-connect.
Mobile safety: screen lock, minimal permissions, update apps/phone, official stores only, enable find-my-device, avoid
cracked apps.
Backups: the 3-2-1 rule (3 copies, 2 media, 1 offsite); back up what you cannot recreate.
Test your restore — "a backup you have never restored is not a backup."

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A folder of harmless practice files on the Desktop named CEA Practice Files containing a few dummy documents (e.g.,
note1.txt , note2.txt , logo.png ).
An external drive or USB flash drive, or a second practice folder if no drive.
A practice/fictional router interface or a set of described router screens for the router-hardening walk-through. Do not
change a live router the class uses. If showing the instructor's own router, use described/remembered settings or a
screenshot prepared in advance — never fiddle with a network others depend on.
The instructor's own phone (or a phone they are authorised to demonstrate on) for the mobile-safety walk-through —
never a learner's phone, and never collect any of its data.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — router hardening walk-through (5 minutes)
Say: "I cannot change the academy's router — that would knock us all offline, and I do not have permission. So I will walk you
through what the settings look like in general, and you will apply it at home on your own router with your permission."
Using a prepared screenshot or description (projector):
1. Admin password: "The router has an admin login, often admin/admin by default. We change this to a strong, unique
password."
2. Wi-Fi security type: show where WPA2/WPA3 is selected (not WEP, not "None"). "Choose WPA2 or WPA3, whichever the
router supports."
3. Wi-Fi password: show where the Wi-Fi password is set. "Long passphrase, not the shop name."
4. WPS: show the WPS toggle and turn it Off. "This removes the hidden spare key."
5. Guest network: show the guest-network option. "Turn it on for customers and visitors."
Expected result: Learners can identify the five settings to look for. (Actual changes happen on their own routers at home.)
What to watch: Emphasise brand differences in menu names; warn that some routers require a restart. Questions: "Why turn
off WPS?" → known weakness. "Why a guest network?" → isolates visitors from your devices.
G.3 Demonstration 2 — mobile safety walk-through (5 minutes)
Say: "Now the phone. I will use my own phone. Watch — and apply the same checks to yours with the screen facing away
from anyone if you prefer."
On the instructor's own phone (projector via screen/camera or described):
1. Screen lock: show the lock settings and auto-lock time.
2. Permissions: open the app list → choose one app → show its permissions and revoke/deny an unnecessary one (e.g., a
game with location/SMS).
3. Updates: show the app store's update list; show OS update location.
4. Official store: point at the store icon; "this is the only place to get apps."

2. Copy the folder to the USB/external drive (drag, or right-click → Copy, then Paste to the drive). Show it appearing on the

4. Restore: copy note1.txt back from the USB drive to the Desktop folder. Open it to show the content is intact.
5. Say: "That is 3-2-1 in action: this USB is copy 2. A cloud copy would be copy 3 offsite. And I just tested the restore — which

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation (fictional, non-identifying). A growing catering business in Port Harcourt — call it Mama Eka's Kitchen — keeps all
its orders, supplier prices, customer contacts and food photos on a single laptop. The owner also keeps a notebook of repeat
customers on her Android phone. The business connects to the open Wi-Fi at a nearby plaza because it is free, and she does her
bank transfers from that network on the phone.
One evening, a thief snatched the laptop bag at a bus stop. The laptop had the only copy of the customer and supplier records.
There was no backup. The phone's screen was unlocked, and it also had no cloud backup of contacts and photos. It took the
business months to rebuild its records from memory, paper scraps and customer messages — and it lost supplier price history
it could not recover at all. A few weeks earlier, the owner had also noticed a strange bank alert that she could not explain; she
wondered whether the open Wi-Fi had played a part.
Relevant facts.
Single copy of critical data on one laptop → no backup at all.
Phone had no screen lock and no backup.
Business banking on open Wi-Fi.
No find-my-device enabled on the phone.
Records were business-critical and impossible to fully recreate.
Decision point. The loss has already happened. The business asks: what should have been in place, and what should be set up
now?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a new laptop and carry on as before.
2. Set up only a cloud backup and change nothing else.
3. Implement the full package: the 3-2-1 backup (days-saved records, tested restores), a phone screen lock + find-my-
device + backup, move banking off open Wi-Fi to mobile data, and secure the home/shop Wi-Fi with WPA2/WPA3, a
changed admin password and WPS off.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. It addresses every cause: no backup, an unprotected phone, and sensitive activity on an
untrusted network. It uses mostly free or low-cost tools (cloud, built-in phone features, router settings) and, crucially, tests the
restore so the backups can be trusted.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 replaces hardware but repeats the same mistake — the next theft would hurt just as much.
Approach 2 is a good start but leaves the phone unprotected and banking on open Wi-Fi still risky.
Approach 3 takes some setup time and a little discipline (regular backup, test the restore), and cloud space may have a
small cost — but it turns a business-ending loss into a minor inconvenience. The one real cost is habit: if backups are not
kept current and tested, the protection fades.
Professional connection. This is the heart of defensive security work for small businesses: protect the network, protect the
mobile device, and ensure recoverable, tested backups. These map directly to the capstone assessment in Week 3 (findings
table with risk levels, fixes and priorities). It also reinforces a professional mantra: prevention first, recovery always.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use as the live troubleshooting guide. Ask the learner to check the relevant row before giving the answer.
K.1 "I left the router on default settings"
Symptoms: Router admin still admin/admin ; WPS on; no guest network. Likely cause: Never changed since installation.
(Confirmed by logging into the router — only on your own authorised router.) How to investigate: Open the router admin
page (address on the sticker) on your own router and check the settings. Safe corrective steps: Change the admin password to
a strong unique one; set Wi-Fi security to WPA2/WPA3; turn off WPS; enable a guest network. How to confirm resolved:
Settings show the new admin password, WPA2/WPA3, WPS off, guest network on. Prevention: Do this once at
setup/replacement; keep the firmware updated.
K.2 "My Wi-Fi password is simple so customers can type it"
Symptoms: Short Wi-Fi password like "shop123" or the business name. Likely cause: Convenience. (Confirmed by reading the
password rules/setting.) How to investigate: Check the Wi-Fi security settings. Safe corrective steps: Change to a long
passphrase; share it via a displayed sign only with trusted people; consider a guest network for customers. How to confirm
resolved: A long, non-obvious passphrase is in use; guest network used for visitors. Prevention: Use the guest network so
customers need not get the main password.
K.3 "I do banking on free public Wi-Fi"
Symptoms: Sensitive logins on open/unknown networks. Likely cause: Free data, not understanding the risk. How to
investigate: Ask where the banking is done; check whether mobile data is available. Safe corrective steps: Move
banking/transfers to mobile data or a trusted network. If a work VPN is provided, use it. Turn off auto-connect to open
networks. How to confirm resolved: Sensitive activity is done on mobile data/trusted network. Prevention: Treat public Wi-Fi
as untrusted; assume it can be watched.
K.4 "My phone has no screen lock"
Symptoms: Phone opens without PIN/biometric; sometimes no auto-lock. Likely cause: Convenience. (Confirmed by checking
lock settings.) How to investigate: Phone Settings → Security/Lock screen. Safe corrective steps: Set a PIN/biometric with a
short auto-lock; avoid obvious PINs; enable Find My Device. How to confirm resolved: Device locks automatically; Find My
Device shows enabled. Prevention: Set this on day one and enable phone backup.
K.5 "I installed a cracked/modded app from a link"
Symptoms: Apps from outside the official store; unexpected ads, pop-ups, battery drain, strange permissions. Likely cause:
Sideloaded/cracked app, often carrying malware or spyware. How to investigate: Check app list and permissions; note apps
not installed via the store. Safe corrective steps: Uninstall the app; scan the phone; review and revoke unnecessary
permissions; reinstall from the official store if genuinely needed. How to confirm resolved: Unknown app gone; no strange
behaviour; permissions tidy. Prevention: Official stores only; no cracked apps.
K.6 "My backup is on the same computer / I never tested it"
Symptoms: Backup drive always plugged into the same machine; no record of a test restore; backup may be stale or empty.
Likely cause: Only one copy and no testing. (Confirmed by checking where the backup lives and when it was last restored.)
How to investigate: List where each copy is; try restoring one file. Safe corrective steps: Add an offsite copy (cloud or a drive
kept elsewhere); schedule regular automatic backups; test a restore and record the date. How to confirm resolved: 3-2-1
satisfied; a test restore succeeded and is recorded. Prevention: Calendar a monthly test restore; keep one copy offsite always.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Each "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after checking — e.g., actually logging into
your own router, reading the backup location list, or performing a test restore. Teach learners to verify before concluding.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today we protected the network, the phone and your data's survival. On Wi-Fi, encryption
(WPA2/WPA3) turns the "postcard" travelling through the air into a sealed coded letter, and we harden the router by changing
the admin password, setting a strong Wi-Fi password, turning off WPS and enabling a guest network. On public Wi-Fi we avoid
banking and sensitive logins, preferring mobile data or a trusted network. On the phone we set a screen lock, limit app
permissions, update apps, use official stores only, and enable find-my-device — and we avoid cracked apps. Finally, the 3-2-1
backup rule (3 copies, 2 media, 1 offsite) is our safety net, and we learned the golden rule: a backup you have never restored
is not a backup.
Five important takeaways:
1. Prefer WPA2/WPA3; an open network is readable by others.
2. Harden the router: change admin password, strong Wi-Fi password, WPS off, guest network.
3. On public Wi-Fi, avoid banking — use mobile data or a trusted network.
4. Lock the phone, limit permissions, update apps, official stores only, enable find-my-device.
5. Follow 3-2-1 and always test a restore.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I explain WPA2/WPA3 versus an open network?
Can I list the router steps to apply on my own router?
Can I audit my own phone for the five mobile-safety items?
Can I build a 3-2-1 plan and prove a restore works?
