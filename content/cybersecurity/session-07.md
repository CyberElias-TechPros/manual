# Security Assessment Method and Risk Rating

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Assess and Improve

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first time assessing a whole business)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6. Learners must already know the basics of accounts and passwords, MFA, safe browsing, phishing, device hardening (updates, Windows Defender, firewall), Wi-Fi safety, and backups. If a learner cannot explain two of those in one sentence each, run the 15-minute refresher at the start of the prerequisite review.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–6 learners learned individual defensive skills — one lock, one door, one room at a time. This session teaches them to walk through a whole small business, room by room, write down what they find, and decide which problems matter most. It is the bridge from "I can secure my own phone" to "I can advise a small business."

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged in; projector ready; printed vignette cards (one business per pair) from Appendix 7A; printed findings-table worksheets (Section H); whiteboard divided into a 3 —3 risk grid drawn before class; the running example Adaeze Fashion written on the board.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define a defensive security assessment in their own words and explain how it differs from hacking or penetration testing.
2. State the rule of authorisation — that a system may only be assessed with the owner's written permission — and give
one example of an authorised target and one of an unauthorised target.
3. Build a written checklist covering at least seven areas of a small business: accounts and passwords, MFA, device updates,
antivirus and firewall, Wi-Fi, backups, staff awareness and phishing risk, and data handling.
4. Gather evidence safely by describing and recording what is seen, without changing anything and without testing other
people's systems.
5. Rate a finding's risk using likelihood × impact and assign Low, Medium or High.
6. Prioritise findings and justify which problem should be fixed first.
7. Produce a findings table with columns for the issue, the risk level, and the reason, for a fictional business scenario.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a simple, familiar picture on the board: a small shop drawn with a door, a window, a
padlock on the door, and a back gate left open. Write underneath it: Adaeze Fashion, Port Harcourt.
What the instructor says:
"Imagine you are the person Adaeze calls when something feels wrong with her shop's security. She says: 'I think my shop
is okay, but I am not sure.' What do you do? You do not force the door to see if it opens — that is breaking in. Instead, you
walk around the shop with her permission, look at the door, the window, the padlock, and you notice the back gate is open.
Then you write it down: 'Back gate — not locked — very easy for someone to enter.' That is today's whole lesson.
Look at the back gate in my drawing. Would you say that is a small problem or a big problem? Why?"
Expected learner response: Learners will likely say the back gate is a big problem, because it is easy to enter and dangerous.
Some may say "small because nothing was stolen yet." Accept both. Then steer toward the key idea:
"The gate is a big problem not because of what already happened, but because it is easy to do (high likelihood) and if it
happens the loss is serious (high impact). That combination is what we call risk. Today you will learn to look at a business
the same way — politely, with permission — and score every weakness as Low, Medium or High."
Why this opener works: It gives a physical, everyday picture (a shop gate) before any technical term, and it introduces the two
ingredients of risk — likelihood and impact — through a question the learners can already answer.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the five prompts on the projector. This deliberately re-uses the eight areas that will
become the checklist.
Question 1: "Name two things that make a password strong."
Expected answer: long, and unique (not reused); mixing letters, numbers and symbols helps; a password manager helps you
keep them unique.
Common gap: learners say only "mix symbols". Remind them that length and uniqueness matter most.
Question 2: "What is MFA, and give one example?"
Expected answer: Multi-Factor Authentication — logging in with two different kinds of proof, e.g. password plus a code
from an app or SMS.
Common gap: confusing MFA with "just a strong password." Clarify: MFA is a second lock.
Question 3: "Why do we install updates on phones and computers?"
Expected answer: updates fix known weaknesses and bugs that attackers use; an out-of-date device is an easier target.
Common gap: "updates make it faster." Redirect to security fixes.
Question 4: "What does a backup protect you against?"
Expected answer: losing data — from theft, fire, accidental deletion, or ransomware.
Common gap: thinking a backup is only for phone photos. Explain it also applies to business records.
Question 5: "If a strange email asks you to click a link and 'confirm your bank details urgently', what do you do?"
Expected answer: do not click; verify through the official app or a known phone number; report it.
Common gap: "click to check if it's real." Correct: clicking is exactly what the attacker wants.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Notice something: every question I just asked is a room in the shop. Passwords, MFA, updates, backups, and phishing
awareness are five rooms. Today we turn those into a written checklist, walk through a fictional business, and score every
problem. When we are done, you will have a findings table — which is the exact document a real small business would
pay for."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Questions 1 and 4, spend an extra 10 minutes re-teaching passwords and
backups from Session 2 and Session 6 notes before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a security assessment actually is
Plain-English definition. A security assessment is a careful, organised look at how a business uses its computers, accounts,
phones, network and people, in order to find weak points and write down what should be improved. It is done with the
owner's permission and its output is a report, not damage.
Why it exists. A small business owner is busy selling. She cannot see her own blind spots — nobody can easily see their own
blind spots. An outside (or internal) person with a checklist can look calmly and notice the open back gate. The assessment
exists so that problems are found before an attacker finds them.
What it does.
It records the current state of security honestly ("here is how things are now").
It identifies weaknesses ("here is where you are exposed").
It rates how serious each weakness is ("this one is High, that one is Low").
It recommends improvements ("do these first").
Familiar analogy. A security assessment is like a health check-up at a clinic. The doctor does not make you sick to test you. The
doctor asks questions, measures, looks, and then writes a report: "Your blood pressure is high — please change your diet." A
security assessment is a check-up for a business's computers and habits.
Real-life Nigerian scenario. A small POS operator in Rumuokoro keeps his phone unlocked, uses the same PIN as his bank app,
and has never updated the phone. A friend who does "small IT work" offers to look at it — but the right way is to ask: "Do I
have your permission and can I write down what I find?" He says yes. The assessment finds three issues and a simple fix list.
Nothing is broken; everything is just written down and improved.
Important limitation / misconception. "Assessment" does not mean breaking in. Some people hear "security assessment" and
picture a hacker in a dark room. That is a different activity. Our course is defensive only. We look, we ask, we record, we advise.
We never attack, exploit, or test someone else's system to prove a point.

The key words: the same action can be legal or criminal depending only on permission. A pen tester and a criminal might use
a similar technique; what separates them is a signed authorisation document. Even a pen tester without permission is a
criminal.
What you, as a Cyber Elias Academy learner, are allowed to assess:
1. Systems you personally own (your own phone, your own laptop).
2. Systems you are given written permission to assess (a real client who signs an authorisation form, or the fictional
businesses in this course).
What you are never allowed to assess: any system that belongs to someone else without their written permission — a school,
a church, a shop, your friend's phone, a website, an office network. Not even "just to check if it's safe." Not even "for their own
good."
Say this to the class:
"There is a very short sentence that separates a professional from a criminal: Do I have written permission? If the answer is
no, you stop. In this course the only systems we touch are our own, and the only businesses we assess are the fictional ones
written on paper. That is the rule for the rest of your career."
Why this matters professionally. If you ever accept payment to assess a business and you did not get written permission, you
could lose your job, be sued, or be arrested — even if you found nothing and damaged nothing. Written permission protects
the business and you.
F.3 Scope and authorisation
Scope means: exactly what am I allowed to look at, and what am I not? A proper assessment always begins with scope, agreed
with the owner before anyone touches anything.
A simple authorisation checklist (used even for fictional clients):
1. Who owns the system? (Name the business and the person giving permission.)
2. What is included? (Which computers, accounts, Wi-Fi, phones, records.)
3. What is excluded? (Everything else — say it out loud.)
4. When? (Start and end time of the assessment.)
5. What will we NOT do? (We will not change settings, we will not test other companies' systems, we will not attempt to
break in.)
6. Permission in writing. For a real client: a signed form. For this course: the fictional client's name on the vignette card.
Familiar analogy. Scope is like a landlord giving a repairman a list of flats. "You may enter flats 1, 2 and 3. Do not enter flat 4
— a tenant lives there." Even if flat 4's door is open, you do not go in. Scope is respect written down.
Real-life Nigerian scenario. A church asks you to check whether its WhatsApp group is safe. The scope is the group's settings
and the leaders' phones — not the personal chats of every member. If you start reading the members' private messages, you
have gone outside scope, even though the church trusted you. Staying inside scope protects that trust.
Misconception. "The owner said 'do whatever you need to.'" That is not scope; it is a vague sentence. Convert it into a written
list of included and excluded items, and ask the owner to confirm it. A real professional never works from a vague verbal
permission.
F.4 Building the checklist — the eight rooms
A checklist is just the eight areas we already learned, written down so we do not forget any. Walk through each room and ask
the same questions every time.
Room 1 — Accounts and passwords.
Are passwords long and unique for each service?
Is there a shared password written on a paper or a WhatsApp message? (Very common in small businesses.)
Are old accounts (former staff) still active?
Room 2 — MFA (multi-factor authentication).
Is MFA switched on for bank, email, and any business app?
On which accounts is it missing?
Room 3 — Device updates.
Are the operating system and apps set to update automatically?
How long has it been since the last update?
Room 4 — Antivirus and firewall.
Is Windows Defender (or another protector) switched on and up to date?
Is the firewall switched on?
Room 5 — Wi-Fi and network.
Is the Wi-Fi password strong and changed from the default?
Is the Wi-Fi encrypted (WPA2 or WPA3)?
Is guest Wi-Fi separated from the business devices?
Room 6 — Backups.
Does a backup exist?
Is it done regularly (daily or weekly)?
Is one copy kept off the machine — for example in Google Drive or on a USB kept elsewhere?
Has anyone actually tried to restore from the backup? (A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a guarantee.)
Room 7 — Staff awareness and phishing risk.
Have staff been taught to spot a fake bank alert, a fake WhatsApp link, a fake "POS reversal" message?
Does anyone know what to do if they click something wrong?
Room 8 — Data handling.
Where is customer information kept (names, phone numbers, account details)?
Is sensitive data sitting in an unlocked phone or a shared notebook anyone can photograph?
Is old data deleted when it is no longer needed?
Say this to the class:
"Eight rooms. Every assessment you ever do is just walking these eight rooms with the same questions. When you
remember the eight rooms, you can never be stuck staring at a blank page."
F.5 Gathering evidence safely
Evidence is what you actually observed, written down in a way that can be trusted. "I think the Wi-Fi is weak" is not evidence.
"The router login page still uses the default password label admin " is evidence — if you saw it with permission.
Rules for safe evidence gathering:
1. Look and ask first. Most evidence comes from asking the owner and reading settings with permission, not from scanning.
2. Change nothing. Do not switch settings, do not delete files, do not "quickly fix it" during the assessment. You are a doctor
doing a check-up, not a surgeon operating. Changing things destroys the evidence and can break the business.
3. Write exactly what you saw. Name the device, the setting, and the state. Use plain words.
4. Date and sign. Note the date, the business, and who showed you.
5. Keep it confidential. A findings list is a sensitive document. Do not post it in a WhatsApp group or leave it on a cyber
café computer.
Evidence record template (used throughout the course):
Date Area (room) What I observed How I know (who showed me / where I looked)
2026-10-05 Wi-Fi Router still uses the default admin password Owner opened the router page and showed me
Familiar analogy. Evidence gathering is like a police officer taking notes at a scene — they write what they see, in order,
without moving the furniture. If they rearrange the room, the notes are useless.
Important limitation / misconception. "A real assessment uses scanning tools." Some professional assessments do, but only
on systems the assessor is authorised to scan, and only by trained professionals. For a small business walk-through and for this
course, asking and observing is the correct and sufficient method. You do not need any attack tool to do this course's
assessment.
F.6 Rating risk — likelihood × impact
Now the most important skill of the session: turning a finding into a risk level.
Two questions for every finding:
1. Likelihood — how easy or likely is it that this weakness gets used? (High, Medium, Low)
2. Impact — if it is used, how bad is the damage? (High, Medium, Low)
Risk = Likelihood × Impact (in plain terms: how easy times how bad).
Then map to a simple level:
Impact Low Impact Medium Impact High
Likelihood High Medium High High
Likelihood Medium Low Medium High
Likelihood Low Low Low Medium
This is the 3×3 grid. Read it like a multiplication table for danger: the top-right corner (easy to happen and very damaging) is
the worst — High. The bottom-left (unlikely and mild) is the least worrying — Low.
Now apply it to real findings:
Wi-Fi password written on a card taped to the counter, admin password still default. Likelihood: High (anyone nearby,
or anyone who photographs the card, can join). Impact: High (on the same network they may reach business devices and
records). → High risk.
One staff member's phone is two years behind on updates. Likelihood: Medium (needs a specific attack). Impact:
Medium (phone holds some customer chats). → Medium risk.
Old staff account from a worker who left is still active. Likelihood: Medium (only that person knows the password, but
they left dissatisfied). Impact: High (could access business email and records). → High risk.
No backup at all. Likelihood of needing a backup: High over a year (phones get stolen, files get deleted). Impact: High
(business records lost forever). → High risk.
Wi-Fi uses WPA2 with a long password. Likelihood: Low. Impact: Medium if it did fail. → Low risk. (This is a good finding;
note it as "satisfactory".)
Why a simple scheme is good. Big companies use more complex scoring with numbers and percentages. For a small Nigerian
business, a plain Low / Medium / High is understood by the owner and still drives the right decisions. Do not over-complicate.
Accuracy matters more than fancy numbers.
Misconception to correct. "If nothing bad has happened yet, the risk is Low." Wrong. Risk depends on how easy and how bad,
not on history. The back gate may never have been used — it is still High risk.
Important limitation. Risk rating is a judgement, not an exact science. Two careful people might rate the same finding Medium
or High. That is acceptable, as long as you show your reasoning ("High likelihood because the password is on a public card;
High impact because it exposes business records"). The reason column is what makes your rating trustworthy.
F.7 Prioritising findings
Once everything is rated, you decide what to fix first. Rating alone is not enough, because two High-risk items might need
different treatment (one is free and instant, the other costs money).
Priority order — a simple rule:
1. High risk + easy/cheap fix → do it immediately (e.g., change the default admin password, switch on MFA, lock the back
gate).
2. High risk + hard/expensive fix → plan it and start soon (e.g., buy an external drive for backups).
3. Medium risk → schedule within the improvement plan.
4. Low risk / already good → note it as satisfactory; revisit later.
This matches the "quick wins first" idea you will use fully in Session 8. For now, just understand the idea: the risk level tells you
what matters; the effort tells you the order.
Familiar analogy. Imagine a leaking roof and a squeaky door in your house. The squeaky door (low risk, easy) you fix today; the
leaking roof (high risk, needs money) you plan and pay for this month. You do both — but in a sensible order.
F.8 Summary of the session
A security assessment is a permission-based, organised look at a business's security that ends in a written report.
Authorisation and scope come first, always. Written permission only.
We are defensive only — we never attack, and we never assess other people's systems.
The eight rooms checklist: accounts/passwords, MFA, updates, antivirus/firewall, Wi-Fi, backups, staff awareness, data
handling.
Gather evidence safely by looking and asking; change nothing; write exactly what you saw.
Risk = likelihood × impact, mapped to Low / Medium / High on a 3×3 grid.
Prioritise by risk level, then by effort (quick wins first).

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
The instructor demonstrates how to think through one fictional business out loud, using the running example.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
The whiteboard with the 3×3 risk grid drawn.
The running example written as a short profile (below), or read aloud.
A blank findings table drawn large on the board.
The example profile (Adaeze Fashion, Port Harcourt):
Adaeze runs a small fashion shop on a busy street in Port Harcourt. She uses one Android phone for everything: bank app,
WhatsApp business, and taking mobile-money payments by POS. Her assistant Chidi also uses the same phone when
Adaeze travels to buy materials. The phone has no screen lock. Adaeze's email password is adaeze123 , which she also uses
for her Instagram and her bank app. MFA is not switched on. The shop's Wi-Fi password is 12345678 and is written on a
paper on the counter. There is no backup of her customer list; it lives only in her WhatsApp. She has two staff and has
never trained them about fake bank alerts. Windows laptop in the back office runs an old version of Windows that has not
updated in months; Windows Defender is switched off because "it was slowing the computer."
G.2 Demonstration — thinking aloud (about 8 minutes)
Say: "Watch how I move through the eight rooms and write each finding in the table. I am not touching anything — this is all
on paper."
Walk the board, room by room:
1. Accounts/passwords: Finding — "Same weak password ( adaeze123 ) used for email, Instagram and bank." Move to
likelihood/impact.
Ask the class: "How likely is this to be used? How bad is it?" Guide them to High × High = High (weak, reused, and it
exposes money).
2. MFA: Finding — "MFA not switched on for bank or email." → High likelihood (weak password already), High impact →
High.
3. Device updates: Finding — "Back-office Windows not updated for months; Defender off." → Medium likelihood, High
impact (records + money) → High.
4. Antivirus/firewall: Folded into Room 3 finding above (Defender off).
5. Wi-Fi: Finding — "Weak Wi-Fi password 12345678 , written on the counter." → High likelihood, High impact (untrusted
devices reach business devices) → High.
6. Backups: Finding — "Customer list only in WhatsApp; no backup." → High likelihood (phones get stolen), High impact
(list lost forever) → High.
7. Staff awareness: Finding — "No training on fake alerts for two staff." → Medium likelihood, Medium impact → Medium.
8. Data handling: Finding — "Phone with bank app has no screen lock, shared with assistant." → High likelihood, High
impact → High.
Then draw the prioritised list on the board:
1. Put a screen lock on the phone (High, free, instant).
2. Change the bank/email/Instagram passwords to unique strong ones + switch on MFA (High, free, same day).
3. Change the Wi-Fi password to a strong one and remove the card from the counter (High, free).
4. Switch Windows Defender back on and update the laptop (High, free).
5. Start a backup of the customer list to Google Drive (High, near-free).
6. Train the two staff (Medium, cheap, next week).
What to watch for / say: "Notice I found the same problem in more than one room? The weak password shows up in accounts,
MFA and the bank app. That is normal — one weakness often creates others. Write each one where it belongs, and it will be
obvious how they connect."
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Which here is a quick win — High risk and free?" (Screen lock, MFA, Wi-Fi password.)
"Which needs a small cost or planning?" (Training, and buying storage for backups if needed.)
"Have I changed anything on Adaeze's phone?" (No — it is all on paper. That is the point.)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chidi runs a phone-repair kiosk near a market in Port Harcourt. He repairs customers' phones and, to test them, he
sometimes logs into customers' WhatsApp and bank apps "just to make sure the app opens." He keeps a notebook of every
customer's name, phone number and sometimes their screen-lock PIN, "in case they come back and forget." He uses the same
password for his own email and his POS terminal. One day he asks you, a CEA learner, to "check if his shop is safe."
Relevant facts.
Chidi handles other people's sensitive accounts as part of his daily work.
He stores customers' PINs in a notebook.
He reuses one password across his own accounts.
He has permission to repair the phones — but not to browse customers' private accounts.
Decision point. Before you even start assessing, what is wrong here, and what does scope and authorisation require you to say?
Possible approaches.
1. Just start assessing the shop's computers as asked, ignoring the phone-login habit — it is "not your business."
2. Point out the ethics problem: logging into customers' private apps is outside the permission customers gave (they gave the
phone for repair, not their WhatsApp and bank). Then, with Chidi's permission, add "handling of customer accounts and
data" to the scope and assess it too.
3. Refuse to help at all.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. The professional response is to name the problem kindly and correctly.
Customers authorised repairing the phone, not reading their WhatsApp. That is over-stepping scope even though the phone
was handed over. The notebook of PINs is also a serious data-handling finding. With Chidi's permission, you assess and record:
(a) customer account browsing — High risk (breaks trust and the law); (b) PIN notebook — High risk; (c) reused password —
High risk; and recommend fixes: stop logging into customers' accounts, test apps with a test account, delete the PIN notebook,
and use unique passwords.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is easy but leaves a serious ethical and legal problem unspoken — and a real assessor must report what they
find within scope.
Approach 2 is the professional route: it respects the customer, protects Chidi (he could be sued or arrested), and still helps
him.
Approach 3 abandons a client who needs honest guidance. Marking the problem is more useful than walking away.
Professional connection. Junior IT support and consultants meet this exact situation constantly — a person with good
intentions and bad habits. The skill is to report facts neutrally ("customer accounts were accessed during repairs") and
recommend a fix, not to insult the owner. Naming a finding is not the same as accusing a person; it is the job.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
K.1 "I rated everything High"
Symptoms: The findings table is a wall of High; prioritising becomes impossible. Likely causes: (a) The learner forgot that some
items are already good. (b) They are confusing "important" with "dangerous." How to investigate: Go back through the eight
rooms and ask, "Is this area already handled well?" Write those as Low/satisfactory. Corrective steps: Re-rate using the 3×3
grid. Force yourself to give a likelihood and an impact reason; when you do, some items will fall to Medium or Low. How to
confirm resolved: The table now contains a mix of High, Medium and Low. Prevention: Always hunt for at least one
satisfactory finding; a good assessment reports strengths too.
K.2 "High" with no reason
Symptoms: Risk Level column filled, Reason column empty or vague ("it is bad"). Likely cause: The learner jumped to a feeling
instead of a judgement. Corrective steps: For each finding, write two short phrases: "Likelihood is ___ because ___; Impact is ___
because ___." If you cannot, re-check your facts. Prevention: Rule of the course — no reason, no rating.
K.3 Assessing without permission
Symptoms: Learner wants to "just check" a school's or a friend's phone/website. Likely cause: Enthusiasm without
understanding the ethics rule yet. Corrective steps: Stop immediately. Remind them: only your own systems, or systems with
written permission. In this course, only the fictional paper businesses. Prevention: Repeat the authorisation checklist before
every practical. The permission question is always first.
K.4 "Fixing" during the assessment
Symptoms: Learner changes a setting on the vignette's "business" or a real device while assessing. Likely cause: Eagerness to
help. Corrective steps: Write it as a finding instead ("Recommended fix: change the password"). Restore anything changed
immediately if it was a real device with permission. Prevention: Repeat the rule: assessors look and record; they do not change.
Fixes happen in Session 8's improvement plan.
K.5 Confusing assessment with penetration testing
Symptoms: Learner asks, "Should I try to log in with a wrong password to test it?" Likely cause: Blending the two activities.
Corrective steps: Clarify: trying to break in is penetration testing, which is not this course and not this activity. We record
"passwords are weak" from what we observe/are told; we do not attack. Prevention: Keep the three-row comparison table (F.2)
visible on the wall.
K.6 Scope creep
Symptoms: The assessment quietly grows to cover areas the owner did not approve. Likely cause: Curiosity. Corrective steps:
Return to the written scope list; if something important is outside scope, ask the owner in writing to add it. Prevention: Re-
read the scope at the start of every assessment; treat it as a fence.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: In every case, a "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check — e.g., you
confirmed a password is reused only when the owner or evidence shows it. Teach learners to confirm before writing a finding.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. In one sentence, what is a security assessment?
Q2. Multiple choice. What is the difference between a penetration test and hacking? a) There is no difference. b) A penetration
test has written permission; hacking does not. c) Hacking is done by professionals; a pen test by criminals. d) A pen test always
damages the system.
Q3. True or false, with reason. "An assessment should change weak settings as it finds them, so the business is fixed faster."
True / False. Why?
Q4. List and explain. Name four of the eight rooms in the assessment checklist.
Q5. Risk rating. A shop's Wi-Fi uses the factory default password that anyone can look up online, and the Wi-Fi also connects
to the computer that holds customer payments. Rate the likelihood and impact, then give the overall risk level, with a reason.
Q6. Prioritisation. You have two High-risk findings: (A) MFA is switched off (free to fix, 5 minutes), and (B) there is no off-site
backup (needs a small purchase and planning). Which do you fix first, and why?
Q7. Ethics scenario. A friend says, "Just quickly test my school's website to see if it is safe." What do you say and do?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. A security assessment is an organised, permission-based look at how a business uses its devices, accounts and habits, to
find weaknesses, rate their risk, and recommend improvements. (Accept any answer with the ideas of permission, finding
weaknesses, and recommending fixes.)
A2. (b) A penetration test has written permission; hacking does not.
A3. False. An assessment looks and records; it does not change settings. Changing things destroys evidence, may break the
business, and goes beyond the assessor's role. Fixes belong in the improvement plan (Session 8), done with the owner's
agreement.
A4. Any four of: accounts/passwords; MFA; device updates; antivirus/firewall; Wi-Fi/network; backups; staff awareness
(phishing); data handling. Each should have a short correct description, e.g. "MFA — is a second lock switched on for
important accounts?"
A5. Likelihood High (the default password is publicly known and easy to try); Impact High (the Wi-Fi reaches a computer
holding customer payments). Overall High risk. Reason: easy to exploit and very damaging.
A6. Fix A first. Both are High risk, but A is free and completes in minutes ("quick win"), while B needs money and planning. The
rule is: same risk level → do the easier/cheaper fix first, and plan the harder one alongside it.
A7. Say no. You do not have written permission from the school, so you may not assess or test it — not even "quickly" or "for
their own good." Explain that with the school's written permission and a defined scope, a qualified person could do it properly;
but without permission it could be illegal and could get both of you in trouble. Offer to help your friend assess their own laptop
or phone instead, since they own it.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that a security assessment is a permission-based health check-up for a business, not
a break-in. You learned that written permission and scope come before anything else, and that we only assess our own
systems or the fictional businesses in this course. You built an eight-room checklist, learned to gather evidence by looking and
asking without changing anything, and learned to rate risk as likelihood × impact on a simple 3×3 grid. Finally, you
practised prioritising findings and writing a findings table with clear reasons.
Five important takeaways:
1. A security assessment needs written permission and a clear scope — always.
2. Defensive assessment ≠ hacking ≠ penetration testing. Permission is the dividing line.
3. The eight rooms: accounts/passwords, MFA, updates, antivirus/firewall, Wi-Fi, backups, staff awareness, data handling.
4. Risk = likelihood × impact, mapped to Low / Medium / High.
5. Prioritise by risk, then by effort — quick wins first.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I explain the three activities (assessment, pen test, hacking) and what separates them?
Can I list all eight rooms from memory?
Can I rate a finding as Low/Medium/High and give a reason?
Can I write a findings table that a business owner would understand?
Connection to the next session: A findings table tells a business what is wrong, but not what to do about it or in what order
over time. In Session 8 you turn your findings into a prioritised improvement plan — quick wins and longer projects, with
clear cost-aware recommendations — and you learn what to do in the first hour of a real incident through a tabletop drill.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner states the permission rule unprompted before starting the exercise.
Learner's findings table shows a mix of risk levels, not all High.
Every risk level is backed by a likelihood-and-impact reason.
Learner identifies at least one satisfactory finding.
Learner refuses, correctly, when given an out-of-scope/unauthorised temptation.
Learner prioritises by risk and effort, not just by feeling.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Assessment = hacking." → Re-show the three-row table; permission is the difference.
"Nothing bad has happened, so risk is Low." → Risk = likelihood × impact, not history.
"I should fix things as I find them." → Assessors record; fixes happen later, with permission.
"Everything is High, so everything is priority one." → Hunt for good findings; use effort to order.
"Scope is just what the owner loosely said." → Scope must be written, with included and excluded items.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Sort-the-findings drill: give ten finding cards and a 3×3 grid; ask the learner to place each.
2. Two-sentence drill: for each finding, force the sentence "Likelihood is ___ because ___; impact is ___ because ___."
3. Permission drill: show five short scenarios (own laptop, friend's phone, a signed client, a school website, a church group)
and ask "allowed or not, and why?"
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a pre-filled eight-room checklist with three rooms already done and a partly filled findings
table; the learner completes the rest.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): add a likelihood number (1–3) and impact number (1–3), multiply them, and derive the level
arithmetically; also propose one free fix and one paid fix for each High finding.
Timing guidance: If the room is slow, shorten the case study (Section J) to a 3-minute mention and protect the full guided and
independent exercise time — the findings table is the priority. If the room is fast, give vignette card 2 in Section I as a bonus.
