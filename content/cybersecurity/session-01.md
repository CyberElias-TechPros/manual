# The Threat Landscape and a Security Mindset

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Hygiene (Stop–Think–Verify)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with cybersecurity)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Basic computer use — using a mouse and keyboard, opening a browser, creating and saving a file. No security knowledge assumed. If a learner struggles with the mouse/keyboard, pair them with a confident learner for the first exercise.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. There is no previous cybersecurity session. It connects to everyday life: nearly every learner has received a scam SMS or a strange WhatsApp message.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged in; a projector connected; the four fictional scenario cards (Section G) prepared as printed sheets or projected; the "Red-Flag Record Sheet" (Section H) printed per learner; decide how you will show the fake bank SMS (as a printed card or a simple slide — never send a real message). Confirm the class understands this is a defensive course with an ethics agreement to sign. Instructor note on tone: This session must feel empowering, not frightening. Learners should leave feeling "I can spot these tricks," not "the internet is a terrifying place I cannot survive." Keep the mood calm, practical and confident.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define cybersecurity in their own words and explain why it matters for ordinary people and small businesses.
2. Explain and apply the "Stop–Think–Verify" habit before clicking, paying, replying or sharing.
3. Name and describe the main malware families — virus, worm, trojan, spyware and ransomware — and give one plain
example of each.
4. Define social engineering and phishing, and explain how they trick people (psychology, not just technology).
5. Describe common Nigerian scam and fraud patterns — fake bank SMS, WhatsApp impersonation, fake job offers, fake
investment schemes — and list their usual red flags.
6. Distinguish correctly between the four core terms: threat, vulnerability, exploit and risk.
7. Explain why small businesses are attractive targets, using at least two reasons.
8. Identify red flags in five fictional scenarios and recommend the safe response for each, scoring at least 4 out of 5.

<!-- kind:materials -->

One computer per learner (academy desktops). A shared computer between two learners is fine; pair them so one reads the
scenario and one writes the verdict, then swap.
Instructor computer connected to a projector or large screen.
Software
A web browser (Chrome). This session uses no special software — it is a think-and-discuss session with checking activities.

None required this session. (Gmail accounts are needed for S2 and S4 — remind learners now.)

the red flags? What is the safe action?
Ethics & Scope agreement (Section 15 of the Design Document) — printed for signature.
Worksheets
Worksheet 1.1 — "Spot the Red Flag" (the record sheet above).
Worksheet 1.2 — "Threat, Vulnerability, Exploit, Risk" matching exercise (in the learner workbook).
Tools
None beyond a browser and paper. A simple slide with the four terms is helpful.
Internet
Not required. If internet is available, the instructor may show a genuine (harmless, informational) example of a scam-

Any computer with a browser works. If a learner has only a phone, pair them with a desktop user and let them participate in

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Put a simple slide or card on the screen showing a fictional text message:
"Dear Customer, your account has been flagged for suspicious activity. To avoid suspension, confirm your BVN and ATM
PIN at this link immediately: bank-secure-verify.example. Thank you — First National Bank."
(This is a made-up message for teaching. The domain .example is a reserved name used in teaching, not a real website.)
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Before we begin — hands up if you have ever received a message like this one. … Almost everyone. Now,
hands up if you nearly believed one. … Good. Today you are going to learn exactly why that message is a trap, and how to
spot the next one in five seconds.
Look at the message again. It says 'Dear Customer' — not your name. It creates fear: 'suspension'. It asks for your BVN
and ATM PIN — things a real bank will never ask for. And it pushes you to click immediately. That is not a bank. That is a
trap wearing a bank's clothes."
Expected learner response: Learners will nod, share quick personal stories, or say "I get these all the time" or "My aunty was
almost caught." Accept all answers warmly. Do not ask anyone to reveal personal financial details. Steer the discussion to how
the trick works, not to anyone's loss.
Then set the tone: Say clearly:
"This course is defensive. We learn to protect, not to attack. We will never try to trick anyone or access a system we do not
own. By the end of these three weeks, you will be the person your family and your shop calls when something looks
suspicious."
Why this opener works: It uses a message almost every Nigerian has seen, it produces instant recognition ("that happened to
me"), and it frames the whole course as protection and competence rather than fear.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a quick oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is the difference between a web browser and a search engine? Give one example of each."
Expected answer: A browser is the program that displays web pages (Chrome, Edge); a search engine is a website you use
inside a browser to find things (Google, Bing).
Common gap: learners use the words loosely. Clarify: "Chrome is the car; Google is the road you drive on."
Question 2: "If a website address starts with https:// and shows a padlock, what does that tell you?"
Expected answer: The connection is encrypted — the information travelling is scrambled so others cannot easily read it.
(The full lesson on what a padlock does not mean is in S3.)
Common gap: many believe "padlock = safe website." Say: "Remember that. We will come back in Session 3 and show you
why that belief is only half true."
Question 3: "When you save a file on a Windows computer, where does it normally go, and how do you find it again?"
Expected answer: Usually the Documents or Downloads folder; you find it with File Explorer.
Common gap: some learners only know the Desktop. Show File Explorer briefly on the projector.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already use the tools — the browser, files, the internet. Today we do not add a new tool. We add a new way of
thinking that keeps you safe while you use the tools you know. It is called a security mindset, and it starts with three
words: Stop. Think. Verify."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes on a short browser tour before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What cybersecurity actually is
The idea. Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, phones, accounts, networks and information from being
stolen, damaged or misused.
Plain-English definition. Cybersecurity is everything we do to keep our devices and data safe — and to keep the wrong people
out.
Why it exists. Money, private information and important records now live on computers and phones. Banks, businesses,
churches, schools and families all depend on digital tools. Wherever there is something valuable, someone will try to take it.
Cybersecurity is the lock on the door.
What it protects. Three things again and again:
Confidentiality — keeping private things private (your PIN, your customers' records).
Integrity — keeping information correct and unaltered (your account balance, your sales records).
Availability — keeping your systems working when you need them (your shop can still take orders).
Real-life comparison. Think of a house. You have a door lock (passwords), a window grill (firewall), a security light (updates),
and neighbours who know you (good habits). No single one of these makes a house perfectly safe — but together, they make
it hard for a thief, who then moves to the easier house next door. Cybersecurity is the same: we are not trying to be perfect; we
are trying to be too difficult to bother with.
Important limitation / misconception. Many people think cybersecurity is only for big companies or "computer people." This
is false. A market trader's WhatsApp, a church's bank app, a student's Gmail — all need the same basic protection. Small and
ordinary can still be a target.
F.2 The security mindset: Stop – Think – Verify
The idea. The single most powerful defence is not software. It is a habit of mind.
Plain-English definition. Before you click a link, open an attachment, send money, share a code, or reply to a message, you
stop, you think, and you verify.
STOP — Do not rush. Scammers depend on speed and urgency. Buying five seconds of calm defeats most attacks.
THINK — Ask: Who sent this? Why are they asking? What are they asking for? Is this normal?
VERIFY — Check through a different, trusted channel. Call the bank using the number on your card. Call your "uncle" on
a number you already have. Open the official app yourself instead of clicking the link.
Why it works. Almost every scam relies on you acting fast and without checking. The moment you slow down and verify, the
trap falls apart.
Real-life comparison. It is exactly what a careful person does at the market. If a stranger rushes up to you saying "quick, quick,
give me ₦20,000, your brother sent me," you would stop, think, and call your brother. Do the same online.
Misconception to correct. "I am too clever to be tricked." Everyone can be tricked when they are tired, busy, scared or tempted.
The mindset protects the intelligent as much as anyone, because it does not rely on being clever — it relies on a habit.
F.3 The malware family
The idea. Malware is short for "malicious software" — any program written to harm, spy on, or take control of a device. It is a
family with several members.
Plain-English definition. Malware is software that does something bad on purpose. Like a family, the members have different
behaviours but the same surname.

F.4 Social engineering — hacking the person, not the computer
The idea. Social engineering is tricking a person into giving away information or access. Instead of breaking the computer, the
attacker breaks the human.
Plain-English definition. Social engineering is a lie told with confidence, aimed at making you do something against your own
interest.
Why it works — the five buttons attackers press:
Button What it means Scam example
Urgency "Act now or lose it." "Your account will be blocked today."
Button What it means Scam example
Authority "I am the bank / the government / the boss." "This is your bank's security department."
Fear Scares you into reacting. "You are in trouble with the police."
Greed Tempts you with easy money. "You have won ₦500,000!"
Helpfulness Exploits your kindness. "Please send me your code, I am stranded."
Phishing. Phishing is the most common form of social engineering: a fake message (email, SMS or WhatsApp) that pretends to
be from someone you trust — your bank, a delivery company, a friend — in order to steal a password, a code, or money. It
"fishes" for your secrets with tempting bait.
Real-life comparison. Social engineering is like a burglar who dresses as a uniformed delivery man and says, "You have a parcel
— just let me in to sign it." The door was locked; the burglar simply asked you to open it.
Misconception to correct. "Phishing is only by email." No — phishing arrives by SMS ("smishing"), by phone call ("vishing"),
and by WhatsApp. The channel changes; the trick stays the same.
F.5 Common Nigerian attack patterns
These are everyday patterns learners will recognise. All names and details here are illustrative and fictional.
1. Fake bank SMS / email. "Your account has been suspended. Click here to verify." The link goes to a fake login page that
steals your details.
Red flags: generic greeting, a link, urgency, asking for PIN/BVN/OTP.
2. WhatsApp impersonation. Someone copies a relative's or friend's profile picture and name, then messages: "I am in
trouble, send ₦50,000 fast."
Red flags: a new number, rush, an unusual request for money, refusal to talk on a call.
3. Fake job offers. "You have been offered a job. Pay ₦10,000 for your uniform/registration." The real job does not exist.
Red flags: a real job never asks you to pay first.
4. Fake investment schemes. "Double your money in one week — guaranteed." Early payers are paid with later payers'
money, then it collapses.
Red flags: "guaranteed" high returns, pressure to recruit friends, no registered company.
Why small businesses are targeted. Small businesses are attractive because:
They often handle real money and customer data but have little protection.
They trust people easily (regular customers, "new suppliers").
They may have one shared account or device, so one mistake affects everything.
Their owner is usually too busy to stop and check — perfect for urgency tricks.
Say this to the class:
"The scammer is not smarter than you. The scammer is patient. They send the same message to thousands of people and
wait for one tired person to click. Your job is simply not to be that tired person — and Stop–Think–Verify makes sure of
it."
F.6 Four words that professionals use correctly: threat, vulnerability, exploit, risk
Learners must not confuse these. Use the locked-house analogy.
Term Plain-English meaning House analogy
Threat Anything that could cause harm. A burglar who might come.
Vulnerability A weakness that lets harm happen. The open window the burglar could use.
Term Plain-English meaning House analogy
Exploit The method used to take advantage of the
weakness.
The ladder the burglar uses to reach the open window.

vulnerability.

The relationship (say it slowly):
"A threat reaches a vulnerability through an exploit, and that produces risk."
Example in one sentence: "A scam message (threat) can trick an untrained employee (vulnerability) by pretending to be the
boss (the exploit method), and the risk is that the shop loses ₦300,000."
Misconception to correct. "Risk" is not the same as "threat." A threat with no vulnerability creates little risk. We reduce risk by
removing vulnerabilities — that is exactly what this course teaches.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Cybersecurity protects the confidentiality, integrity and availability of our data and devices.
The core habit is Stop – Think – Verify.
Malware is a family: virus, worm, trojan, spyware, ransomware.
Social engineering attacks the person; phishing is its most common form.
Common Nigerian patterns: fake bank messages, WhatsApp impersonation, fake jobs, fake investments.
Threat / vulnerability / exploit / risk are different words with different jobs.
Small businesses are targeted because they hold value with little protection and are often too busy to check.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Walk the class through five fictional scenarios on the projector, thinking aloud so learners see how a trained eye scans a
message. All scenarios are invented for teaching. No real messages are opened.
Setup: Display each scenario card one at a time (printed or on a slide). For each, ask the class to call out what they notice before
you explain. Write their observations on the board, then reveal the analysis.
Scenario 1 — The fake bank SMS
"GTB CUSTOMER: Urgent! Your account will be blocked in 2 hours. Re-activate now by confirming your card PIN and OTP at
gtb-reactivate.example."
Think aloud: "Bank name spelled as the bank might shorten it, but the domain is gtb-reactivate.example — not the bank's
real address. It asks for my PIN and OTP — no real bank ever does this. It uses a 2-hour deadline."
Type of threat: Phishing (smishing) / social engineering.
Safe action: Delete; never click; if worried, open the bank's official app or call the number on your card.
Scenario 2 — WhatsApp impersonation
A message from a number you do not recognise, using your cousin's photo and name: "Bro, I lost my phone and this is my
new number. I need ₦80,000 urgently for an emergency. Please send to this account."
Think aloud: "The name and photo can be copied by anyone. A 'new number' plus an urgent money request plus a
different bank account are classic red flags. If it were really my cousin, I could call the old number or our family to confirm."
Type: Social engineering / impersonation / fraud.
Safe action: Do not send money. Call the person on a number you already trust. Warn relatives.
Scenario 3 — Fake job offer
"Congratulations! You have been selected for a work-from-home job paying ₦150,000 monthly. To secure your position,
pay a ₦10,000 registration fee today."
Think aloud: "Real employers pay you; they do not ask you to pay them. 'Secure your position' creates urgency and greed.
No company details, no interview."
Type: Scam / fraud.
Safe action: Do not pay. Verify the company independently. Report to the platform.
Scenario 4 — The USB drive found in a car park
You find a USB flash drive labelled "SALARY 2026" near the office gate. A colleague says, "Let's check whose it is — plug it
in."
Think aloud: "This is a classic trojan delivery. A strange USB could auto-run harmful software or contain a hidden malicious
file. The label is bait."
Type: Malware delivery (trojan/virus).
Safe action: Do not plug unknown drives into any computer. Hand it to security or IT.
Scenario 5 — The "your PC is infected" pop-up
While browsing, a full-screen page appears with a loud alarm: "WARNING! Your computer is infected with 5 viruses! Call
this number or click to clean now."
Think aloud: "A real security tool does not lock your whole screen and shout. This is 'scareware' — a fake alert designed to
make you call a fake 'support' line or download more malware."
Type: Scam / scareware / malware trap.
Safe action: Do not call, do not click. Close the tab (or restart the browser). Run a real scan with Windows Defender.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Which of the five buttons (urgency, authority, fear, greed, helpfulness) is being pressed here?"
"What is the one action that would keep you safe in each case?"
"Which of these could reach your shop or your church WhatsApp group?"
Expected result: Learners begin calling out red flags on their own by Scenario 3 or 4. Praise that — it is the whole point of the
session.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner completes a Red-Flag Record Sheet for five scenarios,
naming the threat type, listing red flags, and choosing the safe action.
Starting materials: A printed Red-Flag Record Sheet per learner, and the five scenarios (reuse the Section G scenarios, or give
slightly different ones so learners must think). A pen. No computer needed — this is an analysis exercise (a deliberate choice:
the brain is the tool).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners work; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your name at the top of the sheet. Remember: everything here is fictional. Do not write any real password, PIN or
personal number anywhere on this sheet.
2. Look at Scenario 1. Read it twice.
3. Fill in Column A — Threat type (malware family, phishing, social engineering, scam, etc.).
4. Fill in Column B — Red flags (list at least two things that look wrong).
5. Fill in Column C — Safe action (the one thing you would do).
6. Pause and predict: Before moving on, cover Column C with your hand and ask yourself: "If I did nothing, what would go
wrong?" Then write the safe action.
7. Repeat steps 2–6 for all five scenarios.
8. When finished, compare with the person beside you. If you disagree on a threat type, explain your reason.

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task: Create a short "Family Safety Advice Card" — a mini leaflet that explains one common Nigerian scam and how to avoid
it, written for a family member who is not in this class. You choose the audience (e.g., your grandmother, a younger sibling, a
shop owner).
Scenario: CEA has asked you to help teach your own family. You must explain, in your own simple words, one threat — for
example, fake bank SMS or WhatsApp impersonation — and give three clear safety steps.
Requirements:
1. Choose one threat from today's session.
2. In 3–5 sentences, explain in plain language how the scam works (do not copy the lecture wording).
3. Give three clear safety steps a person can follow.
4. Add a strong one-line motto (you may borrow "Stop – Think – Verify" or write your own).
5. Keep it on one page, readable by a non-expert.
Constraints:
Do not include any real phone number, account number, link or person's name.
Do not write instructions that could help anyone scam others.
Use simple English; a real family member should be able to follow it.
Time allowance: 15 minutes.
Success criteria:
Names one threat clearly.
Explains how the scam tricks people (mentions at least one "button" — urgency, fear, greed, etc.).
Gives three practical, safe steps.
Reads clearly to a non-expert and contains no real personal data.
Hints available if stuck:
Hint 1: Start with "Have you ever seen a message that says…?" and describe it.
Hint 2: The three steps can be: Stop and do not click; Check with the real bank/person; Report and delete.
Hint 3: End with your motto so it is memorable.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. (This is a realistic but non-identifying account, typical of small-business incidents. Names and details are illustrative;
no real victim or organisation is described.)
A small tailoring shop in Port Harcourt — let us call it a family tailoring business — handled orders by WhatsApp and did its
accounts on one shared laptop. The owner, "Mama C," received an SMS that appeared to come from her bank: "Your account is
about to be deactivated. Confirm your details to keep receiving payments." It was late in the evening, she was tired, and the
shop's POS payments were her livelihood.
Relevant facts.
The message used the bank's real name and colours and a fake but convincing web page.
It asked her to type her card details and the OTP that arrived seconds later.
The shop used the same password for the laptop, the email and the business Facebook page.
Decision point. She had seconds to decide: click and "secure" her account, or stop and check.
Possible approaches.
1. Click immediately to avoid deactivation — the scammer's goal.
2. Ignore it completely and hope for the best — safer, but does not confirm the account.
3. Stop, Think, Verify — do not click; open the bank's official app; call the number on her card; ask a family member.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Verifying through a trusted, separate channel is the professional response. Had she
already clicked, the right immediate steps would be: call the bank to freeze the card, change the reused password everywhere it
was used, and report the incident.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: fast, but hands the scammer everything — catastrophic.
Approach 2: avoids this scam but leaves her unsure; she may still panic later and click a similar one.
Approach 3: takes a few extra minutes, but resolves both the fear and the risk, and teaches the whole family.
Professional connection. This is exactly what security-aware staff do every day: verify through a second channel before
acting on any urgent request. It is the single most transferable habit in the whole course.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Confusing phishing with malware
Symptoms: Learner calls a fake bank SMS "malware." Likely cause: The learner has not separated tricking a person from harmful
software. How to investigate: Ask: "Was the attacker attacking a computer, or tricking a person?" Safe corrective steps: Re-
explain: phishing/social engineering attacks the person; malware is software that harms the device. They can be combined but
are different categories. How to confirm resolved: Learner classifies a message and a file correctly and gives a reason. How to
prevent recurrence: Always ask "person or program?" first.
K.2 Thinking "I will never be tricked"
Symptoms: Learner dismisses the scenarios as "obvious." Likely cause: Overconfidence; they have not yet felt the
urgency/temptation. How to investigate: Ask them to read a scenario as if it arrived at 11 p.m. after a long day. Safe
corrective steps: Explain that scams target tiredness and emotion, not intelligence. How to confirm resolved: Learner gives a
reason for the mindset ("because anyone can be rushed"). How to prevent recurrence: Repeat the "anyone can be tricked"
message every week.
K.3 Wanting to "test" or open a suspicious link
Symptoms: Learner says "let me just click it and see." Likely cause: Curiosity; lack of awareness of the ethics and the risk. How
to investigate: This is a stop-and-correct moment, not an investigation. Safe corrective steps: Firmly remind the class: we
never open real suspicious links, and never test systems we do not own. Use only fictional samples. How to confirm resolved:
Learner restates the Ethics rule correctly. How to prevent recurrence: Re-read the Ethics & Scope statement at the start of any
hands-on session.
K.4 Mixing up threat, vulnerability, exploit and risk
Symptoms: "The threat is that the window is open." Likely cause: The four words sound interchangeable to a beginner. How to
investigate: Ask the learner to match each word to the house analogy. Safe corrective steps: Re-draw the burglar / open
window / ladder / chance table. How to confirm resolved: Learner can place all four words correctly on one example. How to
prevent recurrence: Use the four words in every later session.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can point to the
exact word in the message that is the red flag). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that cybersecurity is about protecting our money, data and devices, and that the
strongest defence starts with a habit: Stop – Think – Verify. You met the malware family — virus, worm, trojan, spyware,
ransomware — and learned that social engineering tricks people, with phishing as its most common form. You studied familiar
Nigerian patterns: fake bank messages, WhatsApp impersonation, fake jobs and fake investments, and you learned why small
businesses are targets. Finally, you learned to tell apart a threat, a vulnerability, an exploit and a risk, and you practised
spotting red flags in five scenarios.
Five important takeaways:
1. Cybersecurity protects confidentiality, integrity and availability — and ordinary people need it, not just big companies.
2. Stop – Think – Verify before you click, pay, reply or share.
3. Malware is a family: virus, worm, trojan, spyware, ransomware.
4. Social engineering attacks the person; phishing is its most common form, and it comes by email, SMS and WhatsApp.
5. Threat, vulnerability, exploit and risk are different words — learn them properly.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner names threat types correctly and gives a reason, not just a word.
Learner applies Stop–Think–Verify spontaneously in discussion ("I would call the bank first").
Learner distinguishes phishing (person) from malware (software).
Learner uses the four risk words correctly and points to the exact red flag in a message.
Learner warns others and stays calm rather than panicking.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Cybersecurity is only for big companies / tech people." → Ordinary users and small shops are common targets.
"A padlock means the site is safe." → Only that the connection is encrypted; the site can still be dishonest (full lesson in S3).
"I am too clever to be tricked." → Scams target tiredness and emotion, not intelligence.
"Phishing only comes by email." → It also arrives by SMS, WhatsApp and phone call.
"Antivirus solves everything." → It is one layer; habits, updates and backups matter too.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Matching drill: give cards with the four risk words and their meanings; learner matches and explains one.
2. Red-flag hunt: give a short fake message and ask the learner to underline every red flag and count them.
3. Button identification: show three scams and ask which "button" (urgency, fear, greed, authority, helpfulness) each presses.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed Red-Flag Record Sheet (threat type already given for scenarios 1 and 2)
and word-bank hints.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write an original fictional scam scenario of their own, label its threat type, list red
flags, and add the safe action — then swap it with a partner to solve. (Still fictional; never real.)
Timing guidance: If the room is slow, shorten the lecture's Section F.5 examples and protect the guided practical (Section H) —
the ability to spot red flags is the priority. If the room is fast, extend the case study discussion and let learners present their
Family Safety Cards.
