# Safe Browsing, Downloads and Recognising Scams

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Hygiene (Stop–Think–Verify)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 (threats, Stop–Think–Verify) and Session 2 (passwords, MFA, password managers). Basic browsing: opening Chrome, typing a web address, using search.

**Connection to previous work:** Your accounts are now well protected. But most attacks arrive as a link, a download or a scary pop-up. Today you learn to look at a web address, a download source and a warning, and judge whether they are safe — protecting yourself even when the site is trying to trick you.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged in; Chrome open; internet available. Prepare the fictional URL cards (Section G/H) and the Verdict Sheet (printed per learner). Have the fictional demo website ready (a simple local HTML page is ideal, so no real site is visited). Test the projector. Read the ethics reminder: all links and samples are fictional or official/benign; never open a real suspicious link. Instructor safety note: This session analyses web addresses. We will never click a suspicious or unknown link to "see what happens." We inspect, compare and reason — using printed cards, a local demo page, or clearly safe official sites only.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what HTTPS/TLS does — it encrypts the connection between the browser and the website — and correctly state
what the padlock does and does not mean.
2. Inspect a web address (URL) and identify the domain, the subdomain and the path, and spot suspicious elements
(misspellings, extra words, unusual endings, numbers).
3. Recognise suspicious links, shortened URLs and urgency tricks, and decide whether a link is safe to click.
4. Choose safe download sources, avoid cracked/warez software, and check file types before opening.
5. Perform basic browser safety habits: keep the browser updated, manage extensions, and handle pop-up scareware
without clicking.
6. Explain the risks of QR codes and public Wi-Fi, and outline safe behaviour for each.
7. Verify a website before entering personal or payment details.
8. Correctly classify at least 4 of 5 example web addresses as safe or unsafe, with a reason.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Put two web addresses on the screen, side by side:
https://www.mybank.com.ng (fictional example — the real bank's own address)
```text
https://mybank-secure-login.example.ng
```
What the instructor says:
"Which of these two is your real bank? … Look closely. The second one has the word 'mybank' in it — but it also has
'secure-login', and the ending is .example.ng , not the bank's real name. Scammers use addresses that contain a trusted
name to make you feel safe.
And here is the shock for many people: both might show a padlock. The padlock does not mean the site is honest. It only
means the connection is scrambled. A robber can have a padlock on his own door too.
Today you will learn to read a web address like a professional — where the real name lives in the address, and where the
fakes hide."
Expected learner response: Many learners will say "the second looks safer because it says secure." That is exactly the
misconception to correct. Others may notice the .example.ng . Praise careful eyes.
Then hook with a question: "When a page screams 'Your PC is infected! Click here to clean it!' — is that real security
software?" Take answers. Most will now guess correctly: no. Say:
"By the end of today, you will know in seconds whether a link is worth clicking — and you will never fall for a scary pop-up
again."
Why this opener works: It attacks the widely held "padlock = safe" belief head-on, uses a banking context every learner cares
about, and promises a skill they can use immediately.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a quick oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the three failure modes of a weak password we said are most dangerous?"
Expected answer: guessing/weak patterns, reuse, and breaches. (Length and uniqueness are the fix.)
Common gap: learners focus on symbols. Re-state: length and uniqueness.
Question 2: "What does MFA add, and which is stronger — an authenticator app or an SMS code?"
Expected answer: a second lock; the authenticator app is stronger because it is not tied to your phone number.
Common gap: thinking SMS is safest. Clarify again.
Question 3: "What are the three words of the security mindset?"
Expected answer: Stop – Think – Verify.
Common gap: rushing. Remind them it applies to every link.
Question 4: "If a message tells you to click quickly 'or your account closes in 10 minutes,' what two things is it doing?"
Expected answer: creating urgency and trying to stop you thinking/verifying — a scam pattern.
Common gap: learners assume it must be real if it sounds official. Reinforce verification.
Bridge to today (say this):
"So far we have taught you to protect your accounts. Today we protect the journey — the link you click. In Session 1 you
learned the trick. In Session 2 you locked the door. Today you learn to read the roadsigns so you never walk into the trap at
all."
If more than a third cannot answer Question 1, spend 3 extra minutes reviewing the S2 recap.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 How the web works — a simple picture
The idea. When you type an address and press Enter, your browser (Chrome) talks to a web server — a computer somewhere
that holds the website — and asks it to send the page. The page travels back and appears on your screen.
Plain-English definition. The internet is a network of computers that can send information to each other. A web server is a
computer that stores and delivers websites. Your browser is the program that requests and displays them.
Real-life comparison. Think of ordering food. You (the browser) call a restaurant (the server), place an order, and the food (the
web page) is delivered to you. The address you type is like the restaurant's phone number — if it is wrong, you reach the wrong
place or nobody.
Why this matters for safety. Because your browser is asking a stranger's computer for information, you must be careful which
stranger you are talking to. That is what the address and HTTPS are about.
F.2 Web addresses (URLs) — reading the roadsign
The idea. A URL (Uniform Resource Locator) is the address of a page on the web. Learning to read it is the single most useful
safe-browsing skill.
Plain-English definition. A URL has parts; the most important part is the domain — the actual name of the site.
Take this address apart:
```text
https://shop.example.com.ng/login/page
  │        │        │    │   │
  │        │        │    │   └── path (the specific page)
  │        │        │    └────── top-level domain (country: .ng)
  │        │        └─────────── top-level domain (type: .com)
  │        └──────────────────── domain name (the ACTUAL site)
  └──────────────────────────── protocol (https = encrypted)
```
The key rule: the real name of the site is the part just before the final domain ending — here, example . Everything to the
left (like shop. ) is a subdomain chosen by the site owner, and attackers can put anything there.
Why scammers exploit this. An attacker can create a subdomain that looks trustworthy: mybank.login-secure.example.com — the
domain is example.com , not the bank. People read from left to right and trust the first trusted-looking word. Train yourself to
read the domain, not the decorations.
Red flags in an address:
Misspellings: faceb00k , gooogle , mybank.ng-secure.example .
Trusted name in the wrong place: bank-verify.example.net (the real name should be the domain, not a word in the path or
subdomain).
Extra words of pressure: secure , login , verify , update , urgent .
Strange endings: a .ng business reached at a .xyz or random address.
Long numbers or random characters: pay-secure12345.example .
Shortened links: like bit.example/3xY7 — you cannot see where they lead.
Real-life comparison. A URL is like a street address on an envelope. The domain is the actual building; the subdomain and path
are rooms inside. A scammer may rent a room in a building with a similar name, hoping you deliver the parcel (your password)
to the wrong address.
Misconception to correct: "If the word 'bank' appears in the link, it is the bank." No — the word can appear anywhere. Only
the domain (just before the final ending) is the real site.
F.3 HTTPS, the padlock and what it really means
The idea. HTTPS is the secure version of the web protocol. It encrypts the traffic between your browser and the website so
others on the network cannot easily read it.
Plain-English definition.
HTTP — the connection is not encrypted; information travels in the open.
HTTPS — the connection is encrypted. The "S" stands for Secure.
TLS (Transport Layer Security) is the technology that does the encrypting behind the scenes. When people say
"HTTPS/TLS," they mean this encryption.
The padlock in the address bar is Chrome telling you: "This connection is encrypted."
Why it exists. On a public network (a café, a hotel), someone could otherwise "listen in" and read your data as it travels.
Encryption scrambles it so that even if they capture it, they cannot easily read it.
What HTTPS protects: the journey of your data — it stops eavesdroppers on the network from reading your password or card
details in transit.
What HTTPS does NOT do (this is the crucial lesson):
It does not prove the site is honest. A scam website can also get a padlock.
It does not mean the site is the official one. Look-alike scam sites use HTTPS too.
It does not protect you from a fake download or a virus you install yourself.
It does not mean your device is safe.
Real-life comparison. HTTPS is like a sealed, tinted envelope. The postman cannot read your letter, so it is private in transit.
But if you wrote to the wrong address (a scammer) on purpose, the sealed envelope still reaches the wrong person. Encryption
protects the journey, not the destination.
Say this to the class, slowly:
"A padlock means 'the road is private.' It does not mean 'the shop is honest.' Check the domain first; the padlock is only
the second question."
Misconception to correct: "I only enter details on sites with a padlock, so I am safe." Better rule: check the domain is exactly
right, then confirm HTTPS, then think before typing.
F.4 Spotting unsafe links and scams
The idea. A link can look innocent but lead somewhere dangerous. Learn the patterns.
Common unsafe-link patterns:
Pattern What it looks like Why it is risky
Look-alike domain
```text
gtbank-secure.example
```
Uses a trusted name in the wrong place
Pattern What it looks like Why it is risky
Misspelling amaz0n.example , whatapp.example Tiny change to fool a quick glance
Shortened URL
```text
bit.example/abc123
```
Hides the real destination
Urgency link "Your account closes in 1 hour — click now" Pressure to skip thinking
Unexpected link A "delivery" link when you ordered nothing Bait for a fake login page
Wrong context A bank link in a WhatsApp group Banks do not ask through groups
The safe-reading habit (Stop–Think–Verify for links):
1. STOP — do not click from a message. Never trust the link text; it can say "bank.com" but point anywhere.
2. THINK — "Was I expecting this? Does the domain match the real company exactly?"
3. VERIFY — type the company's known address yourself, or use the official app. Never rely on the link.
Real-life comparison. A link is like a signpost someone planted in your yard reading "This way to the bank." Anyone can plant a
sign. You would rather walk to the bank you know than follow a sign a stranger left.
Misconception to correct: "The link text shows the bank's name, so it is safe." Link text is just a label. The real destination is
hidden in the code. Never judge a link by its label.
F.5 Safe downloading — the software trap
The idea. Many infections happen because the user installs something unsafe. Safe downloading prevents this.
Plain-English definition. Downloading means copying a file from the internet to your computer. If the file is harmful, installing
it can infect your whole device.
Rules for safe downloading:
1. Use official sources. Download from the official website, the official app store, or a trusted, well-known distributor.
2. Avoid cracked or "warez" software. "Free" cracked paid software, key generators and "activators" are a favourite hiding
place for malware. If something expensive is offered free and illegally, ask yourself what the price really is.
3. Check the file type. Be cautious with .exe , .msi , .scr , .bat (Windows programs and scripts) and Office files with
macros ( .docm , .xlsm ). A "video" that is secretly an .exe is a classic trick.
4. Beware bundled extras. Some installers sneak in unwanted toolbars or adware; read each screen and untick extra offers.
5. Verify the address before downloading — the same domain-reading skill from F.2.
6. Let Defender scan it. Keep Microsoft Defender on (next week's topic) and scan downloaded files from untrusted sources
before opening.
Real-life comparison. Downloading from a random site is like buying medicine from a stranger in traffic instead of a proper
pharmacy. It might be genuine — but if it is not, the harm is serious. Buy from the pharmacy you trust.
Misconception to correct: "If my antivirus is on, I can download anything." Not true. New malware can slip past, and you can
be tricked into allowing it. Antivirus is a seatbelt, not permission to drive recklessly.
F.6 Browser safety basics
The idea. Your browser is your gateway to the internet; keep it safe and clean.
Safe browser habits:
Keep Chrome updated. Updates fix known security holes. If Chrome asks to relaunch to update, let it.
Install only trusted extensions. Extensions can read what you do. Fewer is safer. Remove ones you no longer use, and
never add an extension from a pop-up or a random site.
Handle pop-ups and scareware correctly. A website cannot truly scan your computer, and real antivirus does not lock your
whole screen with a loud alarm and a phone number. That is scareware — a fake alert. Close the tab (or the browser) and,
if worried, run a real scan with Windows Defender.
Do not save passwords in a shared browser. On a shared PC, never let Chrome "remember" your password; use your
Use a browser profile/PIN on shared computers so your history is not exposed.
Real-life comparison. Your browser is like your car. Keep it serviced (updates), do not fill it with junk gadgets (extensions), and
do not believe every warning light a stranger installs on your dashboard.
Misconception to correct: "A pop-up told me I have a virus, so I must act now." Web pages cannot scan your PC. The warning
is the attack. Close it.
F.7 QR codes and public Wi-Fi
QR code risks. A QR code is a square barcode you scan with a phone camera to open a link. Because you cannot read the
address before scanning, a malicious QR code (a "quishing" trap) can send you to a fake site. QR codes on stickers in public
places (parking meters, posters, restaurant tables) can be stuck over the real one.
Safe habit: After scanning, read the address that appears before tapping "Open." If it looks wrong, do not proceed. Use
the official app instead.
Public Wi-Fi caution. Public Wi-Fi (café, hotel, airport, market hotspot) is convenient but shared.
Prefer HTTPS sites (still check the domain) so your traffic is encrypted.
Avoid sensitive logins (banking) on public Wi-Fi when possible; use your mobile data for that.
Beware fake hotspots with names like "FreeWiFi" that are set up to capture data.
Turn off file sharing and "automatic connect to open networks" on your device.
Consider a mobile hotspot from your own phone for anything important.
Real-life comparison. Public Wi-Fi is a crowded market: convenient, but everyone can see and bump into you. Keep your
valuables (bank logins) close, not out in the open.
Misconception to correct: "HTTPS makes public Wi-Fi completely safe." It encrypts your traffic to that site, but a fake hotspot
or a malicious site can still trick you. Prefer mobile data for sensitive tasks.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
The internet connects your browser to web servers; the URL is the address, and the domain is the real name of the site.
HTTPS/TLS encrypts the connection; the padlock means "encrypted," not "honest."
Read the domain; watch for misspellings, trusted names in wrong places, urgency and shortened links.
Download from official sources; avoid cracked software; check file types.
Keep the browser updated, manage extensions, and never trust scareware pop-ups.
Read a QR code's address before opening; be cautious on public Wi-Fi.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. (A realistic, non-identifying account typical of link-based scams. Names and details are illustrative.)
A small school in Port Harcourt paid suppliers by internet transfer. One morning, the bursar received an email that looked like it
came from a trusted building-supplies company, with an invoice PDF and a link: "Download the updated invoice to confirm the
new account details." The address in the email read supplies-school-verify.example , and the page was a near-perfect copy of the
supplier's website.
Relevant facts.
The email displayed the supplier's real name and logo, but the domain was not the supplier's.
The link led to a page asking for the school's online-banking login "to confirm the transfer."
The school had never confirmed a supplier's bank details by email before.
Decision point. The bursar had to decide: download and log in to "confirm," or stop and verify.
Possible approaches.
1. Follow the email and log in to "confirm the transfer" — exactly what the attacker wants.
2. Ignore the email completely and lose the chance to pay on time.
3. Verify through a second channel: call the supplier on a number the school already had, and never log in through a link in
an email.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. The bursar calls the supplier's known number; the supplier says the email is fake. Had the
bursar already logged in, the correct steps would be: change the online-banking password immediately, tell the bank, and
watch the account for unusual activity.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: the fake page captures the login — the account could be emptied.
Approach 2: avoids the scam but may delay a legitimate payment.
Approach 3: a two-minute phone call resolves everything, confirms the truth, and protects the school's money.
Professional connection. This is a classic business email compromise/invoice-fraud attempt and a phishing page. The
professional defence is exactly what learners practised today: read the domain, distrust links from messages, and verify through
a second channel. Invoices and payment details should always be confirmed by a phone call to a known number.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 "It has a padlock, so it is safe"
Symptoms: Learner trusts any HTTPS site and enters details. Likely cause: Confusing encryption (private road) with honesty
(trustworthy shop). How to investigate: Ask them to point to the domain and compare it with the official one. Safe corrective
steps: Re-teach: check the domain first; the padlock only confirms encryption. Scams use HTTPS too. How to confirm
resolved: Learner checks the domain before the padlock and can explain the difference. How to prevent recurrence: Use
"Domain First" every session.
K.2 Reading the address from left to right
Symptoms: Learner trusts mybank-secure.example because "mybank" comes first. Likely cause: Natural reading direction; not
knowing subdomains. How to investigate: Ask the learner to underline the domain (before the final ending). Safe corrective
steps: Practise with several fictional addresses until the domain is found instantly. How to confirm resolved: Learner circles the
correct domain on 5 addresses. How to prevent recurrence: Repetition with fresh examples.
K.3 Clicking to "check" a suspicious link
Symptoms: Learner says "let me just tap it and see." Likely cause: Curiosity; underestimating the risk. How to investigate: This
is a stop-and-correct moment, not an investigation. Safe corrective steps: Firmly remind the class: never click unknown links;
we read them. Use fictional cards only. How to confirm resolved: Learner restates the rule. How to prevent recurrence: Re-
read the ethics reminder at the start of the session.
K.4 Believing a scareware pop-up
Symptoms: Learner panics at "Your PC is infected!" and wants to call the number. Likely cause: Fear; thinking a website can
scan the device. How to investigate: Ask: "Can a web page actually read all your files?" (No.) Safe corrective steps: Close the
tab; restart the browser; run a real Windows Defender scan if worried. How to confirm resolved: Learner calmly closes the
pop-up and does not call/click. How to prevent recurrence: Repeat "websites cannot scan your PC."
K.5 Downloading from an unofficial source
Symptoms: Learner downloads paid software free from a random site. Likely cause: Cost; not knowing the malware risk. How
to investigate: Check the source domain and file type. Safe corrective steps: Cancel; download from the official site or store;
scan with Defender; keep only official copies. How to confirm resolved: Learner identifies the official source and explains why.
How to prevent recurrence: Rule: official sources only; no cracked software.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: "Unsafe" is a likely verdict from reading the address; it becomes confirmed only when you
verify the domain against the official one (or after a controlled check). Teach learners to reason and verify, never to click to
confirm.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What does HTTPS protect, and what does it not prove about a website?
Q2. Multiple choice. In the address https://secure.login.mybank.example.com/verify , what is the real domain? a) secure b)
login.mybank c) example.com d) mybank
Q3. Explain in your own words. Why should you never judge a link by the words shown in the message (the link text)?
Q4. Practical identification. Is this safe or unsafe, and why?
A WhatsApp message: "Your parcel is held. Pay ₦2,000 here: bit.example/x9 ."
Q5. Short answer. Give two rules for downloading software safely.
Q6. Scenario. A full-screen pop-up says, "WARNING! Your PC has 5 viruses! Call this number now!" What is happening, and
what should you do?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. HTTPS encrypts the connection between your browser and the website, protecting data in transit from eavesdroppers. It
does not prove the site is honest or official — a scam site can also use HTTPS. Always check the domain as well.
A2. (c) example.com . (The real site name is just before the final ending; secure and login.mybank are only subdomains.)
A3. Because the visible text is only a label; the actual destination is hidden in the code behind the link. A link can display
"mybank.com" but point anywhere. You must read/verify the real domain or type the official address yourself.
A4. Unsafe. It is unexpected (you did not order anything), it uses a shortened link that hides the destination, and it creates
pressure with a payment demand. Safe action: do not click; track parcels only through official sites/apps; delete.
A5. (Any two) Use official sources only; avoid cracked/warez software; check the file type (beware .exe , macros); avoid
bundled extras; verify the domain; scan with Defender.
A6. It is scareware — a fake alert. Websites cannot scan your computer, and real security software does not lock your screen
and ask you to call a number. Actions: close the tab (or restart the browser); do not call or click; if worried, run a real scan with
Windows Defender.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned to read a web address like a professional: find the domain (the real site name), and
watch for misspellings, trusted names in the wrong place, shortened links and urgency. You learned that HTTPS/TLS encrypts
the connection and the padlock means "private road," not "honest shop." You learned to download only from official sources
and avoid cracked software, to keep the browser updated and clean, and to recognise scareware pop-ups as attacks. Finally,
you covered QR code risks and safe public Wi-Fi behaviour, and you practised classifying fictional links correctly.
Five important takeaways:
1. Read the domain — the real site name is just before the final ending.
2. The padlock means encrypted, not honest; check the domain first.
3. Never click links from messages; type the official address or use the app.
4. Download from official sources only; no cracked software; check file types.
5. Scary pop-ups are the attack — close the tab; a website cannot scan your PC.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I find the domain in any web address?
Can I explain what a padlock does and does not mean?
Can I decide if a link is unsafe without clicking it?
Can I tell a scareware pop-up from a real warning?
Connection to the next session: You can now judge a link. But the most common way links arrive is by email — and phishing
has become very convincing. Next session we open the anatomy of a phishing email, learn the psychology attackers use, and
practise the correct response to a suspect email: don't click, don't reply, verify, and report.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner finds the domain instantly and ignores left-to-right distractions.
Learner correctly separates "encrypted (padlock)" from "honest/ official."
Learner refuses to click an unknown link and instead verifies through an official route.
Learner recognises scareware and closes it calmly.
Learner chooses official download sources and can say why.
Learner checks a QR code's revealed address before opening.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Padlock means safe/honest." → It means encrypted only.
"If the trusted word is in the link, it is the real site." → Only the domain is the real name.
"A website warned me I have a virus, so it is real." → Websites cannot scan your PC.
"Free cracked software is harmless." → A common malware hiding place.
"Public Wi-Fi is safe if I use HTTPS." → Prefer mobile data for sensitive logins; watch for fake hotspots.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Domain hunt: 10 addresses; the learner underlines only the domain, quickly.
2. Safe/unsafe sort: cards with URLs; the learner sorts into two piles and defends each.
3. Pop-up drill: show three fictional alerts; the learner states the safe action for each.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): give a domain-highlighted set of cards and a checklisk with the four checks in order.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a 5-question quiz for a family member on link safety, including the answer key,
using only fictional examples.
Timing guidance: The domain-reading practical is the priority; if running late, shorten the QR/public-Wi-Fi discussion to a 2-
minute summary and protect the guided practical (Section H). If the room is fast, extend the case study and let learners present
their posters.
