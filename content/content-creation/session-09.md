# Capstone — Assemble, Present and Review

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Publish, Measure and Package (final session)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner, applying Sessions 1–8 (no new software today)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** All of Sessions 1–8: niche, avatar and pillars (S1); shot footage (S2); clear audio and a script (S3); a first CapCut edit (S4); B-roll, music and a Canva thumbnail (S5); hooks, captions, CTAs and hashtags (S6); a one-week calendar and brand kit (S7); published/draft links and an analytics note (S8). Connection to previous session The one-week calendar and analytics note from S7–S8 now feed the final package. In S8 you learned to read views, retention, saves and shares; today you turn that reading into a short spoken defence — "here is what I made, why, and what the numbers told me." Session 9 teaches no new material: it gathers every deliverable into one folder, checks it against the rubric, and presents it.

**Preparation before class:** (1) Print the Capstone Assessment Rubric (one per learner) and the peer-feedback form (Section I; two per learner). (2) Load the model Adaeze package on the projector (Instructor Guide). (3) Prepare the sign-off sheet: name, business, folder path, links, marks, signature. (4) Read the rubric and instructor guide in full. (5) For classes above about eight presenters, prepare a second room/assessor. (6) Remind learners to bring their whole package — videos, graphics, calendar, captions and links.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
This session delivers LO10 — Present and defend a complete content package: the learner can assemble a full package
(videos, graphics, calendar, captions, links) and present it, explaining niche, audience, hooks, shot choices, edits and metrics.
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a complete package in CEA-Content-<YourName> : three edited vertical captioned videos, three Canva graphics (two
posts + one thumbnail), a one-week calendar, a captions document, and published links or ready-to-publish drafts.
2. Organise and name files clearly so a client could open the folder and understand it without help.
3. Run a final quality check against the rubric and fix the biggest gaps under time-limited build conditions.
4. Deliver a 3–5 minute presentation explaining niche, audience, hooks, shot choices, edits and metrics in their own
words.
5. Answer viva ("why") questions on niche, hook, music/B-roll legality, B-roll placement, audio ducking, caption structure,
retention, consent, thumbnail wording, strongest video, honesty and one improvement.
6. Give and receive structured peer feedback using a simple, respectful form.
7. Explain the certification conditions honestly.

Hardware

to the projector.
Instructor computer connected to the projector; whiteboard and markers; a tripod (optional).
Software
CapCut (free) for final trims, caption fixes or exports; Canva (free) for finishing a graphic or thumbnail.

Sample files / examples

(Instructor Guide Section 4).
Worksheets / forms
The Capstone Assessment Rubric; the peer-feedback form; the self-check checklist (Section H); the sign-off sheet.
Accounts / Internet
The learner's own free publishing account to show a published or draft link; if content is private, an exported draft or

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two folders side by side. First, the tidy CEA-Content-<YourName> tree — 01_Videos ,
02_Graphics , 03_Planning , 04_Raw_Footage , README.txt . Second, a messy example: VID_20261009_12938.mp4 , IMG_5521.png , and a
caption saved as "New Text Document.txt".
What the instructor says:
"Three weeks ago most of you watched videos on your phone. Today you are not watching — you are presenting. Both
folders hold the same amount of work. One you would happily send to a client paying thirty thousand naira; the other
would make that client think you are not serious, even if your videos are good. Nothing new to learn today — everything
to finish, organise, check and explain. A beautiful package you cannot explain cannot pass. That rule is fair, because a real
client will ask 'why' too."
Show of confidence: Ask every learner to hold up their device and tick off six things out loud: three videos, three graphics, a
one-week calendar, a captions document, published/draft links, and a clean folder. Most have nearly everything and feel a small
panic about one missing item — normal, and useful.
Reframe the panic (say this):
"If you are missing one or two things, you are not in trouble — you have 45 minutes to finish them. What would put you in
trouble is arriving with two videos instead of three, because the package must be complete. Write one line on your rubric:
the ONE missing thing you will finish first."
Why this works: It puts the two pass-deciders on the table — completeness and the ability to explain — and converts
nerves into one task per learner.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run a fast oral round-robin, one question per learner, so everyone speaks before presenting. Put the questions on the projector.
Q1 (S1–S2): "In one sentence, what is your niche and who is your audience avatar?"
Expected: a specific topic/group, e.g. "affordable Ankara styles for young working women in Port Harcourt," and one
named person like Blessing, a 27-year-old bank worker. Watch for a broad topic ("fashion") and ask "for whom and where."
Q2 (S2–S3): "Name one thing that makes phone footage look professional, and one thing that ruins audio."
Expected: stability and good light (window light, no backlighting); a mic far from the mouth or a noisy room ruins audio.
Watch for learners crediting the camera — it is stability, light and mic distance.
Q3 (S4–S5): "What is B-roll for, and what does 'legally usable music' mean?"
Expected: B-roll covers cuts and holds the eye; legal music comes from the CapCut library, the YouTube Audio Library, or
licensed free music — never ripped from random sites.
Q4 (S6): "What are the four parts of a caption, and how many hashtags should a beginner use?"
Expected: hook, value, CTA, hashtags; 3–5 relevant tags (up to 10 acceptable) — relevance over volume.
Q5 (S7–S8): "What does a retention graph tell you, and what would you do if it drops in the first two seconds?"
Expected: how long people watched; an early drop means the hook was weak, so open stronger next time.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Not one of those questions was about a camera. Every one was about a decision — who, why, how, what next. When you
present, I am not checking whether your video looks like a Nollywood film; I am checking whether you can tell me why it
opens the way it does. Explain your decisions and you have learned the craft."
If more than a third cannot answer, spend five extra minutes on the four caption parts and hook–body–CTA, which recur in
every viva.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What the capstone is, and what "complete" means
The idea. The capstone is a complete content package for one small business — Adaeze Fashion, or a business you chose
with approval. It is not a written exam and not one project; it is the whole body of course work, finished, organised and
explained. It proves, in one folder, that you can do the whole job a small client would hire you for.
Plain-English definition. A complete package means three edited vertical videos, three Canva graphics, a one-week
calendar, a captions document, and published links or ready-to-publish drafts — plus a short live presentation and viva.
The six deliverables:
# Deliverable What "good" looks like
1 Three edited videos Vertical (9:16), 30–60s, captioned, clear voice, tight cuts, B-roll and/or music, a clear hook, a
defined purpose each
2 Three Canva graphics Two posts plus one thumbnail; readable text; one focal point; consistent brand colours and
fonts
3 One-week calendar Seven days; columns filled (date, platform, format, pillar, hook, caption idea, status); varied
pillars; a lighter day
4 Captions document A hook, caption, CTA and 3–5 hashtags for each video; roughly 40–80 words each
5 Published links or
drafts
A real public link, or a clearly named exported draft; a WhatsApp Status screenshot counts
6 Presentation + viva A 3–5 minute spoken walk-through and answers to "why" questions
Why "complete" is a hard gate. If any of the three videos or three graphics is entirely absent, the package is incomplete and
the learner cannot pass on this attempt — a client who asked for three videos and three graphics does not want two videos and
a promise. On the day, complete beats pretty.
The honesty rule. Every piece of music and footage must be legal, and every person who appears must have given permission.
A package that uses a stolen song or films a customer without consent loses Criterion 6 and can fail the legal/privacy pass
condition. Never trade trust for a quick win.
F.2 Assembling and organising the portfolio folder
The idea. Everything goes into one folder named CEA-Content-<YourName> , with subfolders for videos, graphics, planning and
raw footage, plus a short README. This is 3 marks (Criterion 1) and the first thing an assessor sees.
Expected folder structure (show it on the projector):
```text
CEA-Content-<YourName>/
├── 01_Videos/
│   ├── video1_shop_intro.mp4
│   ├── video2_care_tip.mp4
│   └── video3_customer_proof.mp4
```
```text
├── 02_Graphics/
│   ├── post1_new_stock.png
│   ├── post2_promo.png
│   └── thumbnail_1280x720.png
├── 03_Planning/
│   ├── content_calendar_week1.xlsx        (or .docx / a clear photo of a paper sheet)
│   ├── captions_document.docx
│   ├── script_video1.docx
│   └── storyboard_video1.(jpg/png/pdf)
├── 04_Raw_Footage/                        (optional but recommended backup)
└── README.txt
```
Naming. Use plain, descriptive names — video2_care_tip.mp4 , not VID_20261009_12938.mp4 — numbered 1, 2, 3 in presentation
order.
The README (one short paragraph). Name the business, the platforms, and one line about each video, e.g.: "Adaeze Fashion
— Port Harcourt Ankara shop. Video 1: shop introduction (Instagram Reel). Video 2: Ankara care tip (TikTok). Video 3: customer
proof (Instagram Reel)."
Keep a backup of raw footage. Phones get lost, cards corrupt. 04_Raw_Footage is your insurance — if a last-minute export fails,
you cannot fix it without the source. A real client judges your professionalism by whether the folder looks organised.
F.3 The final quality check against the rubric
The idea. Before you present, run your package through the exact rubric the assessor will use. You are not guessing what "good"
means — the rubric tells you.
The nine rubric criteria (30 marks) at a glance:
# Criterion Marks Self-check question
1 Package completeness & folder
organisation
3 Are all six deliverables present and clearly named?
2 Video hook & shooting craft 5 Do all three videos hook in the first 3 seconds; is footage stable,
lit, framed?
3 Video audio quality 3 Is the voice clear, close to the mic, above the background?
4 Video editing — cuts & captions 5 Are silences cut; are captions correct at 3–5 words per line?
5 Video enhancement — B-roll, music,
transitions
3 Does B-roll cover cuts; does music sit under the voice?
6 Legal & safe media use 2 Is all media allowed; is consent obtained where people appear?
7 Graphics — 2 posts + 1 thumbnail 5 On-brand, readable, thumbnail matches the video?
8 One-week content calendar 2 Are all 7 days filled with varied pillars?
9 Captions document 2 Does each video have a hook, value, CTA and 3–5 hashtags?
How to run the self-check (10 minutes) — fix in this order:
1. Completeness. Confirm all six deliverables exist and open. A missing video is fatal; a shaky one is not.
2. Captions. Auto-captions often carry wrong words. Fix them and keep 3–5 words per line.
3. Audio balance. Play a video on the speaker; if you cannot hear the voice over the music, lower (duck) the music.
4. Thumbnail match. Make it honestly represent the video; clickbait loses Criterion 7 and 6.
5. Calendar and captions document. Fill empty cells.
6. Polish last. Only then worry about a fancy transition or colour.
Worked example. Adaeze's self-check: "Video 2 captions had two wrong words — fixing. Music in Video 1 too loud at 0:30 —
lowering. Biggest gap: Video 2 audio slightly noisy — will re-export with the fan off." She named her biggest gap and a fix —
the honesty the viva rewards.
F.4 Preparing and structuring the 3–5 minute presentation
The idea. Your presentation is a spoken walk-through of the package, about 3–5 minutes. You open files, show the work, and
explain the decisions behind it. It is not a script reading and not a feature list.
Why it matters. The presentation carries a separate 5-mark viva component and convinces the assessor the skill is in your head,
not just in the software. A strong presenter with a modest package can score highly; a weak presenter with a beautiful package
loses the "explains why" marks.
A reliable 5-part structure:
Time What you do Example line
1. Introduction
~30s Name the business, niche
and avatar
"I created a one-week package for Adaeze Fashion, an Ankara
shop in Port Harcourt. My niche is affordable Ankara for young
working women; my avatar is Blessing, a 27-year-old bank worker
who hates being overcharged."
2. Package tour
~1
min
Open the folder; show the
videos, graphics, calendar
and captions
"Here is my folder. Video 1 introduces the shop, Video 2 teaches
an Ankara care tip, Video 3 is customer proof."
3. Key decisions
~2
min
Explain why: hook, shot
choices, edits, music
"I opened Video 2 with the problem — 'Your Ankara is fading
because of this one mistake' — because my avatar's biggest worry
is wasting money on clothes that fade."
4. Metrics
~45s Read the analytics note in
plain words
"Video 1 got 214 views; retention dropped at second 2, so my text-

person."
5. Close
~30s State your strongest video
and one improvement
"My strongest is Video 3 because the customer story earns trust;
my first improvement is a stronger opening frame on Video 1."
How to speak. Stand, look at the audience, and open each file before you talk about it. Play only 5–8 seconds of each video
and describe the rest. Do not read a script — it sounds flat and fails the "explains key decisions" line: three bullet cards are fine,
paragraphs are not.
F.5 Running the viva — answering "why" questions
The idea. The viva is a short spoken Q&A after (or during) your presentation. The assessor asks three to five "why" questions
from the viva bank, and you answer in your own words. A package can be copied, but understanding cannot be faked.
The viva bank — what you may be asked (learn the reasons):
Question What a good answer shows
"What is your niche, and why?" A narrow topic/group; focus helps people remember
the page.
"Why does your video start the way it does?" The first 3 seconds make a viewer stop; a problem,
question or claim that matters to the audience.
"Where did your music and B-roll come from, and may you use them?" CapCut library, YouTube Audio Library, or licensed
free music; B-roll shot by you. Never ripped.
"Why did you put B-roll where you put it?" To cover cuts/jump cuts and hold the eye, roughly
every 5–7 seconds.
"How did you make the voice clear under the music?" By ducking — lowering the music under the voice so
the voice is always loudest.
"Why is your caption in that structure?" Hook line, short value, CTA, 3–5 hashtags; ~40–80
words with line breaks.
"What does the retention graph tell you, and what would you change?" How long people watched; an early drop = weak
hook, a mid-video drop = slow pacing.
Question What a good answer shows
"How did you get permission to film people?" Asked each person directly (a guardian for under-
18s), explained where it would appear, respected a
no.
"Why is a thumbnail with three strong words better than a busy one?" Readable at a glance; one focal point; strong
contrast; honest.
"Which of your three videos is strongest, and why?" A comparison referencing hook, value, editing or CTA
— not just "the most likes."
"Why is buying followers or a fake review a bad idea?" Dishonest, damages trust, breaches platform rules,
builds no real customers.
"If you had one more week, what would you improve first, and why?" A specific, prioritised answer; the reasoning matters
more than the choice.
How to answer well. Use your own words — if you feel yourself reciting, stop and say it plainly. Give a reason, not a fact: "I put
B-roll at 0:08 to cover the jump-cut where I changed my sentence" beats "I put B-roll at 0:08." Be honest about weak points.
Point at specifics.
The honesty question. If asked about fake engagement, do not be clever: buying followers or posting fake reviews is dishonest,
damages trust, can breach platform rules, and builds no real customers. CEA expects the straight answer.
F.6 Peer-feedback etiquette
The idea. While your classmate presents, you fill a peer-feedback form. Peer feedback makes the presenter better, teaches you
to see craft, and is done with respect.
The three rules of good peer feedback:
1. Be specific. "It was nice" helps nobody. "The hook on Video 2 made me stop because I also hate clothes that fade" tells the
presenter exactly what worked.
2. Be kind and honest together. One genuine strength, then one improvement. Never mock or compare to "better" learners.
3. Comment on the work, not the person. "The captions were a little small" — not "you are bad at captions."
What the form contains (see Section I). A strength, an improvement, one idea for the next video, and a thank-you line. The
presenter keeps the form as evidence of peer review. When receiving feedback: listen without interrupting, do not defend, write
it down, and thank the person. You need not act on every comment, but you must hear it.
Every line of feedback should be specific, tied to the rubric, and worded as help, not attack. Section G.4 shows a full worked
example.
F.7 The Session 9 run-of-show (105 minutes)
# Block Time What happens
1 Brief and restate the rules 5 min Opening and ground rules (Section D); the two-hint limit; the deliverable list.
2 Model presentation 8 min Instructor walks the reference Adaeze package (Section G).
3 Build sprint 45
min
Learners finish, organise and self-check (Section H). Instructor circulates; two-
hint limit.
4 Presentations and viva 40
min
~4–5 min per learner; two parallel rooms if the class is large.
5 Wrap-up, reflection, sign-

7 min Recap, certificate conditions, sign-off sheet, close and celebrate.
The two-hint limit. Each learner may ask a maximum of two hints, and a hint is a question, not an answer. If a learner says "my
video is too long, what do I do?", reply with a question: "Which parts repeat themselves — a greeting, a pause, or a second
example?" The learner still does the work, because a capstone the instructor edits is not the learner's work.
If time runs short: cut the model presentation to a quick mention, set the reflection as take-home, and protect the
presentations — a learner who does not present has an incomplete capstone. For more than eight presenters, run two parallel
rooms with a second assessor using the same rubric.
F.8 Certification conditions
The idea. At the end of Session 9, the instructor confirms who is ready for certification. The certificate is earned by meeting
clear conditions, not by attendance alone.
Conditions to be recommended for the CEA Certificate in Content Creation:
1. Attendance: at least 7 of the 9 sessions (a gate, but never a substitute for competence).
2. Assignments: all submitted and judged satisfactory.
3. Capstone package: a pass — at least 50% overall AND a complete package.
4. Legal and privacy: music and footage used legally; consent obtained where people appear.
5. Presentation: the learner has presented the package and answered the viva.
The pass rule in one sentence: To pass you need 50% or more overall, a complete package, and to have presented and
explained it. A pretty package you cannot explain cannot pass.
Verification. The certificate verifies at cea.ng/certificates/verify — quote your reference when sharing it.
If a learner is not yet competent. Below 50%, an incomplete package, or an inability to explain the work means "not yet."
Remediation is targeted: find the single biggest gap, do one focused practice, then retake. A retake is normal and is not a failure
— the learner keeps all work already done.
Record-keeping before anyone leaves. Record on the sign-off sheet: name, business used, folder path, published/draft links,
marks, signature.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
The demonstration happens before the build sprint, so learners have a model in mind while they work.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
On the projector: the model Adaeze package — the folder tree, the three video descriptions (5.1), the three graphics (5.2),
the sample caption (5.6), the sample calendar (5.5) and the sample analytics note (5.7).
A partly completed peer-feedback form and the rubric on screen, so learners see which line each demo aims at.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the model presentation (5 minutes)
Say: "Watch how I present, then you will do the same. Notice I do not read — I explain." Walk the reference package aloud
using the five-part structure from F.4, in about four minutes:
"This is my package for Adaeze Fashion — an Ankara shop in Port Harcourt. My niche is affordable Ankara for young
working women; my avatar is Blessing, a 27-year-old bank worker who hates being overcharged. (Open the folder.) Here is
my folder: 01_Videos , 02_Graphics , 03_Planning , and a README.
Video 1 introduces the shop, opening on a close-up of a bright Ankara dress with 'The Ankara shop Port Harcourt keeps
talking about' — a clear, confident claim in the first three seconds stops my fast-scrolling avatar. The owner then speaks
in window light, with B-roll of hands arranging dresses covering the cuts. Video 2 teaches a care tip, opening with the
problem — 'Your Ankara is fading because of this one mistake.' Video 3 is customer proof: a customer smiling, with 'She
almost did not buy this dress' — honest, because she really did hesitate. My graphics share the brand colours and fonts,
and the thumbnail is three words, 'NEW ANKARA STYLES,' matching Video 1.
On metrics: Video 1 got 214 views, but retention dropped at second 2, so my text-only hook was weak — next time I will
open with the dress on a person, not a hanger. My strongest video is Video 3 because the customer story earns trust; if I
had one more week, I would reshoot Video 1's opening frame."
Then point at the rubric: "Notice I touched every marked thing — hook, shooting craft, audio, captions, B-roll, legal media,
graphics, calendar and captions document — and I explained why. That is the standard."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — answering three viva questions aloud (7 minutes)
Say: "Now watch me answer viva questions with a reason, not a fact." Answer each in about 40–60 seconds, thinking aloud:
1. "Where did your music and B-roll come from, and are you allowed to use them?"
"The music is from the CapCut library, which is free to use inside the app. The B-roll — hands arranging dresses,

2. "How did you make sure the voice is clear under the music?"
"I used ducking. In CapCut I selected the music track and set the volume low, around 15–20 percent, so it sits under
my voice but is still audible. I tested it on the phone speaker — if I could not hear my own words clearly, I lowered the
music more."
3. "What does the retention graph tell you, and what would you change next time?"

them — almost always a weak hook. Next time I would open with the dress on a person instead of a text card on a
hanger, because a face and movement stop the scroll better than words alone."
Then ask the class: "Did you notice I never just said 'I lowered the music'? I said why and how I checked — the difference
between a fact and an answer. In my metrics answer I admitted the hook failed and named the fix. That honesty is what the viva
rewards."

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Blessing (a CEA Port Harcourt learner) used a church media team as her capstone business instead of Adaeze Fashion.
The church wanted to grow its youth outreach, but its page posted only event flyers captioned "Come and worship with us,"
and attendance was flat. Blessing built a complete package: three videos (a Sunday testimony from a young member, a 40-
second "what to expect on Sunday" walk-through, and behind-the-scenes of the media team), three graphics (two invitation
posts and a thumbnail), a one-week calendar, a captions document, and a published draft link for the testimony.
Relevant facts. The testimony opened with the young member's face and the words "I almost stopped coming to church" — a
strong, honest hook. The walk-through used B-roll of the building, instruments and a smiling greeter while the voice explained
what a first-time visitor should expect. Blessing asked the member and her parent (she was 17) before filming, told her where it
would appear, and respected one person who declined. The calendar rotated pillars — teach, show, trust — instead of seven
identical invitations. Her analytics note: the testimony draft got 180 views in three days, lost about half the viewers around
second 2, earned 11 shares and 3 comments, all replied to.
Decision point. When the media lead asked "why is this different from our old flyers?", what should Blessing say?
Possible approaches. 1) "It just looks better." 2) "I used a hook, a clear structure, legal music, captions and a varied calendar —
every post has a reason and a next step, and I read the numbers so we know the hook needs work." 3) "I copied a bigger
church's videos."
Recommended solution. Approach 2. The difference is not decoration but method: a hook that earns the first three seconds,
captions for people watching with sound off, legal music, a balanced calendar, and an honest reading of the numbers.
What actually happened. The church used the package for one month of outreach. Three young people told the team they
came because of the testimony. Blessing named the limit honestly in her viva: "It was 180 views on a draft, not a viral video; the
hook still loses people at second 2, so the next one must open with a face and movement." The church then asked her to handle
its weekly posts for a small monthly fee.
Trade-offs / honest limits. The package did not make the church famous; it moved the needle slightly and gave the team a
repeatable method. The weak hook was discovered by reading retention, not guesswork. Getting consent cost time and one
person said no — respecting that no is part of the job.
Professional connection. This is exactly the work a small content creator is paid to do — for a church, a shop or a salon: a
strong hook, clear captions, legal media, a varied calendar and an honest reading of the numbers. Blessing's first real paid client
came directly from her capstone package.
