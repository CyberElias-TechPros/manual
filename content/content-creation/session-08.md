# Publishing and Basic Analytics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Publish, Measure and Package

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (phone use; no software skill assumed)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–7: a niche and audience avatar (S1); a finished edited video (S4–S5); a thumbnail (S5); captions with hook and CTA (S6); a one-week calendar with brand choices (S7). A learner without a finished video may follow with a classmate's or the instructor's sample. Connection to previous session In Session 7 you planned what to post, when, and made your posts look like one consistent brand. Today you put the plan into action: you publish (or schedule, or keep a private draft) and then read the numbers that come back. The S7 calendar is the map; today you drive the road and read the dashboard.

**Preparation before class:** Have ready: (1) the Adaeze Fashion video and thumbnail (S5–S6) to upload; (2) a free TikTok/Instagram/YouTube account logged in on the projector device with working internet; (3) two sample analytics screenshots (one "many views, no action", one "fewer views, strong action"); (4) three sample comments (praising, complaining, price); (5) the analytics-note template and Simulation brief (I.4) printed per learner; (6) tested Wi-Fi — publishing needs a live connection.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what "publishing" means across TikTok, Instagram, YouTube Shorts and WhatsApp Status, and how it differs from
editing or exporting.
2. Publish, schedule, or prepare a ready-to-publish draft / unlisted link when they prefer to keep content private.
3. Choose a sensible posting time using general guidance and their own audience habits, knowing no time guarantees
success.
4. Write a good first (pinned) comment that adds value, repeats a CTA, or starts a conversation.
5. Explain the basic analytics terms in their own words: views, reach, watch time, average view duration, retention, saves,
shares, comments, follows.
6. Read a retention graph and say what an early drop (weak hook) and a mid-video drop (slow pacing) mean, then name one
specific change.
7. Handle comments politely and professionally — praise, questions and complaints.
8. Apply the ethics of engagement: no fake likes, no bought followers, no fake reviews, respect privacy, keep replies
professional.
9. Complete a short analytics note that names real numbers and what they suggest.
10. Publish or prepare one item and record its first-day numbers (Assignment 8 and part of the capstone evidence).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Show two analytics screenshots side by side. Cover the numbers at first. Do not say which is "better" yet.
Post A: 1,200 views, 0 saves, 0 shares, 1 comment ("Nice").
Post B: 210 views, 14 saves, 6 shares, 9 comments (people asking "how much?" and "where is your shop?").
What the instructor says:
"Both are posts from small Port Harcourt businesses. One looks like a winner because its number is bigger. Show of hands:
which post actually brought the business more customers?"
Take a quick vote. Most point at Post A (1,200 is bigger than 210). Then correct gently:
"The big number is views — how many times the video was seen. But Post B: people saved it, shared it, and commented
asking the price and address — those are buying signals. Post A was seen a lot and moved nobody; Post B moved many.
Today you will learn to read all the numbers, not just the loud one."
Expected learner response: Many argue views matter. Accept it — it is the misconception the session corrects.
Then reveal the theme on the board:
"It is not how many people saw it. It is what they did next."

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a fast oral round-robin with the questions on the projector. The goal: every learner is ready to publish their own work.
Q1 — "What is on today's row of your S7 calendar — platform, format and hook?" Expected: a concrete post (e.g.,
"Monday, Instagram Reel, the '3 reasons' hook"). Common gap: no finished calendar. Handling: pair with a classmate; they
publish their draft or the class sample.
Q2 — "Where is your finished video and thumbnail, with captions, a hook and a CTA?" Expected: on the phone / in the
gallery. Common gap: not exported. Clarify: a draft inside CapCut is not publishable — it must be exported so the gallery and
social apps can see it.
Q3 — "What is the exact CTA in your caption?" Expected: something specific like "DM 'FASHION'", "Comment your style",
"Call to order". Common gap: vague or no CTA. Clarify: a CTA turns a view into a measurable action today.
Q4 — "Name one thing that makes your page look like one brand." Expected: same colours, fonts, logo, tone, brand
hashtag. Common gap: inconsistent style. Clarify: consistency builds trust — and trust is what makes people save, share and
buy.
Bridge to today (say this):
"For seven sessions you planned, shot, recorded, edited, designed and wrote. Today the work finally leaves your phone and
meets the real world — the morning you open the door. Once the door is open, watch the shop floor: how many came
in, how long they stayed, what they asked. That is analytics."
If more than a third of the class has no finished video, spend 5 extra minutes publishing the Adaeze sample as a whole-class
activity — these skills can be taught from any content.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What "publishing" actually means
The idea. Publishing is the moment your content becomes visible to other people on a platform — when the video you made
in CapCut moves from your phone to where a stranger can find it. Editing and exporting are private; publishing is shared.
Plain-English definition. To publish is to put your content out where an audience can see it. The button may be Post, Share,
Upload or Publish — the word changes by app and version.
What publishing is NOT. It is not editing (cutting/captioning in CapCut), exporting (saving the file to the gallery) or designing
(the Canva thumbnail). Those produce a file; publishing shares the file. Many beginners stop at the file and wonder why
nothing happens.
Limitation. Publishing does not guarantee views or make content good; it only makes your work available. Whether people
watch, save and buy depends on the hook, value and CTA you built earlier — and how you read the numbers and improve.

F.3 Best posting times — general guidance, not guarantees
The idea. "Best time to post" means the time your audience is most likely to be online. There is general guidance, but no magic
minute guarantees success.
Time window (WAT) Why it often works
Early morning (6–8am) People wake and scroll before work or school.
Lunch break (12–1pm) People relax and check phones at midday.
Evening (6–9pm) The strongest window for most Nigerian audiences; people are home and scrolling.
Weekends More free time; often good for entertainment and shopping content.
Why it is only guidance. Your audience is not "everybody" — it is a specific group (for Adaeze: young working women in Port
Harcourt). A worker who sleeps early may watch at 1pm; a church audience may be busiest on Sunday. Most apps show when
your followers are active — use it as a hint.
The more important lesson. For a beginner, consistency beats perfect timing. Posting three times a week at a "good-
enough" time beats posting once a month at the "perfect" time. Do not let the hunt for the perfect hour stop you from posting.
Common misconception. "8pm will blow up; 3pm will fail." Timing is a small dial, not the main switch. Hook, value, CTA and
consistency matter far more.
F.4 The first comment and the pinned comment
The idea. The first comment is a comment you leave on your own post right after publishing; a pinned comment stays at the
top. The caption may be long and the CTA buried; a first/pinned comment puts a short, useful message right where people
look.
What goes in a good first / pinned comment:
1. Extra value — a tip or small bonus ("Tip: wash ankara inside-out to keep the colour").
2. A repeat of the CTA — "DM the word 'FASHION' for the price list."
3. A question to start conversation — "Which colour would you wear to church: burgundy or navy?"
4. Hashtags — on Instagram many creators put them in the first comment to keep the caption clean.
5. Important details — a WhatsApp number, shop address or link not in the caption.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion. After publishing the "3 reasons" reel, she pins:
"Colour shown is burgundy (also in navy and forest green). ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' for this week's price list. Which colour is
your favourite — comment below. ðŸ’¬"
Common misconception. "The first comment is only for hashtags." It can hold hashtags, but it is most powerful when it adds
value or starts a conversation. Never use it to make false claims.
F.5 What analytics are and why they matter
The idea. Analytics are the numbers a platform shows about how your content performed — how many saw it, how long they
watched, what they saved, shared and said. They are your dashboard.
Plain-English definition. Analytics = "what happened after I posted" — the platform's honest report of how people reacted.
Why they exist. Without numbers you are guessing. You cannot tell whether the hook worked, whether people stayed, or
whether the CTA moved anyone. Analytics replace guessing with evidence, so you can decide what to change.
Limitation. Analytics are not a score of your worth nor a direct measure of money. A 5,000-view video might earn nothing; a
150-view video from the right local audience might earn ten orders. Read the numbers as clues, never verdicts — read a
handful, say honestly what they suggest, and name one change.

F.7 Reading the retention graph and what to change
The idea. The retention graph (audience retention) is a line from the start of your video to the end, showing the percentage
of viewers still watching at each moment. Where the line falls, people left; where it stays flat, people stayed; a spike often
means people rewatched that moment.
How to read it: the start is always high (near 100%) because most who click watch the very start; read left to right (down =
leaving, flat = staying); the end tells you how many reached the finish.

immediately.

Worked example — Adaeze's reel. The graph stays high for one second, then plunges at second 2 when the video shows a
still hanger with no movement, then flattens when she talks about the dress. Diagnosis: the hook lost people. Change: open
with a person wearing the dress and moving, text hook on screen at once. This is the situation in capstone viva question 7
and the sample analytics note.
Common misconception. "A drop at the end means it was bad." No — some end-drop is normal. Worry about early drops
(weak hook) and middle drops (weak pacing).
F.8 Community management — handling comments politely
The idea. Community management is how you behave in comments and messages after publishing: replying, thanking,
answering, defusing. It is the human side of publishing, where small businesses win trust — a comment is a person talking in
public, so the comments are the front desk.
Rules for polite, professional replies:
1. Reply quickly, ideally within a few hours.
2. Thank praise simply and warmly — never argue with a compliment.
3. Answer questions fully — price, size, location, or exactly where to find it.
4. Stay calm and non-personal with complaints. Never trade insults. Move serious details to a private DM.
5. Never share private customer information — no full names, phone numbers, addresses or order details of another
customer in public.
6. Turn negatives into service. Apologise honestly if wrong and offer a real next step.
7. Delete/report only spam and abuse — never delete honest criticism just because it stings.
8. Heart/thank good comments to show appreciation.

F.9 The ethics of engagement — grow honestly
The idea. There is a right and a wrong way to grow. The wrong way — buying followers, fake likes, fake reviews — is
dishonest, breaks platform rules and builds nothing real. The right way — good content, honest claims, real engagement —
builds a business that lasts. Your reputation in Port Harcourt is your real asset; platforms also remove fake followers and can
restrict or ban accounts that cheat.
Do this ✅ Never do this â Œ
Reply honestly to comments and DMs. Buy followers, likes, views or comments.
Use real customer comments (with permission) as testimonials. Write fake reviews or fake testimonials.
Show products as they really are. Use a filter or claim that misleads buyers.
Ask happy customers to leave an honest review. Pay for a positive-only fake review.
Ask friends to share content they genuinely like. Run engagement pods that mass-like dishonestly.
Respect privacy — never post a customer's details without permission. Screenshot private chats or customer data publicly.
Take responsibility for mistakes. Delete honest criticism or insult a complaining
customer.
Worked example. A competitor offers to "add 5,000 real Nigerian followers" for ₦5,000. Adaeze refuses — those followers
would never buy, would make her posts look empty of real engagement, and could get her flagged. Instead she spends the
₦5,000 promoting her best reel honestly.
Common misconception. "Everyone buys followers; it is normal." Common is not correct. It cheats the numbers, not
customers. Honest growth is slower, but a slow, real audience is worth far more than a fast, fake one.

them is exactly what the viva rewards. "214 views, drop at second 2, changing my hook" is a strong answer.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ebere runs a small restaurant, "Ebere's Pot," on a busy road in Port Harcourt. Her food is popular, but her social page
barely moves. She posts about once a week, whenever she remembers, never looks at the numbers, and assumes social media
"just does not work for food businesses."
Relevant facts.
Her food is good — the shop is busy at lunch — but her videos always open with five seconds of a still pot, and she
posts at random times (4am, midnight).
She has never opened her analytics, never replied to a comment, and once deleted a video after one day because it "only
had 40 views."
A competitor, De Sauce Place, posts consistently in the evening, replies to every comment, and shows the first bite right at
the start.
Decision point. Should Ebere (a) stop posting, (b) keep posting exactly as she does and hope, or (c) look at her analytics, fix
her opening, post at better times, and reply to comments?
Possible approaches.
1. Stop posting. Free, protects her from disappointment — but keeps her invisible to new customers.
2. Keep the same habits. No change, so the same result.
3. Read the numbers and adjust. Ebere opens her analytics: most videos lose half their viewers in the first three seconds,
during the still-pot opening. She changes to a spoon lifting steaming jollof with the first bite from second one, posts in
the evening, replies to every comment and pins one with address and price, and checks numbers after three days, not
one day.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ebere's problem was never the food or the camera — it was the hook, the timing, the
silence in the comments, and never reading her own numbers. All four are fixable for free in one weekend.
Illustrative result. Before: still-pot opening; retention dropped at second 3; average view duration 3 seconds of 30; about 40
saves a month; almost no comments. After a month of evening posting, first-bite hooks and replies: retention stayed high
through the first 8 seconds; average view duration rose to 9 seconds; saves reached about 110; and "Where is your shop?"
came weekly, with the address pinned. Ebere did not "go viral" — she became visible and trusted to the people near her, and
lunchtime got busier.
Professional connection. Social media managers are paid to do exactly what Ebere learned: post consistently, read analytics,
adjust the hook, and manage the community. A creator who can publish and then explain what the numbers mean and
what to change is far more valuable than one who can only edit. This session's skill — reading the dashboard — separates a
hobby poster from a paid manager.

## N. Reflection and Recap
