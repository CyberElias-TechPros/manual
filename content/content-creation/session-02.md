# Shooting with Your Phone

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: See and Plan (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (no prior camera experience assumed)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 concepts: niche, audience avatar, pillars and hooks. A learner should have chosen a subject/business and written at least one hook. If a learner missed Session 1, give them the two-minute catch-up: "Choose a business and one hook idea before we shoot." Connection to previous session In Session 1 learners decided who the content is for and what it says. Today they learn to capture it: how to hold the phone, use light, frame a shot, choose shot types and settings, and plan a shot list. The raw footage shot today becomes the material edited in Week 2.

**Preparation before class:** Charge phones where possible; ensure at least one tripod or phone stand per 2–3 learners; have the ring light ready; find a room or area with good window light; have Worksheet 2.1 ("Shot List") and Worksheet 2.2 ("Rule of Thirds Practice") printed; prepare one sample clip shot badly and one shot well for demonstration. Ensure Wi-Fi is on for any app installation needs. Clear a space where learners can move and shoot safely.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why a smartphone camera can produce professional-looking video and list the factors that matter more than the
camera itself (stability, light, framing, audio).
2. Hold or mount a phone so that footage is stable and shake-free, using a tripod, stand or improvised bracing.
3. Use light correctly — natural window light, a ring light, and simple fill — and avoid backlighting.
4. Apply the rule of thirds when framing a subject.
5. Identify and use at least four shot types: wide, medium, close-up and over-the-shoulder.
6. Choose vertical or horizontal framing appropriate to the target platform and explain the reason.
7. Set basic phone camera options — resolution, focus lock, grid and exposure — for a clean shot.
8. Build a shot list that plans at least five shots for a short piece of content.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone with a working camera per learner (shared is fine).
Tripod or phone stand: academy-provided. Ideally one per 2–3 learners. Improvised stands (stacked books, a wall ledge, a
chair back) are acceptable and should be demonstrated.
Ring light (academy-provided, at least one), plus access to natural window light.
Optional: earphone mic (introduced fully in Session 3) for demonstration.
Software
The phone's built-in camera app. No other software is required today.
Optional: any free camera app that allows grid and manual focus; the built-in app is usually enough.
Accounts
None required today.
Sample files / examples
Instructor prepares two short sample clips (both under 15 seconds) recorded before class: one deliberately shaky, dark and
backlit; one stable, well-lit and framed with the rule of thirds. Store on the instructor's phone or the academy computer.
A printed or on-screen example shot list.
Worksheets
Worksheet 2.1 — "Shot List" (printed, 1 page with ~6 rows: shot number, shot type, description, notes).
Worksheet 2.2 — "Rule of Thirds Practice" (one page with a light grid overlay for sketching frames).
Tools
Whiteboard and markers.
A wall or window area clear for the lighting demonstration.
Internet
Not required for shooting. Wi-Fi helpful only if a learner needs to install or update a camera app.
Alternatives for learners who lack a facility
Learners without a phone pair with a classmate and act as director ("move closer", "hold steady") — directing is the same
skill. They can also use academy devices if available. All planning work (shot list, framing sketches) is done on paper and is
fully valid.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Play (or describe) the two sample clips back to back: the bad clip first, then the good clip. Both feature the
same person — ideally the instructor or a willing learner — saying the same short line (e.g., "This is Adaeze Fashion —
affordable outfits for working women").
What the instructor says:
"Both of these clips were shot on a phone. Same phone, in fact. Same person. Same words. So why does the first one look
like a homemade video and the second looks like something a real brand posted?
(Wait for answers.)
It is not the phone. The phone cost the same. The difference is how it was shot: the second one was steady, it had good
light, the person was framed properly, and we could hear clearly. That is the whole secret of shooting with a phone. Today
you learn those four things."
Concrete hook (do this): Ask the class to clap once, then clap again but with their hands held far apart and loose. Ask: "Which
clap was clearer?" (The firm, close one.) Say:
"That is what it feels like when footage is stable and clear versus shaky and messy. Viewers decide in the first second —
just like the hook we learned last time."
Expected learner response: Learners usually say the second clip was "brighter", "not shaking", "the person was clear". Confirm:
those are light, stability and framing — the three pillars of shooting — and today we master them.
Why this opener works: It destroys the myth that gear is the problem and replaces it with specific, learnable skills on the phone
the learner already owns.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run a quick oral round-robin. Put these questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a niche? Give your own example."
Expected answer: the specific subject your content focuses on, e.g., "affordable office lunches in Port Harcourt".
Common gap: learners give a broad topic ("food"). Remind them: audience + place + problem.
Question 2: "What is a hook, and how long do you have to grab attention?"
Expected answer: the opening of your content; roughly the first 3 seconds (sometimes 1).
Common gap: thinking the hook is only words. Remind them the first visual counts too — which is exactly why today's
shooting matters.
Question 3: "From your Session 1 plan, what is one hook you will shoot today, and who is it for?"
Expected answer: a learner's own hook and their avatar.
Common gap: learners who did not complete the assignment. Pair them with someone who did, and ask them to adopt
that classmate's plan for today's practical (with permission).
Bridge to today (say this):
"Last time you decided what to say and who to say it to. A great hook written on paper is useless if the video looks dark
and shaky — people scroll before they hear your words. Today we make sure the picture earns the hook a chance."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1 or 2: spend 10 extra minutes reviewing niche/audience/hooks
from Session 1 before continuing, using the whiteboard. Shooting is only worthwhile if there is a plan behind it.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Your phone is a real camera — and the myths
The idea. The camera in a modern smartphone is genuinely capable of professional-quality video. The things that once
required expensive equipment — decent resolution, slow motion, good autofocus — are now in almost every phone.
Plain-English definition. "Shooting" means capturing the video and audio you will later edit. A shot is a single continuous
recording from one camera position.
Why the myth exists. People believe good video needs an expensive camera (a "DSLR" or "cinema camera"). Those cameras are
useful, but they only matter after the basics. A well-shot phone video beats a badly-shot professional-camera video almost
every time, because viewers care about clarity, stability, light and sound — not the brand of the camera.
The four factors that matter more than the camera:
1. Stability — no shaking.
2. Light — bright, even, on the subject's face.
3. Framing — the subject well placed in the frame.
4. Audio — clear sound (fully covered in Session 3).
Analogy. A phone camera is like a good pen. A skilled writer with a simple pen produces better work than a careless writer with
an expensive fountain pen. The tool helps, but the hand matters more.
Common misconception. "More megapixels = better video." For video, megapixels are far less important than light, stability
and focus. A 12-megapixel phone with good light beats a 48-megapixel phone shot in a dark room.
Limitation. Phone cameras struggle in very low light, produce "grainy" (noisy) footage, and have limited zoom before the
picture gets soft. We work with these limits: shoot in good light, avoid heavy digital zoom, and move the phone closer instead
of zooming.
How a learner recognises good phone footage. Steady, bright on the face, subject clearly placed, and easy to hear. If any of
those is missing, the phone is not the problem — the technique is.
F.2 Stability — no shaky video
The idea. Stability means the phone does not shake while recording. Shaky footage is one of the fastest ways to lose a viewer.
Why it exists as a skill. Handheld phones move with every heartbeat and breath. Without support, even a calm hand produces
micro-shakes that look unprofessional and can make viewers feel uncomfortable.
How to get stable footage:
1. Use a tripod or phone stand. This is the easiest and best method. Set it to the subject's eye level.
2. Improvise a stand when there is no tripod: stack books, rest the phone on a wall or table edge, or use a chair back. Make
sure it cannot slide or fall.
3. Brace your body if handheld: hold the phone with both hands, tuck your elbows into your ribs, plant your feet shoulder-
width apart, and hold your breath for a few seconds while recording.
4. Move slowly and deliberately if you must move; avoid fast pans.
5. Lean on something: a wall, a doorframe or a table can steady you.
Analogy. Holding a phone steady is like pouring water into a cup — small movements spill everywhere. Supporting the phone
is like setting the cup on a table first.
Worked example. For a talking-head clip of Adaeze Fashion's owner, the phone sits on a tripod at her eye level, about 1.5
metres away. No movement at all. For a handheld walkthrough of the shop, the operator holds the phone with both hands,
elbows tucked, and walks slowly to reduce bounce.
Common misconception. "A little shake is fine, editing will fix it." Video stabilisation in editors can help a little, but it crops and
softens the image and cannot fully rescue badly shaky footage. Get it right at the moment of shooting.
Limitation. Some artistic content uses intentional movement (a smooth reveal, a slow push-in). This is fine — intentional
movement is different from accidental shaking. The rule is: be in control of the camera.
F.3 Lighting — natural light first, ring light second, never backlight
The idea. Lighting is how the subject is lit. Good light is bright, soft and on the subject's face. Most beginner footage fails
because of light, not framing.
Why it exists as a skill. Phone cameras need light to record clean images. In dark scenes, the camera raises its "ISO" (light
sensitivity) and the image becomes grainy and soft. More light = cleaner picture.
The three working light sources for this course:
1. Natural window light. The best and cheapest source. Put the subject a step or two facing a window. Outside it may be the
sun; inside it is the window. Soft daylight is ideal.
2. Ring light. A circular light, often mounted around the phone or on a stand, that lights the face evenly and adds a nice small
catch in the eyes. Great for indoor talking-head videos, especially in the evening.
3. Simple fill. If one side of the face is much darker, add a second light (a ring light, a lamp, or a white reflective surface such
as a white wall or a piece of white card) on the dark side to soften shadows.
The most common lighting mistake — backlighting. Backlighting means the bright light (a window or the sun) is behind the
subject, so the camera sees a bright background and a dark face. The subject looks like a silhouette. Fix: turn the whole setup
around so the light is in front of the subject's face, or move the subject so they face the light.
Analogy. Lighting a person is like lighting a portrait on a wall. If the lamp is behind the painting, the painting looks dark. The
lamp must be in front unless you want a silhouette.
Worked example. Adaeze shoots at 10am in a room with one big window. She places her chair facing the window (not with her
back to it). The window floods her face with soft light; the background is a plain wall behind her. The result is a bright, even face
and a clean background.
Common misconception. "Brighter is always better." Not quite — hard light (direct harsh sun) creates ugly strong shadows
and makes people squint. Prefer soft light: open shade, a window, or a ring light. Also avoid mixed colours (a yellow bulb plus
blue daylight can make skin look odd).
Limitation. At night, natural light is gone, so a ring light or a well-placed lamp is essential. Phone "night mode" is for photos,
not ideal for smooth video; prefer adding light.
F.4 Framing and the rule of thirds
The idea. Framing is where you place the subject and the scene inside the phone screen. The rule of thirds is a simple guide for
placing things attractively: imagine the screen split into a 3×3 grid (two vertical lines, two horizontal lines). Put the important
things on or near those lines and their crossings, not dead centre.
Why it exists. The rule of thirds gives a balanced, professional look that "feels right" to the eye. Placing a person exactly in the
middle can look flat; placing them slightly to one side and their eyes near the top line looks natural.
How to apply it:
1. Turn on the phone's grid (a setting in almost every camera app), which draws the 3×3 lines for you.
2. For a talking-head video, place the subject on one of the vertical lines (left or right), not the exact centre.
3. Place the eyes near the top horizontal line.
4. Leave headroom — a little space above the head, not a large gap.
5. Use the empty side ("lead space" or "looking room") for on-screen text, so the text does not cover the face.
6. Keep the horizon level and on a horizontal line if shooting landscape.
Analogy. The rule of thirds is like hanging a picture on a wall — it looks best slightly off-centre and at the right height, not
shoved into the middle.
Worked example. A close-up of Adaeze speaking: her eyes sit on the top-left crossing point, she is placed on the left vertical
line, and the right two-thirds of the frame is empty background where we will add the caption text and the price.
Common misconception. "The subject must be in the centre." Centring is fine for some shots (a direct, symmetrical product
shot), but for most people-on-camera shots, the rule of thirds looks better. Both are valid tools.
Limitation. The rule of thirds is a guideline, not a law. Sometimes a centred or symmetrical frame is exactly right (a product
shot, a formal announcement). Use it as a reliable default.
F.5 Shot types — wide, medium, close, over-the-shoulder
The idea. A shot type describes how much of the subject and scene fills the frame. Mixing shot types keeps content interesting
and gives the editor choices later.
Shot type What it shows Best used for
Wide (establishing) The whole scene/room; the person is small Showing where we are; setting the scene
Medium The person from about the waist up Talking to camera; most common shot
Close-up The face, hands or a product in detail Emotion; showing a detail like fabric or a price tag
Over-the-shoulder Looking past the subject at what they see Demonstrations, unboxing, showing "how it's done"
Why mixing matters. If every shot is a medium shot of the same person, the video feels flat. A wide to open, a medium to talk,
a close-up to show the detail, and an over-the-shoulder to demonstrate makes the content feel alive — and gives the editor
material to cut between.
Analogy. Shot types are like the pages of a photo album: wide photos show the place, close photos show the smiles. Together
they tell a story.
Worked example. A 30-second Adaeze Fashion video might be: wide shot of the shop (establishing), medium shot of Adaeze
explaining the outfit (talking), close-up of the fabric and stitching (detail), over-the-shoulder shot of her packing the order
(action).
Common misconception. "Close-ups are only for faces." Close-ups are excellent for products, hands, textures and prices.
Limitation. Very wide shots on a phone can look empty or grainy; very tight close-ups may lose focus, because phones have a
"minimum focus distance" — if too close, the image blurs. Move back a little and zoom gently or tap the subject to refocus.
F.6 Vertical or horizontal — which platform wants which
The idea. Vertical footage is tall (like the phone held upright); horizontal footage is wide (like a TV). You choose based on
where the content will be watched.
Why it matters. Most short-form social platforms (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, WhatsApp status) are watched on
a phone held upright, so they prefer vertical. Longer, "cinema-style" content (a full YouTube video, a landscape product demo)
may suit horizontal.
Rule of thumb for this course: choose vertical (9:16) for the short 30–60 second videos in the capstone, because they are
made for phone-first platforms. Choose horizontal (16:9) only when you know the content is for a wide screen (a TV, a laptop,
YouTube long-form, or an in-store screen).
A trap to avoid — mixing orientations in one edit. If you shoot some clips vertical and some horizontal, the editor has to
crop or add big black bars, which looks messy. Decide the orientation before you shoot and keep it consistent for the whole
video.
Analogy. Vertical vs horizontal is like choosing a portrait or landscape painting to fit the wall you will hang it on. Match the
shape to the space.
Worked example. Adaeze Fashion's launch-week reels are all shot vertical, because they will be posted as Reels and TikToks.
The only horizontal footage they keep is a longer "about the shop" video for their Facebook page.
Common misconception. "I can always crop later." Cropping vertical from horizontal loses most of the frame and quality;
cropping horizontal from vertical adds bars. Shoot the right orientation now.
Limitation. Some phones default to the orientation of the first frame; check before recording, because rotating mid-clip
produces footage that is wrongly oriented.
F.7 Phone settings for a clean shot
The idea. A few simple camera settings improve every shot. You do not need to change much — just the right few.
Settings to set once:
1. Resolution and frame rate. Set video to 1080p (Full HD) at 30fps (frames per second) as the safe default. 1080p is sharp
enough and not too heavy. Some phones offer 4K, but 1080p is lighter to edit. Keep 30fps for normal talking and
demonstrations. (Higher fps like 60 is for smoother motion or slow-motion; it also takes more storage.)
2. Grid. Turn the grid on — it draws the rule-of-thirds lines.
3. Storage awareness. Check free storage before a shoot. HD video fills a phone quickly.
Settings to use while shooting: 4. Focus and exposure lock. Tap and hold on the subject's face until you see "AE/AF Lock"
(Auto Exposure / Auto Focus lock). This stops the camera from "hunting" for focus or changing brightness mid-shot. To unlock,
tap elsewhere. 5. Exposure adjustment. After locking, a little sun-slider appears; slide up to brighten, down to darken. Nudge
slightly brighter if the face is dark. 6. Do not use digital zoom. If you need to be closer, walk closer. Digital zoom makes the
picture soft. 7. Tap record and let it run with a small buffer. Start recording a couple of seconds before the action and stop a
couple of seconds after, so the editor has room to trim (you will use this in Session 4).
Analogy. These settings are like checking your car mirrors before you drive — small habits that prevent big problems.
Common misconception. "Higher resolution is always better." 4K is great but heavy; for phone-based editing, 1080p is the
smart default because it edits smoothly and saves storage.
Limitation. Different phones place the grid and focus-lock slightly differently. The instructor should demonstrate on a couple of
phones, or ask learners to find the settings on their own device with help.
F.8 Planning a shot list
The idea. A shot list is a simple written plan of the shots you will capture: what each shot shows, its type, and any notes. It is the
shooting version of the storyboard.
Why it exists. Without a shot list, you shoot randomly, then discover in editing that you are missing a key shot (for example, no
close-up of the product). With a shot list, you shoot with purpose and save time.
A shot list usually has:
Shot number (1, 2, 3…)
Shot type (wide / medium / close-up / over-the-shoulder)
Description (what happens / what is shown)
Notes (location, lighting, who is speaking, any text on screen)
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion, 30-second launch reel (vertical):
# Type Description Notes
1 Close-up Text on screen: "New drop" + hand pulls a dress off the rack By the window, bright
2 Medium Adaeze smiles and says the hook line Tripod, eye level, face the window
3 Close-up Fabric and stitching detail Slow, steady; tap to lock focus
4 Over-the-shoulder Adaeze packing the dress into a branded bag Rear camera; steady hands or tripod
5 Medium Adaeze says the call-to-action + price Same setup as shot 2
Analogy. A shot list is a shopping list. You go to the market with it so you come home with everything you need instead of
forgetting the onions.
Common misconception. "Shot lists are only for professionals." Beginners benefit most, because they forget shots they cannot
re-shoot easily.
Limitation. A shot list is a guide, not a script set in stone. If you spot a better shot on location, take it — then update the plan.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
One phone, one tripod, one ring light.
Two pre-recorded sample clips (bad and good) from Section D.
A window area and a plain wall.
A willing learner as the subject (or the instructor films themselves).
Worksheet 2.1 (Shot List) visible on projector.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — stability and framing (7 minutes)
Say: "Watch how the same shot changes when I fix stability and framing."
Do this step by step on the projector or big screen:
1. First, record a deliberately shaky shot: hold the phone one-handed, arms out, and pan quickly. Play it back. Ask: "How does
this feel?" (Uncomfortable.)
2. Now set the phone on the tripod at eye level. Turn on the grid. Place the subject on the left vertical line with eyes near the
top line.
3. Record a steady medium shot. Play it back.
4. Ask: "What changed?" (Steady, balanced, professional.)
Expected result: a visibly steadier, better-framed clip. Reasoning to state aloud: "The camera didn't change — the support,
the grid and the placement did."
Deliberate error to show and correct: Put the subject dead centre with too much headroom, then correct it to the rule of
thirds. Say: "Small changes, big difference."
Questions to ask: "Where should the eyes sit?" "What do we do with the empty side of the frame?" (Leave it for text.)
G.3 Demonstration 2 — lighting, then the backlight mistake (8 minutes)
Say: "Light is the number-one thing beginners get wrong. I will show you both the right way and the wrong way."
Step by step:
1. Put the subject facing the window. Record a 10-second clip. Play it back. Point out the bright, even face.
2. Now move the subject so the window is behind them. Record again. Play it back. The face is now dark — this is
backlighting.
3. Ask: "Which looks like something a brand would post?" Let learners explain.
4. Fix it: move the subject back to face the window, or add a ring light in front. Record again — bright again.
5. Turn off the window light metaphorically by moving to a dim corner, then switch on the ring light and show how it lights
the face evenly.
Expected result: learners can instantly tell good front-lighting from bad backlighting and describe how to fix it.
Errors to watch and correct:
Subject squinting in harsh sun → move to open shade or a window.
Ring light too close/harsh → move it back, or diffuse by angling it.
Mixed colour light (window + yellow bulb) → turn one off or match them.
Questions to ask: "Which direction is the light coming from?" "Is the light on the face or behind the head?" "If this were posted
now, would people see her face?"
G.4 Demonstration 3 — a quick shot list walk-through (optional if time)
Show the Adaeze Fashion shot list (Section F.8) on the projector and explain how each line tells the shooter what to capture and
why. Emphasise that the editor in Week 2 will be grateful for the close-ups and over-the-shoulder shots.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner or pair shoots a short clip (about 10–15 seconds) applying good lighting
and the rule of thirds, using a tripod/stand or braced stance.
Starting materials: A phone, tripod/stand (shared), a window area, Worksheet 2.2 for framing sketches, and one hook line from
Session 1.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners work; instructor walks the room):
1. Open the camera app and turn the grid on (settings).
2. Set video to 1080p, 30fps (or the closest available).
3. Choose your location: face the strongest light (a window or outdoors in shade). Never put the light behind the subject.
4. Set the phone on the tripod/stand at the subject's eye level, or brace it firmly and stand steady.
5. Frame using the rule of thirds: place the subject on a vertical line; put the eyes near the top horizontal line; leave the
empty side for text.
6. Tap and hold on the face to lock focus and exposure; nudge the exposure slider so the face is bright but not blown out.
7. Pause and predict: before recording, ask the pair: "If we post this right now, is the face bright and is the framing balanced?
Predict whether viewers would keep watching."
8. Record 10–15 seconds. Have the subject say the hook line from Session 1. Hold still. Count three seconds before and
after the speech.
9. Play it back. Check: steady? bright face? subject on a thirds line? clear enough?
10. Improve in one take: change one thing (move closer for a medium shot, or add fill light) and record again.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 5: "Where is the empty space, and what will we put there?" (Text/caption.)
After step 7: "Will this be too dark or too bright? Why?"
Expected result: each learner/pair has at least two clips — a first attempt and an improved one — that are steady, well-lit and
framed on the rule of thirds.
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Face is dark → light is behind the subject; turn the setup around.
Footage is shaky → use the stand, or brace elbows into the ribs.
Subject in the centre → turn on the grid and move them to a line.
Blurry subject → too close; move back slightly and tap to focus.
"AE/AF Lock" won't hold → different phones differ; demonstrate on that device.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Grid is on and used.
Video resolution set to 1080p/30fps.
Subject is facing the light (no backlight).
Focus/exposure locked on the face.
Subject placed on a rule-of-thirds line.
Clip is steady.
At least two takes, with one improvement made.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Emeka is the media volunteer at a mid-sized church in Port Harcourt. He records the Sunday service on his phone to
post a short recap clip during the week. His clips always look dark (the church auditorium has big windows behind the pulpit),
sound far away, and are often shaky because he films standing in the aisle. The pastor is happy the church is posting, but the
clips get few views and members do not share them.
Relevant facts.
The strongest light (the windows) is behind the preacher — classic backlighting.
Emeka films handheld from the back of the hall, so the subject is far away and tiny.
He never plans shots; he just records and posts.
The phone and the church's willingness are both fine; the technique is the problem.
Decision point. Should Emeka (a) keep filming the same way, (b) buy an expensive camera to "fix" it, or (c) change his shooting
technique — reposition for light, get closer, stabilise and plan shots?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep filming as before. No change, so no improvement.
2. Buy a better camera. The camera is not the problem; the same backlight and distance would still hurt a pricier camera.
3. Change the technique. Move to a position where the preacher faces the light (or bring a light in front), shoot from nearer
the front, brace the phone or use a small tripod, and plan three key shots (wide of the auditorium, medium of the preacher,
close-up of a worshipper's hands or the choir).
Recommended solution. Approach 3. It costs nothing but attention and produces a dramatic improvement.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 repeats the failure.
Approach 2 spends money without solving the actual cause.
Approach 3 requires Emeka to move during the service and plan ahead, but it directly fixes lighting, distance and stability
— the real causes.
Professional connection. Every professional videographer scouts the location for light and chooses camera positions before
shooting. The rule "face the light, get close, stay steady, plan your shots" is exactly what paid shooters do. Emeka's fix is a
professional habit, not a beginner trick.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
K.1 The subject's face is dark (backlighting)
Symptoms: The background is bright but the face is dark or shadowed. Likely cause: The main light (window/sun) is behind
the subject. How to investigate: Look at where the light is coming from relative to the subject. Corrective steps: Turn the
setup so the subject faces the light, or move the subject, or add a ring light in front. How to confirm resolved: The face is
clearly bright on the playback. Prevention: Before every shot, ask "which way is the light coming from?"
K.2 Footage is shaky
Symptoms: The image moves, wobbles or bounces. Likely cause: Handheld one-handed filming; fast movement; heavy
breathing. How to investigate: Watch the playback; notice where the shake happens. Corrective steps: Use a tripod/stand;
brace elbows into the ribs; plant feet; record in shorter, still segments. How to confirm resolved: The playback is smooth.
Prevention: Default to a stand; only hand-hold with a braced stance.
K.3 Subject is blurry
Symptoms: Soft or out-of-focus subject. Likely cause: The phone is too close (closer than the minimum focus distance); focus
was not locked; or the phone moved. How to investigate: Tap the subject and watch whether it sharpens. Corrective steps:
Move back a little; tap and hold to lock focus; keep the phone still. How to confirm resolved: Edges and eyes are sharp on
playback. Prevention: Lock focus before recording and respect the minimum distance.
K.4 Everything is in the centre
Symptoms: The subject sits dead centre with no balance. Likely cause: Grid is off; habit. How to investigate: Turn on the grid
and look at the 3×3 lines. Corrective steps: Move the subject onto a vertical line; place eyes near the top line. How to
confirm resolved: Framing feels balanced and there is space for text. Prevention: Always shoot with the grid on.
K.5 Footage is grainy or "noisy"
Symptoms: The image looks speckled, especially in dark areas. Likely cause: Not enough light; the camera raised its sensitivity.
How to investigate: Check the scene's brightness. Corrective steps: Add light (window or ring light); move to a brighter spot.
How to confirm resolved: The image is cleaner and smoother. Prevention: Never shoot in dim light if you can add or move to
light.
K.6 Mixed orientation in one project
Symptoms: Some clips are tall, some are wide; editing shows black bars. Likely cause: The shooter forgot to keep the phone in
one orientation. How to investigate: Play back all clips together. Corrective steps: Decide vertical or horizontal and re-shoot
any wrong-orientation clips that cannot be trimmed. How to confirm resolved: All clips match. Prevention: Announce the
orientation at the top of every shoot and check before each clip.
K.7 The wrong thing is in focus
Symptoms: The background is sharp but the subject is soft, or focus keeps changing. Likely cause: Autofocus chose the wrong
target; no focus lock. How to investigate: Tap the subject and watch the focus shift. Corrective steps: Tap and hold on the
face to lock focus before recording. How to confirm resolved: The subject stays sharp throughout. Prevention: Always lock
focus on the main subject.
Likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after checking it — for example, seeing which direction the
light comes from, or watching the playback for shake. Always confirm before correcting.

Q2. Multiple choice. The subject's face is dark but the window behind them is bright. This is called: a) Overexposure b)

Q4. Practical identification. Name each shot type: a) A person shown from the waist up talking to camera b) A close view of a

Q5. Error / scenario. A learner records a clip in a dark room, handheld, one-handed, and then zooms in with digital zoom. List
three problems this will cause and one fix for each.
Q6. Short answer. Why should you record two extra seconds before and after each shot?
Q7. Explain in your own words. When is horizontal (landscape) the better choice, and when is vertical better?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. Stability, light, framing and audio.
A2. (b) Backlighting.
A3. The rule of thirds is a framing guideline that divides the screen into a 3×3 grid; you place the subject on or near one of the
vertical lines and their eyes near the top horizontal line, keeping a little headroom and leaving empty space on one side (for
text). It makes the shot balanced and professional.
A4. a) Medium shot, b) Close-up, c) Wide (establishing) shot, d) Over-the-shoulder shot.
A5. Problems and fixes (any three): (1) grainy/dark footage → add light (window or ring light); (2) shaky footage → use a tripod
or brace the phone; (3) soft/blurry picture from digital zoom → move closer instead of zooming. (Also accept: focus problems
→ lock focus.)
A6. Because the extra footage gives the editor room to trim cleanly in and out of the shot (a "handle" or buffer), so the final
video does not start or end abruptly.
A7. Horizontal suits wide screens: a full YouTube video, a laptop/TV, or an in-store screen. Vertical suits phone-first platforms:
TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and WhatsApp status.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner sets up a steady shot using a stand or braced stance without being told.
Learner checks the light direction before shooting and keeps the face bright.
Learner uses the grid and places the subject on a thirds line.
Learner chooses and names shot types deliberately, not randomly.
Learner locks focus/exposure, avoids digital zoom, and adds a buffer.
Learner can explain why each shot was chosen.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"I need an expensive camera." → Correct: technique beats gear; phone + light + hold matters most.
"Editing will fix the shake." → Correct: stabilisation only helps a little; shoot it steady.
"More megapixels/4K is always better." → Correct: 1080p is the smart default for phone editing.
"Centre is always best." → Correct: the rule of thirds usually looks better for people.
"Digital zoom is fine." → Correct: walk closer instead.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Steady hand drill: record the same object three ways — one-handed, two-handed braced, on a tripod — and compare.
2. Light hunt: in one room, find three spots and describe the light in each; shoot the same subject in all three.
3. Framing over the grid: give printed frames with a grid and ask learners to sketch where the eyes and subject should go.
4. Shot-type scavenger hunt: with one subject, capture a wide, medium, close-up and over-the-shoulder, and label each.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a pre-written shot list with types and only the descriptions blank; pair the learner with a strong
partner for the shoot.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise and assignment as written.
Level 3 (challenge): add a deliberate, smooth camera movement (a slow push-in or a reveal) while keeping the shot stable,
and shoot a 10-second "before/after" lighting comparison where the learner explains both clips in one spoken take.
Timing guidance. Lighting is the highest-value skill today, so protect Demonstration 2 (lighting and backlighting) and the
guided practical. If time is short, reduce the knowledge check to Q1–Q4 and shorten the independent exercise to five shots
planned and only two or three shot. Never cut the guided practical — it produces the footage the learner needs for Week 2.
