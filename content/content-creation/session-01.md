# Content Foundations: Niche, Audience and Hooks

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: See and Plan (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (no prior experience assumed)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** None. A learner only needs to own or have access to a smartphone with a camera and be able to open apps on it. Connection to previous session This is the first session. It replaces the old habit of "posting randomly and hoping" with a clear method: choose a niche, know your audience, pick pillars, and lead with a strong hook. Every later session (shooting, editing, writing, publishing) depends on the decisions made today.

**Preparation before class:** On the projector: this session's slides or the three worksheets printed. Have the running capstone example ready — a fictional Port Harcourt fashion business called Adaeze Fashion — with a simple logo or name card on screen. Have Worksheet 1.1 ("My Niche and Audience") and Worksheet 1.2 ("Three Hooks and a Storyboard Seed") printed, one set per learner. Ensure the Wi-Fi is on in case learners want to look at example pages. Have a whiteboard and markers. No software is required for this session.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words what content creation is and give one example of content that gives value to an audience.
2. Define a niche and explain why a focused niche beats posting about "everything".
3. Build a simple audience avatar naming who the audience is, what they need, and what stops them from getting it.
4. Choose three content pillars for a chosen niche and explain what kind of content belongs in each.
5. Explain what a hook is and why the first three seconds of a video decide whether the rest is watched.
6. Write three hook lines for a chosen topic in spoken, natural language (not labels).
7. Distinguish value-driven posting from random posting and justify a posting choice using the audience avatar.
8. Produce a storyboard seed — a three-line plan of the beginning, middle and end — for one of their hook ideas.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Before class, write two questions on the board:
1. Which Instagram/TikTok accounts do you always stop to watch? 2. How many of them make you buy something,
learn something, or laugh?
What the instructor says:
"Raise your hand if you have ever scrolled past a video in one second because it looked boring. (Most hands go up.) Now
raise your hand if you have ever watched a whole video twice because it was interesting. (Many hands.)
The difference between those two videos is not the phone. Both were probably shot on a phone like yours. The difference is
planning. Somebody decided who the video was for and what the first second would say. That is content creation. Today
we learn the thinking behind the video — before we ever touch a camera."
Concrete hook (do this): Show or say three real first-lines (hooks) and ask learners to pick which one would make them keep
watching:
1. "Welcome to my channel, please subscribe."
2. "Adaeze Fashion — quality clothing."
3. "This ₦8,000 dress made a bride's sister cry at her wedding."
Expected learner response: Learners almost always choose line 3 and can often say why — it tells a story, creates curiosity,
mentions a price, and promises emotion. Confirm their instinct: the first line is called a hook, and it decides whether anyone
hears the rest.
Why this opener works: It starts from something every learner already does (scrolling), proves they already have taste, and
creates the exact problem the course solves: not "how do I get a good camera" but "how do I make people stop and watch."

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Since this is Session 1, there is no technical prerequisite. Instead, run a quick experience round to surface what learners already
know and to build confidence. Put these three questions on the board and take short answers.
Question 1: "Name one business near your house that people talk about a lot. Why do people talk about it?"
Expected answers: good food, good service, affordable price, funny owner, nice packaging.
Purpose: this shows that "attention" comes from something specific and repeatable — not luck. That thing can be
planned.
Question 2: "When you open YouTube or TikTok, what makes you decide to keep watching?"
Expected answers: interesting start, it's funny, it's useful, someone I like is in it, the picture is clear.
Purpose: connect to the idea of the hook and to quality.
Question 3: "Have you ever posted something and nobody watched it? Do you know why?"
Expected answers: "I don't know", "maybe bad timing", "I didn't know who it was for".
Purpose: name the gap. Most beginners do not know who they were talking to. Today we fix that with a niche and an
audience avatar.
Gap handling: If the room is quiet, ask in pairs first ("turn to your neighbour, one minute"), then collect. Do not correct any
answer as "wrong" — every honest answer is useful data. Bridge with this line:
"You already know when something is good to watch. Today we turn that instinct into a method any business can use."
If the class is very new to the topic: spend two extra minutes asking each learner to say, in one sentence, a business or person
they would like to make content for. This becomes their working project for the rest of the course.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What content creation actually is
The idea. Content creation is the work of planning, making and sharing material (videos, photos, graphics, captions) that gives
something useful, entertaining or interesting to a specific audience.
Plain-English definition. Content creation is not "posting". Posting is pressing a button. Content creation is everything that
comes before and after the button: deciding who you are talking to, what you will say, how you will shoot it, how you will edit
it, the words you put around it, and when you put it out.
Why it exists. Businesses, churches and people need attention to grow, but attention is scarce — people scroll past thousands
of posts a day. Content creation exists as a craft because some posts stop the scroll and most do not, and the difference can be
learned.
What it does. Good content does at least one of these for the audience:
Informs (a shop announces a new stock or a price),
Teaches (a tailor shows how to measure a shoulder),
Entertains (a restaurant owner does a funny skit),
Inspires (a church shares a short encouraging word),
Connects (a personal brand shares an honest story).
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader versus a quiet shop. The quiet shop keeps its doors closed and hopes people
come. The market trader calls out, shows samples, arranges goods attractively, and knows exactly which customers buy what.
Content creation is the trader's skill: showing and telling the right people, in the right way.
Common misconception. "Content creation is about being famous." Not true. A tailor with 200 local followers who buy is more
successful than a random account with 50,000 followers who buy nothing. We create content to reach and serve a specific
audience, not merely to collect followers.
Limitation. Content creation cannot fix a bad product. Content can bring people to a business; it cannot make them happy if
the product or service is poor. Content and quality must work together.
How a learner recognises content creation in the wild. Look at any account that grows steadily. You will find a clear topic
(niche), a consistent look (branding), strong first lines (hooks), and regular posting. None of that happens by accident.
F.2 Choosing a niche — focus beats "everything"
The idea. A niche is the specific subject or area your content focuses on.
Plain-English definition. Your niche answers the question: "What am I mainly about?" Examples: affordable fashion for young
women; local Port Harcourt food; church teaching clips; small-business photography tips; secondary-school maths lessons.
Why niche matters. Social platforms and audiences reward focus. If your page is about everything — fashion today, football
tomorrow, prayer the next day — the platform does not know who to show it to, and the audience does not know why to
follow. A clear niche tells both the platform and the audience exactly what to expect. Expectation builds trust; trust builds
growth.
What a good niche looks like. It is usually the overlap of three things:
1. What you know or can produce (your skill or access).
3. What you can keep doing (turning up regularly, even when you are busy).
Write this on the board as three overlapping circles. The niche is the middle.
Analogy. A niche is like a signboard on a shop. "Shoes and bags" tells a customer instantly. "We sell many things" tells them
nothing, so they walk past.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion. Adaeze sells clothes in Port Harcourt. "Fashion" alone is too broad. Narrow it:
affordable ready-to-wear for young working women in Port Harcourt. Now we know who she is for: young women who work
and want to look good without spending a fortune. This is specific enough to plan a week of posts around.
Misconception 1: "A niche is too small — I'll run out of things to post." In practice, a narrower niche makes posting easier,
because everything you shoot is on-topic. Misconception 2: "I must pick forever." No. Start focused; you can widen or adjust
later once you know what your audience responds to.
Limitation. A niche is a starting boundary, not a cage. The same business can still post a variety of formats (video, photo, story,
testimonial) — as long as they serve the same audience.
F.3 Defining the target audience — the audience avatar
The idea. Your target audience is the specific group of people your content is made for. An audience avatar is a simple,
friendly description of one typical person in that group, so you stop talking to "everybody" and start talking to a real person in
your head.
Why it exists. When you try to speak to everyone, you move nobody. When you picture one person — Mama Ngozi who runs
a small restaurant, or Chinedu who just finished NYSC — your words become specific, warm and useful, and specific content
performs better.
What an avatar includes (keep it short):
Name and age (make a realistic person).
What they do (job, business, school, church, family).
What they need (the problem your content helps with).
What stops them (money, time, trust, fear, location).
Where they spend time online (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, WhatsApp status).
What they react to (price, honesty, humour, results, faith).
Analogy. An avatar is like writing a letter to one friend instead of shouting into a market. A letter can be personal; a shout
cannot.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion's avatar.
Field Example
Name Blessing
Age 27
What she does Works at a bank in Port Harcourt, lives in Rumuokoro
What she needs Smart, affordable outfits she can wear to work and church
What stops her Busy; dislikes long shopping trips; afraid of being overcharged
Where she is online Instagram and WhatsApp status in the evening
What she reacts to Price shown clearly, real photos, fast replies
Now every Adaeze Fashion post can ask: "Would Blessing stop for this? Does it answer her worry about price? Can she act on it
easily?"
Common misconception. "My audience is everyone." Say gently: if everyone is your audience, no one feels spoken to. Also: the
avatar is a helpful picture, not a rule that excludes people — it just keeps your message sharp.
Limitation. An avatar is a starting guess. Real comments, questions and DMs are the ground truth. Update the avatar as you
learn what your real audience actually asks.
F.4 Content pillars — the three (or four) themes you rotate
The idea. Content pillars are the two to four main themes your content rotates around within your niche. If the niche is your
shop, the pillars are the shelves.
Why they exist. Without pillars, you stare at your phone asking "what do I post today?" Pillars answer that question ahead of
time. They also keep your content balanced: not all selling, not all jokes, not all teaching.
Common pillar sets. A simple and reliable set for a small business:
1. Value / teaching — tips and how-tos that help the audience (e.g., "3 ways to style one skirt").
2. Showcase / product — the actual products or services, shown attractively.
3. Trust / behind-the-scenes — real people, real process, real stories (the tailor at work, a happy customer).
4. Connection / humour or faith or community — light, human content that builds warmth.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion's pillars:
Style tips (how to combine outfits for work and church)
New arrivals & offers (clear price posts)
Customer stories (a real buyer wearing the outfit; behind-the-scenes of packing orders)
Analogy. Pillars are like a restaurant menu with sections: starters, mains, drinks. Customers know what to expect, and you
always know what to cook.
Common misconception. "Every post must sell." If every post says "buy, buy, buy", the audience gets tired and stops following.
Teaching and trust content earns the right to sell occasionally.
Limitation. Pillars should not be so rigid that you ignore a timely opportunity (e.g., a festival or a customer request). Pillars
guide; they do not forbid.
F.5 The hook — why the first 3 seconds decide everything
The idea. A hook is the opening of your content — the first sentence, the first frame, the first three seconds — designed to
make a person stop scrolling and keep watching.
Why it exists. People scroll fast. If the first moment is slow, unclear or dull, they leave, and nothing later can be seen. The hook
is the doorway: if nobody comes through the door, the beautiful room inside does not matter.
Plain-English definition. A hook is a reason to keep watching, given in the very first second or two.
What makes a strong hook (the C-P-C pattern):
C — Curiosity: make them want to know the rest ("You have been tying your wrapper the wrong way").
P — Problem or promise: name the pain or the reward ("This 1 trick saves ₦2,000 per style").
C — Clarity: still tell them what the video is about — mystery without meaning bores people.
Worked example — turning a weak opener into a hook.
Weak (no hook) Strong (hook)
"Hi everyone, welcome to my page." "If you have less than 5 minutes to dress, watch this."
"Today I want to talk about dresses." "This ₦8,000 dress made a bride's sister cry."
"Please follow for more fashion." "Three outfits, one skirt — under ₦12,000."
How long is "the hook"? On short-form video, roughly the first 3 seconds (sometimes 1 second). It can be a spoken line, on-
screen text, a movement, or a striking first image — often all of them together.
Analogy. A hook is like the first page of a novel or the first drumbeat of a song. If the opening does not grab you, you close
the book or change the song.
Common misconception. "The hook is only the words." No — the first visual matters just as much. A dark, shaky first frame
with no face and no text can lose people even with a clever line.
Limitation. A hook promises; the body of the video must deliver on that promise. A hook that tricks people into watching
something disappointing loses trust and hurts future posts. Honest hooks only.
F.6 Value-driven posting versus random posting
The idea. Value-driven posting means every post is made with a clear reason and a clear audience in mind. Random posting
means posting whatever comes to mind, whenever, with no plan.
Why it matters. Random posting is tiring and inconsistent — you post for two days and disappear for two weeks, and the
audience never learns what to expect. Value-driven posting is repeatable: you know your niche, your avatar, your pillars and your
hooks, so you can plan a week in one sitting.
A simple test for any proposed post. Ask these three questions:
1. Who is this for? (Name the avatar.)
2. What does it give them? (Inform, teach, entertain, inspire or connect — choose one.)
3. What should they do next? (A clear next step — the call-to-action, taught in Session 6.)
If you cannot answer all three, the post is probably random.
Analogy. Value-driven posting is like a shop that opens at the same time every day with goods arranged on purpose.
Random posting is a shop that opens whenever the owner feels like it and sells whatever is left in the store.
Misconception. "Being planned means being boring or fake." No — planning makes space for your real personality, because
you are no longer panicking about what to post.
Limitation. Value-driven posting still requires consistency to work. One excellent planned post a month will not grow an
audience; steady, on-topic posting will. Consistency is taught properly in Session 7.
F.7 The running example — Adaeze Fashion
Throughout this course we use one fictional Port Harcourt business so every learner can see the same method applied end to
end. Introduce it clearly today:
Adaeze Fashion — a small ready-to-wear fashion business in Port Harcourt, run by a young woman named Adaeze. It
sells affordable clothes to young working women. Its content niche is "affordable, smart outfits for young working women in
Port Harcourt." Its audience avatar is Blessing (Section F.3). Its pillars are style tips, new arrivals/offers, and customer
stories. It is preparing for a launch week and needs a full week of content — which becomes the capstone.
Learners may adopt Adaeze Fashion or bring their own small business/personal brand with instructor approval. Using one
shared example makes it easier to compare work and give feedback, while still allowing learners to personalise.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)

This session's demonstration is a live planning demonstration on the board and projector. The instructor builds an entire
content plan for a business the class can see, thinking aloud.

Whiteboard split into four areas: Niche, Avatar, Pillars, Hooks.
A simple "business card" on the projector: a second fictional business, different from Adaeze Fashion, so learners see the

The three example captions from Section C (one strong, two weak).

G.3 Demonstration 2 — pillars and a hook (10 minutes)
Say: "Now I choose three pillars and write a hook that would stop Emeka's thumb."
Step by step on the board (Pillars column):
1. Value/teaching: "How to eat well on ₦1,500 a day in Port Harcourt."
2. Showcase/offer: "Today's plate: rice, beans, stew and chicken."
3. Trust/behind-the-scenes: "How we cook our stew from scratch every morning."
Then write hooks (Hooks column) and evaluate against the C-P-C pattern (Curiosity, Problem/Promise, Clarity):
Weak: "Come and buy our food." → No curiosity, no reason.
Better: "This ₦1,500 lunch keeps office workers full until 6pm." → Promise + clarity + price.
Strong: "If lunch always finishes your salary by the 10th, watch this." → Curiosity + problem + clarity.
Ask learners to rate each hook using the pattern. Expected result: learners can point out that strong hooks name a real
problem and still say what the video is about.
Deliberately demonstrate a weak hook and fix it: Show the three captions from Section C. Ask the class to rank them, then
rewrite the weakest one together on the board.
Errors to watch and correct:
Hooks written as labels ("fashion video") instead of spoken lines. Fix: say it the way a person talks.
Clickbait hooks that promise something the video does not deliver. Explain that trust is the real asset.
Overlong hooks — the hook should fit in the first breath.
Questions to ask throughout: "Who is this for?", "What does it give them?", "Would Emeka stop scrolling?", "Does the hook
deliver what it promises?"

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner produces a one-page plan for a business or personal brand of
their choice: a niche, an audience avatar, three content pillars, and three hooks.
Starting materials: Worksheet 1.1 ("My Niche and Audience Avatar"), Worksheet 1.2 ("Three Hooks and a Storyboard Seed"), a
pen, and (optionally) a phone for reference.
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners work; instructor walks the room):
1. Choose your subject. Write the name of one business or personal brand you will plan for this week. It may be your own, a
family business, a church, or a fictional one. Example: "Chisom's Salon" or "Adaeze Fashion".
2. Write the raw description in one line: what does it sell or offer, and to whom roughly? Example: "A salon in Port Harcourt
that does braids and natural hair."
3. Narrow it into a niche using the three circles (what you know / what people want / what you can keep doing). Write it in
one clear sentence.
4. Build your audience avatar in the table on Worksheet 1.1: name, age, what they do, what they need, what stops them,
where they are online, what they react to.
5. Pause and predict: Before moving on, ask learners to say aloud: "My avatar's biggest worry is ____." (e.g., "cost", "time",
"trust"). This worry should shape the hooks.
6. Choose three content pillars. Pick from: value/teaching, showcase/product, trust/behind-the-scenes,
connection/humour/faith. Write a one-line example post idea under each pillar.
7. Write three hooks for your business, using the C-P-C pattern. Each hook must be a spoken line, not a label. Example for
the salon: "If your braids always pull at the edges, this style protects your hairline."
8. Check each hook against the three questions: Who is it for? What does it give them? Does it say clearly what the video is
about?
9. Storyboard seed (Worksheet 1.2): pick your best hook and write three quick lines — Beginning (the hook moment),
Middle (the main point), End (what the viewer should do next). This is the seed of a storyboard you will draw properly in
Session 3.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 4: "Will our hooks talk about price, speed, or results? It depends on the avatar's worry."
After step 7: "Which of your three hooks would make you stop scrolling? Why?"
Expected result: each learner has a completed Worksheet 1.1 (niche + avatar + pillars) and the top half of Worksheet 1.2 (three
hooks), plus a three-line storyboard seed.
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner stuck on niche → ask "What could you make 20 posts about without getting bored?"
Avatar too broad ("everybody") → ask "Give me one specific person's name and job."
Hooks sound like labels → ask the learner to say it out loud to you as if to a friend; write down what they said.
Hooks all about selling → remind them of the pillars; ask for one teaching hook and one trust hook.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
I wrote one clear niche sentence.
I completed the audience avatar with a real obstacle.
I chose three content pillars with one example idea each.
I wrote three hook lines in spoken language.
I checked each hook with the three questions.
I wrote a three-line storyboard seed.

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)

Task: Plan content for a different business on your own, without copying your practical work or your neighbour's.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi runs a small catering business from her kitchen in Port Harcourt. She cooks for small events — birthdays,
church gatherings, office parties. She has a smartphone and posts occasionally, but her posts are inconsistent. Sometimes she
posts a photo of a pot of jollof rice with the caption "Better food 4 u". Sometimes she posts nothing for three weeks. She gets
almost no bookings from her page; all her customers come by word of mouth. She is frustrated and wants to give up on "social
media".
Relevant facts.
Ngozi cooks very well; her word-of-mouth reputation is strong.
Her page does not say what she does, who she cooks for, or how to book.
She posts whatever she is doing that day, with no plan.
She has never chosen a niche or described an audience.
Competitors who post clean, clear content get more enquiries even though their food is not better.
Decision point. Should Ngozi (a) keep posting random food photos and hope, (b) give up on content and rely only on word of
mouth, or (c) pick a niche, define her audience, choose pillars and write strong hooks?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep posting randomly. Free, but it is not working and wastes effort.
2. Abandon content entirely. Word of mouth still works, but it does not scale and she stays invisible to new customers.
3. Plan like a creator. Ngozi chooses a niche ("affordable event catering for small church and office gatherings in Port
Harcourt"), builds an avatar (a church committee member booking food for 40 people), picks pillars (menus/prices, behind-
the-scenes cooking, happy customers), and writes hooks such as "Feeding 40 people on a small budget? This menu did it."
Recommended solution. Approach 3. It costs nothing but an hour of planning, and it turns her existing skill and reputation into
visible, bookable content.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 requires no effort but repeats the failure.
Approach 2 protects her from effort but caps her growth.
Approach 3 takes a planning session and consistent effort, but every post now works toward bookings, and her excellent
cooking finally gets discovered.
Professional connection. Every content agency and social media manager starts by asking the same four questions: What is the
niche? Who is the audience? What are the pillars? What are the hooks? Ngozi's problem is the exact problem professional
creators solve. Learners who master this session's skills can charge to solve it for businesses like hers.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live guide. When a learner gets stuck, ask them to check the relevant row before giving the answer.
K.1 The niche is too broad
Symptoms: The niche reads like "fashion", "food", "music" or "everybody". Likely cause: The learner is afraid to leave people
out. How to investigate: Ask, "Can you name 10 different posts that all clearly belong to this topic?" If not, it is too broad.
Corrective steps: Narrow by audience (who), place (where) or problem (what they need). Example: not "food", but "affordable
office lunches in Port Harcourt". How to confirm resolved: The learner can name three on-topic pillars and one specific person
the content is for. Prevention: Always finish a niche by naming the audience it serves.
K.2 The audience avatar is really "everyone"
Symptoms: Avatar row says "men and women, all ages" or "anybody who likes fashion". Likely cause: The learner has not
pictured a real person. How to investigate: Ask for one person's name, age and job. Corrective steps: Build the avatar around
one realistic person, like Blessing or Emeka. Everyone is still welcome to consume the content — the avatar just sharpens the
message. How to confirm resolved: The learner can state the avatar's biggest worry in one sentence. Prevention: Name the
obstacle, not just the person.
K.3 Pillars all say "sell"
Symptoms: All three pillars are variations of "promote the product". Likely cause: The learner thinks content equals advertising.
How to investigate: Count how many pillars teach, help or build trust. Corrective steps: Replace at least one pillar with
value/teaching and one with trust/behind-the-scenes. How to confirm resolved: At least one pillar would be useful even to
someone who never buys. Prevention: Rotate teaching and trust between selling posts.
K.4 Hooks are labels, not spoken lines
Symptoms: Hooks read like "fashion tips video" or "product post". Likely cause: The learner is describing the post instead of
opening it. How to investigate: Ask the learner to say the hook aloud to you as if to a friend. Corrective steps: Write down
exactly what they said aloud. That spoken version is the hook. How to confirm resolved: The hook sounds natural when
spoken and creates curiosity or names a problem. Prevention: Always test hooks by saying them out loud.
K.5 Hooks that overpromise (clickbait)
Symptoms: The hook promises something the video never delivers. Likely cause: The learner is chasing attention only. How to
investigate: Ask, "Does the video actually show or answer this?" Corrective steps: Change the hook to promise only what the
content honestly gives. How to confirm resolved: After watching the content, the learner still believes the hook was fair.
Prevention: Remember that trust is the asset; keep hooks honest.
K.6 Nobody can tell what the business is
Symptoms: After the plan, a stranger still cannot say what the business sells or to whom. Likely cause: The niche sentence is
vague. How to investigate: Show the niche sentence to a classmate and ask them to explain it back. Corrective steps: Rewrite
until a classmate can repeat it correctly. How to confirm resolved: A classmate can restate the niche and audience without
help. Prevention: Test every niche sentence on one other person.
Likely vs confirmed: Each "likely cause" becomes a "confirmed cause" only after you check it — for example, by trying to
name 10 on-topic posts, or by asking a classmate to explain the niche back. Always confirm before correcting.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers are in the Instructor Key below. Do not show learners the key until they have attempted all
questions.)
Q1. Short answer. In your own words, what is content creation, and how is it different from just "posting"?
Q2. Multiple choice. Which is the best example of a specific niche? a) Fashion b) Food c) Affordable ready-to-wear for young
working women in Port Harcourt d) Everything I like
Q3. Explain in your own words. Why is it a problem to say "my audience is everybody"?
Q4. Practical identification. Look at this avatar row and say what is missing: "Audience: young people who like nice things."
Q5. Error / scenario. A learner writes this hook: "Fashion video, watch it." Explain two things wrong with it and rewrite it as a
stronger hook.
Q6. Short answer. Name the three parts of the C-P-C hook pattern and give a one-line example that uses all three.
Q7. Explain in your own words. Why do content pillars make posting easier?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. Content creation is the whole work of planning, making and sharing material that gives value to a specific audience —
choosing who it is for, what it says, how it is shot and edited, and the words around it. "Posting" is just pressing the button;
content creation is everything before and after the button.
A2. (c) — it names a clear audience, product and place.
A3. Because if you speak to everyone, no one feels spoken to; the platform cannot tell who to show your content to, and your
message becomes vague. A specific avatar makes the wording sharp and useful.
A4. Missing: a specific audience (a real person), what they need, and what stops them. It should name one real person, their
need and their obstacle.
A5. Problems: (1) it is a label, not a spoken line; (2) it gives no curiosity, promise or reason to watch — it doesn't say who it is
for or what it delivers. Strong rewrite example: "This ₦8,000 dress made a bride's sister cry at the wedding." (Accept any hook
that is a spoken line and uses C-P-C.)
A6. C = Curiosity (make them want to know more), P = Problem or Promise (name the pain or reward), C = Clarity (still say
what the video is about). Example: "If lunch finishes your salary by the 10th, this ₦1,500 plate will fix that."
A7. Because pillars answer the question "what do I post today?" ahead of time. They give you ready themes to rotate, keep your
content balanced, and keep you consistent with your niche. (Accept any answer mentioning planning ahead and consistency.)

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that content creation is not about having an expensive camera — it is about clear
thinking. You chose a niche (what you are mainly about), built an audience avatar (one real person you are speaking to), picked
content pillars (the themes you rotate), and learned that a hook — the first 3 seconds — decides whether anyone watches the
rest. You also saw the difference between value-driven posting and random posting, and you practised all of it on real, familiar
Nigerian businesses.
Five important takeaways:
1. Content creation is planning + making + sharing value for a specific audience — not just pressing "post".
2. A focused niche beats posting about everything.
3. Your audience avatar should be one realistic person with a real need and a real obstacle.
4. Three content pillars (value, showcase, trust) keep you balanced and consistent.
5. The hook — the first 3 seconds — decides whether the rest of your content is ever seen.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I write my niche in one clear sentence?
Can I describe one real person my content is for, including what stops them?
Can I name three pillars and give an example post for each?
Can I write a hook that makes me want to keep watching?
Can I tell whether a planned post is value-driven or random?
Connection to the next session. You now know what you will make and for whom. Next session we pick up the phone and
learn to shoot that content: how to hold the phone steady, how to use light, how to frame with the rule of thirds, which shot
types to use, and when to shoot vertical or horizontal. The hooks and storyboard seed you wrote today become the plan for
what you will actually film next.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner writes a niche specific enough to plan around and can defend it.
Learner's avatar names a real person with a genuine obstacle, not "everybody".
Learner's pillars are balanced — at least one teaches or builds trust rather than selling.
Learner's hooks are spoken lines that create curiosity and are still clear.
Learner can look at a weak hook and explain why it is weak, then improve it.
Learner connects hooks to the avatar's worry ("she fears being overcharged, so I mention price").
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Content creation = buying a good camera." → Correct: it starts with planning; great cameras on unplanned content still
fail.
"My niche is too small." → Correct: narrow makes posting easier and audiences trust focus.
"My audience is everyone." → Correct: everyone means no one; name one person.
"The hook is only the spoken words." → Correct: the first visual matters equally.
"Every post should sell." → Correct: teaching and trust earn the right to sell.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Niche narrowing drill: give five broad topics (fashion, food, music, faith, fitness) and ask the learner to narrow each to a
place + audience + problem.
2. Avatar builder: hand out a blank avatar card and a photo or description of a real customer; complete it together.
3. Hook rewrite: give five weak openers ("Hi guys welcome back") and ask the learner to rewrite each as a strong spoken
hook using C-P-C.
4. Sort the pillars: give eight example posts and ask the learner to sort them into value, showcase, trust.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a half-completed Worksheet 1.1 with the niche already chosen and the avatar table partly
filled; the learner completes the obstacle and writes two hooks with help.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise and assignment as written.
Level 3 (challenge): write a fourth pillar (e.g., a community/humour pillar), write five hooks, and add a short paragraph
explaining how each hook targets a specific worry of the avatar. Optionally, plan two different avatars for the same business
and compare which content suits each.
Timing guidance. The lecture and demonstration are the backbone of this session; protect at least 18 minutes for the guided
practical (Section H) because the learner's personalised plan carries into every later session. If time runs short, shorten the case
study (Section J) to a two-minute mention and reduce the independent exercise to two hooks instead of three — but never skip
the guided practical, since it produces the learner's own working plan.
