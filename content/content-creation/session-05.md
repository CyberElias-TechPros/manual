# Editing II + Thumbnails — B-roll, Music, Transitions, Text and Canva Graphics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 3: Production (Editing and Graphics)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (building on Session 4)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 4 first cut (a 30–60 second vertical video with corrected captions) and a working CapCut project. A Canva account (free) must be created before or at the start of this session.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 you made a tight first cut — mostly a talking clip with captions. Today you make it hold attention: lay B-roll over your voice, add music (legally), use a transition or two, add text overlays, improve the colour, then design a thumbnail in Canva so people actually click.

**Preparation before class:** Canva accounts created (or create at start); CapCut B-roll permission; the Session 4 project ready to open; a small B-roll shot list written; royalty-free music note; example thumbnails printed for a "clickable or not?" vote; academy Wi-Fi on.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what B-roll is and why it keeps viewers watching.
2. Add B-roll as an overlay clip above their talking-head video in CapCut and synchronise it to the spoken words.
3. Add music from the CapCut library (or a royalty-free source), reduce the music volume so the voice is clear, and explain
why copyrighted songs must be avoided.
4. Add a transition and justify why transitions should be used sparingly.
5. Add a text overlay / title (business name, offer or key point) and make it readable.
6. Apply basic colour/tone adjustments (brightness, contrast, saturation) to make footage look consistent and professional.
7. Export the finished video with the correct vertical settings.
8. Explain what a thumbnail is and why it drives clicks.
9. Design a thumbnail at 1280 × 720 in Canva using a template, brand colours, readable text and a face/emotion, and
export it as an image.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, prepare the same 40-second talking clip in two versions:
Version A: talking head only (the Session 4 style).
Version B: the same talking head with B-roll laid over the voice — hands folding fabric, a close-up of the product, the shop
front — plus quiet background music.
Play A, then B. Ask for a show of hands: "Which one would you watch to the end?"
What the instructor says:
"Same person, same words, same length. The only thing I changed is what you see while the person is talking. In version
one, your eyes get tired of the same face. In version two, your eyes are busy — hands, product, shop — so your brain
stays awake and you keep watching.
The extra shots I laid over the talking are called B-roll. Today you will add B-roll and music to your own video. And then
— because a great video nobody clicks is a wasted video — you will design the thumbnail that makes people click."
Expected learner response: Learners will pick version B and may say "it is more interesting", "I can see the product", "it is not
boring". Accept all answers. Write "B-roll = the shots over the voice" on the board.
Then reveal the hook: Show the six example thumbnails on the projector and ask, "If you saw all six on YouTube, which would
you click? Which would you never click?" Count the votes. Say:
"Look at what made you click — a face, big words, bold colour. That is not luck. That is design. By the end of today, you
will each make one of these, for free, in Canva."
Why this opener works: It makes the value of B-roll visible (attention), then immediately connects editing to the next
deliverable (the thumbnail), and it starts the session with a real decision the learners make themselves.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "Name the five core editing actions from last session."
Expected answer: trim, split, delete, reorder, undo.
Common gap: learners forget "undo". Remind them it is the safety net — cut boldly, undo freely.
Question 2: "What export settings did we use for vertical videos?"
Expected answer: 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4.
Common gap: learners say "1080p" without the height. Clarify 1080 × 1920 is the vertical (9:16) form of 1080p.
Question 3: "Why must we correct auto-captions before publishing?"
Expected answer: because they mis-hear names, places and local words (Adaeze, Ankara, Port Harcourt, naira).
Common gap: some think captions only matter for the deaf. Clarify: most people watch with the sound off, so captions are
for everyone.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your first cut is solid — but it is still one face talking. Think of it like egusi soup with only the soup: it is good, but it is
missing the meat and the fish that make it a real meal. B-roll and music are the meat and fish. And the thumbnail is the
signboard outside the restaurant that makes people come in. Today we finish the meal and hang the signboard."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five core actions, spend 5 extra minutes re-demonstrating them quickly on the
projector before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 B-roll — what it is and why it works
The idea. B-roll is extra footage laid over the top of your main (talking) footage, so the viewer sees something relevant while
they hear your voice.
Plain-English definition. The main footage — a person talking to camera — is called the A-roll. B-roll is everything else:
hands, product, shop front, walking, scenery, an action. You show B-roll over the voice to illustrate what is being said.
Why it exists. The human eye gets bored quickly with the same picture. A face talking for 60 seconds, with nothing changing,
makes viewers scroll away even if the words are useful. B-roll solves this: it gives the eye something to look at and the ear
something to listen to at the same time.
What it does:
Holds attention — the picture keeps changing.
Covers cuts — when you cut out a pause or a stumble in the talking, B-roll hides the jump so it looks smooth.
Shows, not just tells — instead of saying "our fabric is strong", you show hands stretching the fabric.
Adds feeling — a slow close-up of a smiling customer feels warm; a quick shot of a busy shop feels energetic.
Real-life comparison. Think of a commentator on radio versus television. On radio, you only hear the voice. On television,
the voice continues but you also see the players, the replay, the crowd. Television holds your attention because your eyes have
something to do. B-roll turns your A-roll from radio into television.
How much B-roll? For a talking video, aim to cover a cut with a B-roll shot every 5–7 seconds or so. You do not need B-roll
over every single second — sometimes the face and the emotion are the point. Balance.
Limitation / misconception. B-roll must match what is being said. If you are talking about the dress and you show a shot of a
shoe, the viewer gets confused. Also, B-roll does not replace a clear message — it decorates it. A boring script with beautiful B-
roll is still a boring video.
F.2 How to add B-roll in CapCut (overlay)
The idea. In CapCut, B-roll goes on an overlay track — a second video track that sits above your main video track. The overlay
plays on top while the main footage continues underneath.
Steps (phone; wording may differ by version):
1. Open your Session 4 project.
2. Move the playhead to the moment where you want the B-roll to appear.
3. Tap Overlay (or the ï¼‹ Add overlay / Add track button).
4. Select your B-roll clip and add it.
5. The B-roll now sits on a track above the main video. Drag its edges to set exactly when it starts and ends — ideally it covers
the words it illustrates.
6. Trim its start/end like any clip.
7. Repeat for your other B-roll shots.
Synchronising to the words. Place the B-roll so it appears just as the speaker mentions the thing. If Adaeze says "look at the
fabric", the fabric close-up should appear on "fabric", not five seconds later.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion "look at the fabric" moment:
Time A-roll (audio) B-roll shown
0:00–0:04 "Stop! Before you buy that dress…" Adaeze holding dress (A-roll)
0:04–0:10 "Reason one: the fabric does not fade." Close-up: fabric being stretched
0:10–0:16 "Reason two: it fits all sizes." Dress on mannequin
0:16–0:22 "Reason three: perfect for church." Adaeze smiling (A-roll)
0:22–0:30 "DM 'FASHION' to order." Shop front, then product
Important note on overlay audio. When you add B-roll, mute its own sound (set its Volume to 0) so we do not hear two audio
tracks fighting. The voice should come from the A-roll. You usually mute the overlay clip (volume 0) unless its natural sound is
important.
Common misconception. Learners think they need expensive extra footage. B-roll can be shot in one minute with the phone:
hands with the product, the shop front, a close-up, a walking shot. Session 2 taught exactly these shots.
F.3 Music and sound effects
The idea. Music sets the mood and covers the silence; sound effects (SFX) are short sounds (a "whoosh", a "pop", a click) that
add energy to a cut.
Why music matters:
It makes the video feel professional and alive.
It sets the emotion: calm for a testimonial, upbeat for a sale.
It fills quiet gaps.
The copyright rule (teach this clearly):
Never use a popular song you do not own. Recording a song playing on another phone and laying it over your video is still
a copyright problem.
Use the CapCut built-in music library — the tracks there are provided for use in CapCut. (Availability and licence wording
vary by track and version; check the track's label.)
Use royalty-free music (sources listed in the Lab Pack: CapCut library, YouTube Audio Library, Pixabay). "Royalty-free"
means you can use it without paying per use.
For a business video especially, staying legal protects the business from takedowns and complaints.
How to add music (phone; wording may differ):
1. Tap Audio on the toolbar.
2. Choose Sounds / Music (the library) or Extract / Import for your own file.
3. Search and tap a track → Add (or the plus/check). It drops onto an audio track.
4. Trim the music to the length of your video.
Volume balance — the most important setting.
The voice must always be the loudest thing. Music is background.
Lower the music volume so it sits under the voice. In CapCut, select the music clip → Volume and drag it down (a typical
comfortable setting is well below the voice; the exact number differs by track — use your ears).
Some versions offer ducking (auto-lowering music under speech, or "reduce background sound"). If available, it does this
for you. Wording differs by version.
Sound effects. Add a short SFX (whoosh, pop) at a cut or a text pop-in for energy. Use them sparingly — one or two, not on
every clip.
Worked example. Music set to a calm, low level under a testimonial voice; a single soft "whoosh" as the thumbnail-style text
appears at the start. Voice clear, mood set, energy at the right moment.
Common misconception. "Loud music = professional." No. If the viewer cannot hear the words, they leave. The words are the
message; the music is the carpet under the words.
F.4 Transitions — use sparingly
The idea. A transition is a short effect between two clips — a fade, a slide, a zoom — that moves the viewer from one shot to
the next.
Why they exist. A transition can smooth a jump or add a little style.
The golden rule for beginners: Use few, and only where they help. The default and best cut is the plain hard cut (clip ends,
next begins). Professional editors use simple fades or cuts far more than flashy effects.
How to add (phone; wording may differ):
1. Find the join between two clips on the timeline.
2. Tap the small transition icon/box at the join.
3. Choose a transition (e.g., Fade / Dissolve).
4. Adjust its duration (keep it short).
Worked example. Use a fade from black at the very start and a fade to black at the very end — clean and professional. Leave
the joins in the middle as plain cuts.
Limitation / misconception. Loading every join with spinning, zooming, star-wipe effects makes the video look like a
beginner's slideshow and distracts from the message. When in doubt, use a cut.
F.5 Text overlays and titles
The idea. A text overlay is words placed on top of the video — a title, the business name, a key point, a price, or an offer. A
title is a text overlay used at the start to name the video or brand.
Why they matter:
They make key information unmissable (business name, price, the "3 reasons").
Like captions, they help viewers who watch with the sound off.
They add branding and structure.
How to add (phone; wording may differ):
1. Move the playhead where the text should appear.
2. Tap Text → Add text (or a template).
3. Type the words.
4. Choose a font, colour, size and position.
5. Drag its edges on the timeline to set how long it stays.
Making text readable:
Big and bold — readable at a glance on a small screen.
High contrast — white text with a dark outline/stroke, or light text on a dark box.
Short — a few words, not a paragraph.
On-brand — use the business's colours and font where possible (we will set these in Canva).
Keep it clear of the platform's buttons (top and bottom edges).
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion:
0:00–0:03: big title "3 reasons to love this dress" (white with dark stroke).
0:20–0:30: "DM 'FASHION' to order" (on-brand pink background box).
Common misconception. Learners pile text everywhere and the video becomes a poster that happens to move. Text should
punctuate the video, not cover it.
F.6 Colour and tone basics
The idea. Colour/tone adjustment changes how the picture looks — how bright, how contrasty, how warm (orange) or cool
(blue) it is. Filters are preset looks; Adjust lets you change values yourself.
Why it matters. Footage shot on different days, in different light, can look inconsistent — one clip bright-yellow, the next
dark-blue. A small consistent adjustment makes the whole video feel like one professional piece.
The basic controls (phone; wording may differ):
Control What it does Beginner use
Brightness Overall light/dark Lift a slightly dark clip
Contrast Difference between light and dark Add a little "punch"
Saturation Colour intensity Slightly up for a lively feel; down for a calm one
Temperature Warm (orange) ↔ cool (blue) Warm for cosy/food; cool for clean/minimal
Filter A preset look applied to the whole clip Pick one and keep it consistent across clips
Worked example. Two clips: one warm (shot in the evening), one cool (shot at noon). Apply the same filter to both so they
match. Now the video feels like one piece.
Limitation. Over-editing colour makes skin look unnatural and food look fake. Small, consistent adjustments beat extreme ones.
Consistency across clips matters more than any single adjustment.
F.7 Exporting the final video
Same as Session 4: 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4. Before exporting the final:
Play the whole video once with sound on — voice clear above music? Captions correct?
Play it once with sound off — does the story still make sense with captions and B-roll?
Check the first 3 seconds (the hook) and the last 3 seconds (the CTA).
Check no unwanted end card/watermark (see Session 4).
Then export, and confirm the file plays from the gallery.
F.8 Thumbnails — the signboard that drives clicks
The idea. A thumbnail is the single still image that represents your video before someone plays it — on YouTube it is the
picture next to the title; on Reels/TikTok the "cover" serves the same job. It is your video's signboard.
Why it exists / why it matters. People scroll fast. Before they read your title or watch a second, they see the picture. A strong
thumbnail makes them stop and click; a weak one makes them scroll past a great video. On YouTube, the thumbnail plus title
are the biggest driver of whether anyone clicks. This is why serious creators spend real time on it.
What makes a thumbnail work:
1. A face and emotion — people click on faces, especially expressive ones (surprise, joy, curiosity). A clear face with feeling
beats a plain product shot.
2. Very few words — 3–5 big words. The thumbnail text is a hook, not a sentence.
3. High contrast — the subject stands out from the background; text is readable even at small size.
4. Brand recognition — consistent colours and fonts so followers recognise you instantly.
5. Honesty — the thumbnail must match the video. Clickbait that does not deliver makes viewers leave and stop trusting
you.
Real-life comparison. A thumbnail is the signboard outside a shop. A bright, clear, inviting sign makes people enter. A faded,
cluttered, unreadable sign makes them walk past — even if the shop inside is excellent.
Standard sizes:
YouTube thumbnail: 1280 × 720 pixels (16:9, landscape). This is what we design today.
Reels/TikTok cover: vertical 1080 × 1920 (9:16). Same principles, tall shape. (If you only make one today, make the 1280
× 720; you can adapt it vertical later.)
Common misconception. "Any picture from the video will do." No — a random paused frame is usually mid-blink or blurry. A
designed thumbnail with a chosen expression, clean background and 3 words performs far better.
F.9 Canva — designing the thumbnail
The idea. Canva is a free online design tool with ready-made templates. You do not need to be an artist; you start from a
template and change it.
Why Canva: it is free (there is a paid Pro tier, but the free tier is enough for everything today), works in a browser or app, and has
thousands of templates — including YouTube thumbnail templates already sized at 1280 × 720.
Key terms:
Template — a ready-made design you edit.
Elements — shapes, icons, photos, graphics you can add.
Text — headings/subheadings you type.
Brand colours / fonts — your business's consistent colours and fonts. (Canva's saved "Brand Kit" is a paid Pro feature; on
the free plan you can still keep your colours and fonts consistent by writing down their names/hex codes and reusing them,
or by saving an example design and copying from it.)
How to design a YouTube thumbnail in Canva (steps; wording may differ by version):
1. Sign in at canva.com (or the app).
2. Search templates for "YouTube Thumbnail" — these are pre-sized to 1280 × 720. Choose one you like.
3. Or create a custom size: Create a design → Custom size → 1280 × 720 px.
4. Replace the photo: upload or search for an image of your subject with a clear, expressive face (or your product). Drag it
into the frame.
5. Edit the text: 3–5 big words (the hook). Use a bold, readable font.
6. Add brand colour: set the background or a shape to your brand colour so it matches your video and posts.
7. High contrast: put light text on a dark area (or vice versa); add a colour block or outline behind the text.
8. Keep it clean: remove clutter; make sure the face and the words are the two biggest things.
9. Preview small: zoom out so the design is small, like a real thumbnail in a feed. If you cannot read it, make the text bigger.
10. Download: tap Share → Download, choose PNG (best quality) or JPG. Save it to your gallery/desktop.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion thumbnail (1280 × 720):
Photo: Adaeze holding the Ankara dress, smiling, looking at camera.
Big text (top-left): "3 REASONS"
Smaller line under it: "Why this dress sells out"
Brand colour: a pink block behind the text; Adaeze's face on the right, to the light.
Result: readable at small size, on-brand, honest (the video really does give 3 reasons).
Limitation. A beautiful thumbnail cannot save a bad video. The thumbnail gets the click; the content keeps the viewer. Do
both.
F.10 Summary of the session
B-roll = extra shots laid over your voice; it holds attention and covers cuts. Place it every ~5–7 seconds and match it to
the words.
Music sets mood; lower it so the voice stays loudest; use royalty-free/CapCut library music, never copyrighted songs.
Transitions — use few; usually a plain cut, with fades at the start/end.
Text overlays — big, short, high-contrast, on-brand.
Colour — small, consistent adjustments across clips.
Export = 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4.
Thumbnails drive clicks: face + emotion, 3–5 big words, high contrast, on-brand, honest.
Design the thumbnail in Canva at 1280 × 720, download as PNG.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
The Adaeze Fashion Session 4 project open in CapCut on the projector.
The B-roll pack loaded.
Canva open in a browser, logged in, on the search page for "YouTube Thumbnail".
An expressive photo of a person ready to drop into the thumbnail.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — B-roll overlay (5 minutes)
Say: "Watch where the B-roll goes — above the main video, on its own track."
Do this, narrating:
1. Move the playhead to "the fabric does not fade".
2. Tap Overlay → select the fabric close-up → Add.
3. Point at the new track above the main video.
4. Drag the B-roll's edges so it starts exactly on "fabric" and ends before the next sentence.
5. Select the B-roll → set its Volume to 0 (mute the overlay so the voice stays clean).
6. Play the moment so the class sees the picture change while the voice continues.
7. Add a second B-roll over the next sentence to show the "every 5–7 seconds" rhythm.
Expected result: The talking continues but the picture switches to relevant shots. Say: "This is why people watch to the end."
Ask: "What would happen if I put the shoe shot here while she talks about the fabric?" → Confusion; B-roll must match the
words.
G.3 Demonstration 2 — music, volume and a transition (4 minutes)
1. Tap Audio → Sounds, search a calm track, tap Add.
2. Select the music track → Volume → drag it down until it clearly sits under the voice.
3. Play and ask the class: "Can you still hear every word? Is the music just a carpet?" (Adjust live until it is right.)
4. At the very start of the video, tap the first clip's start → transition → Fade, short duration. Repeat a fade at the very end.
5. Say: "One fade at the start, one at the end. Everything in the middle is a plain cut. That is professional."

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mama Chika's Kitchen, a small restaurant in Port Harcourt, posts a video every day: a plate of food on a table with
soft background music. The food is genuinely excellent, but the videos get very few views and almost no orders. Mama Chika is
frustrated and is thinking of stopping the videos.
Relevant facts.
The videos are just the plate, filmed from one angle, with no person and no movement.
The captions say only "Delicious food ðŸ ²" (plus an emoji).
There is no B-roll of cooking, no steam, no hands serving, no customer tasting.
The thumbnail/cover is a random frame where the plate is half out of shot.
When a rival restaurant started posting short clips of food sizzling in the pan, steam rising, a customer's first bite, their
views rose sharply.
Decision point. What should Mama Chika change — the content style, the thumbnail, or both?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep posting the same style and hope.
2. Change both: add B-roll (sizzling pan, steam, hands serving, customer tasting) and design a clear, appetising thumbnail
with big words like "HOT & FRESH" and a price, in brand colours.
3. Only change the thumbnail and leave the video as it is.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. The rival's rise came from two things working together: moving B-roll
that makes the food feel alive, and a clickable thumbnail. Approach 3 helps clicks but the video still fails to hold attention
once played (the viewer sees a static plate). Approach 1 changes nothing.
Reasoning / workflow:
Re-shoot 4–5 quick B-roll shots: pan sizzling, steam, rice being served, a customer smiling at the first bite.
Lay B-roll over a short voice line ("Fresh jollof, ready in minutes — only ₦1,500").
Keep music low; use the restaurant's colour (warm orange) consistently.
Design a thumbnail: a big steaming plate (hero) + a happy customer face, 4 words "FRESH JOLLOF DAILY", warm brand
colour, high contrast.
Trade-offs.
Approach 2 costs one afternoon of extra shooting and editing, but it directly addresses both problems (attention and clicks)
and can be repeated for every dish.
Approach 3 is faster but only fixes half the problem — a great thumbnail leading to a boring video loses trust.
Approach 1 is free but keeps her exactly where she is.
Professional connection. Restaurants, salons and shops all hire content creators to do exactly this: capture appetising B-roll

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the relevant row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Music is louder than the voice
Symptoms: You cannot hear the words over the music. Likely cause: Music volume too high, or the voice clip is quiet. How to
investigate: Play the video; try to hear every word. Corrective steps: Select the music track → lower its Volume; if needed,
raise the voice clip's volume slightly. If available, use ducking (auto lower music under speech). How to confirm resolved:
Every word is clearly audible; music is a background "carpet". Prevention: Set music low first, then play-test before exporting.
K.2 Used a copyrighted song
Symptoms: A popular song plays; the platform may mute the video, block it, or strike the account. Likely cause: Music taken
from the phone or another video instead of the library. How to investigate: Check where the track came from. Corrective
steps: Delete it and add a CapCut library / royalty-free track instead. How to confirm resolved: The track is from a
licensed/royalty-free source. Prevention: Only ever use the CapCut library or listed royalty-free sources.
K.3 B-roll does not match the words
Symptoms: A shoe appears while the speaker talks about a dress; viewers look confused. Likely cause: Overlay clip placed
without checking the sentence. How to investigate: Play the join and read the caption at that moment. Corrective steps:
Move the B-roll to the sentence it illustrates, or swap it for a matching shot. How to confirm resolved: The picture matches the
words. Prevention: Plan the B-roll shot list per sentence before editing.
K.4 The video feels like a slideshow (too many effects/transitions)
Symptoms: Spinning/zooming between every clip; the message gets lost. Likely cause: A transition was added to every join.
How to investigate: Watch the video; count the transition effects. Corrective steps: Remove most transitions; keep plain cuts,
with a fade at start/end only. How to confirm resolved: The video flows smoothly without distraction. Prevention: Rule of
thumb — "when in doubt, use a cut".
K.5 Text overlay is unreadable or covers the subject
Symptoms: Text is tiny, low-contrast, or sits over a face. Likely cause: Default size/placement, or too many words. How to
investigate: Zoom the preview out to a phone size. Corrective steps: Use fewer words, bigger font, add a stroke/colour block,
and move it to a clear area. How to confirm resolved: Visible and readable at a glance. Prevention: Check overlays on a small
preview before export.
K.6 Colour looks inconsistent between clips
Symptoms: One clip yellow, the next blue; the video feels "patched". Likely cause: Clips shot in different light with no matching
adjustment. How to investigate: Compare adjacent clips. Corrective steps: Apply the same filter or similar Adjust values to
each clip (brightness/contrast/temperature). How to confirm resolved: Clips look like they belong together. Prevention: Shoot
in similar light and apply one consistent look.
K.7 Canva thumbnail text is unreadable at small size
Symptoms: It looks fine big, but you cannot read it when zoomed out to feed size. Likely cause: Too many words, thin font, low
contrast. How to investigate: Zoom the design down to thumbnail size on screen. Corrective steps: Cut to 3–5 words, use a
bold font, add a colour block or outline, increase size. How to confirm resolved: Readable when small. Prevention: Design big,
then always check small before downloading.
K.8 Canva download / account problems
Symptoms: Download button greyed out or file will not save. Likely cause: Certain template elements are Pro-only, or the
internet dropped. How to investigate: Hover the element — Pro items are marked with a crown/Pro tag. Corrective steps:
Replace Pro elements with free ones; reconnect to internet; try PNG; check storage. How to confirm resolved: The design
downloads and opens. Prevention: On the free plan, prefer free templates and free elements; check before you spend time
designing.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Check before fixing — look at the volume number, read the caption at the join, hover the
Canva element. Confirm the cause, then correct.
