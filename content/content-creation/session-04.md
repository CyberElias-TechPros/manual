# Editing Basics in CapCut — Cuts, Trim, Split, Reorder and Captions

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 3: Production (Editing)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first time inside a video editor)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The learner must have (a) phone footage shot in Session 2, (b) a 30-second script written in Session 3, and (c) a working phone or academy machine with CapCut installed. If a learner has no footage, give them the academy "sample clip pack" (Section C) so they can still follow the full session.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 3 you wrote a 30-second script with a Hook (3s) + Value (20s) + CTA (7s). That script is your shopping list. Today we open CapCut and turn the raw clips you shot into a real, tight, captioned video that follows that script.

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and signed in on every learner device; sample clip pack copied to machines without learner footage; charger cables; academy Wi-Fi on; projector showing the instructor's phone (screen mirror) or instructor machine running CapCut; the Session 3 script of the running example (Adaeze Fashion) printed for the demo.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words what video editing is and why raw footage must be edited before publishing.
2. Create a new CapCut project set to a vertical 9:16 aspect ratio and import footage from the phone or machine.
3. Identify and describe the four zones of the CapCut screen — the preview, the timeline, the playhead, and the toolbar —
and describe what each does.
4. Perform the five core edit actions — trim, split, delete, reorder and undo — on a real clip without help.
5. Match their clips to their Session 3 script so that the video follows the planned Hook → Value → CTA order.
6. Apply basic pacing by removing dead air, stumbles and long pauses so the video flows.
7. Generate auto-captions in CapCut and correct at least three caption errors (spelling, names, Nigerian words).
8. Export a finished video correctly for vertical platforms — 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4 — and confirm the file plays.
9. Produce a complete 30–60 second first-cut video with captions that can be published to Instagram Reels, TikTok or
YouTube Shorts.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One smartphone per learner (the phone they shot with in Session 2), fully charged, with at least 8 GB free storage.
One computer per learner or per pair (academy machines). CapCut has a desktop app, but the phone version is what we use
by default because most learners publish from a phone.
Instructor device connected to the projector, ideally with screen mirroring so the class sees every tap on the phone in real
time.
A short USB cable or a WhatsApp-to-self / cloud transfer method to move footage between phone and machine if needed.
Software
CapCut (free). Install from Google Play (Android) or the App Store (iOS), or the CapCut desktop app for Windows/Mac.
Create or sign in to a free account so projects are saved.
A phone gallery / Files app to locate footage.
Optional: Google Drive or a memory card reader to move big files.
Sample files
The instructor prepares a "sample clip pack": 6–8 short clips (wide, mid, close, plus 2 B-roll inserts) of a fictional business,
so learners with no footage can still complete the whole session. Store it on each academy machine and on a shareable link.
Worksheets
Worksheet 4.1 — "CapCut Screen Map" (printed, 1 page; learners label the four zones and the icons).
Worksheet 4.2 — "Caption Correction Sheet" (learners write corrected caption lines).
Accounts
Free CapCut account. No paid subscription is needed for any task in this course.
Internet
Internet is needed only to sign in, download CapCut and (optionally) use the online template store. The core editing actions
in this session work offline once the app is installed and the footage is on the phone.
Alternative for learners who lack a facility
Any Android or iOS phone with CapCut works. If a learner has no phone, pair them with a classmate who does, or let them
use the CapCut desktop app on an academy machine. The five core actions (trim, split, delete, reorder, undo) work the
same way on desktop; the buttons are simply clicked with a mouse instead of tapped.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, have two versions of the same footage ready.
Version A: a raw, unedited 2-minute clip of a person talking to camera — with the "umms", a long silence at the start, a
stumble, and a repeated sentence.
Version B: the 40-second edited version of that same clip, captions on, tight.
Do not explain the difference yet. Play both, back to back.
What the instructor says:
"This is the same person, the same camera, the same lighting. The only difference is what I did after the recording. Watch
version one … now watch version two. Which one would you keep watching on TikTok? Which one looks like a business
and which one looks like a mistake?
The person did not become a better speaker between the two videos. I simply removed the boring parts, cut the mistakes,
and added captions. That job has a name: editing. It is the single skill that separates a video people scroll past from a
video people watch to the end. Today, you learn to do it."
Expected learner response: Learners will almost always pick version two and say things like "the second one is shorter", "the
first one is too long", "I can read the second one". Accept all answers and write their words on the board. Then reveal that the
raw clip was not deleted — it was cut.
Then reveal the hook: Tap play on version B and tap the screen to freeze on a caption. Say:
"Everything you see here — the cutting, the captions, the tightness — was done in a free app on a phone, in about
fifteen minutes. By the end of today, you will each do the same to your own footage from Session 2."
Why this opener works: It creates an immediate, visible gap between "raw" and "edited", it sets the standard (short, tight,
captioned), and it removes the fear that editing needs expensive software or a laptop.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this quickly as an oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "In your Session 3 script, what are the three parts, and how many seconds each?"
Expected answer: Hook (first 3 seconds), Value (about 20 seconds), CTA (last 7 seconds) — about 75 words in total for 30
seconds.
Common gap: learners remember "hook" but forget the CTA. Remind them the CTA is where you tell the viewer what to do
(order now, DM us, visit the shop).
Question 2: "Why do we shoot vertical for Reels, TikTok and Shorts, and not horizontal?"
Expected answer: Because people watch those platforms on a phone held upright; a vertical video fills the screen. Horizontal
is for YouTube long videos and for some church/event footage.
Common gap: some learners think horizontal is "more professional". Clarify that for short vertical platforms, 9:16 fills the
screen and gets more watch time.
Question 3: "In Session 3 we said record audio close to the mouth — how close?"
Expected answer: About 15–30 cm from the mouth, face the mic, quiet room, no fan/noise.
Common gap: learners confuse "close" with "shouting". Restate: close = near the mic, not loud.
Bridge to today (say this):
"So you have footage. You have a script. Right now your footage is like a bag of unwashed rice with stones in it — good
food, but not ready to eat. Editing is the washing and picking. We remove the stones (the stumbles, the silence, the
mistakes) and keep only the clean rice (the good parts). CapCut is the bowl and the sieve. Let us begin."
If more than a third of the class cannot state the three script parts, spend 5 extra minutes re-reading one learner's script aloud
and marking it Hook / Value / CTA together before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What video editing actually is
The idea. Video editing is the job of taking the raw recording the camera captured and arranging, removing and adding pieces
until the video says exactly what you want, in the shortest, clearest way.
Plain-English definition. Editing is choosing which seconds the viewer sees and in what order. You keep the good, delete the
bad, and put the pieces in the right order.
Why it exists. A camera records everything — the wait before you speak, the "sorry, I will start again", the passing okada, the
two minutes when your hand was in front of the lens. Nobody wants to watch that. Editing exists because raw footage is
almost never watchable, and edited footage can be.
What it does. Editing:
Removes dead air, mistakes and repeats.
Reorders clips so the story makes sense.
Shortens a long recording into a tight one.
Adds captions, music, text and effects.
Sets the pace — how fast the video moves.
Real-life comparison. Think of cooking. The market (your shoot day) gives you raw ingredients. Before anyone eats, you wash,
cut, remove spoilt parts, and arrange the food on the plate. Your mother's stew is not just thrown in a pot — it is prepared.
Editing is preparing the video for the viewer's plate.
Important limitation / misconception. Editing cannot fix a video that has no clear idea, no light, and no sound. If the footage is
dark, shaky and the audio is noise, no amount of editing saves it. Good shooting makes good editing easy. This is why we
spent Week 1 learning to shoot and write first.
How a learner recognises it. Any video you watch on a phone that feels "smooth", "fast" or "easy to follow" has been edited.
When a person on screen finishes a sentence and the next sentence starts immediately, with no pause — that pause was cut
out.
F.2 The CapCut screen — four zones
The idea. CapCut is a free video editor made by ByteDance (the company behind TikTok). It runs on phones and on
computers. Before you can edit, you must know the four parts of the screen. The wording of buttons may differ slightly by
version, but the four zones are always present.
Zone What it is What it does
1. Preview window
The big rectangle at the top Shows the video at the current position. Tap the play button to watch.
Zone What it is What it does
2. Timeline
The horizontal strip at the
bottom
Shows your clips in order, left to right. This is your "workbench".
3. Playhead
The thin vertical white line
on the timeline
Marks the exact moment you are at. When you play, the playhead
moves. When you cut, you cut at the playhead.
4. Toolbar
The row of icons below the
timeline
Contains the tools: Split (scissors), Speed, Volume, Text, Audio,
Transitions, Delete, and more.
Real-life comparison. The timeline is like a film strip lying flat on a table. Each picture frame is a moment. The playhead is
your finger pointing at one frame and saying "cut here". The preview is the small TV showing what the frame looks like.
Key vocabulary you will hear today:
Clip — one piece of video on the timeline.
Track — a horizontal line on the timeline that holds clips. The main video track is at the bottom; extra tracks (for overlays,
music, text) sit above it.
Trim — cut a little off the start or the end of a clip.
Split — cut a clip into two pieces at the playhead.
Playhead — the position marker.
Dead air — silence or empty space where nothing useful happens.
Common misconception. Learners think CapCut "deletes" footage permanently. It does not. When you cut, you are only
removing that piece from this project; the original file stays safe in your phone gallery. This is why you must never edit the
only copy of anything — but with CapCut you are always working on a copy, so you are safe.
F.3 Getting started — creating a project and importing media
Step one: New project. Open CapCut. On the home screen tap New project (in some versions it says "Create new"). A blank
project opens.
Step two: Choose the aspect ratio. This is critical. The aspect ratio is the shape of the final video. For Instagram Reels, TikTok
and YouTube Shorts we use 9:16 (vertical). In CapCut, you usually pick the ratio when importing, or you can change it later in
the Ratio / Aspect ratio menu (wording may differ by version). Choose 9:16.
Why 9:16 matters: 9:16 means 9 units wide and 16 units tall — a tall rectangle, exactly the shape of a phone screen held
upright. If you edit in the wrong ratio, your video will have black bars on the sides and look amateurish.
Step three: Import. Tap Add media / Import and select the clips you shot. On a phone you will usually see your gallery; pick
the clips. Do not pick all 20 clips. For a 30-second video, select only the clips that match your script — usually 6–12 short
clips.
Step four: Add to timeline. After selecting, tap Add. The clips drop onto the timeline in the order you selected them.
Worked example — importing for the Adaeze Fashion video:
Script: a 30-second "3 reasons to love our new Ankara dress" video.
Clips shot in Session 2: (1) Adaeze walking into the shop — wide; (2) close-up of the fabric; (3) Adaeze holding the dress;
(4) the dress on the mannequin; (5) Adaeze smiling to camera; (6) a B-roll shot of hands folding the dress.
Import all six, then we will trim each one down on the timeline.
Common misconception. Learners import everything and end up with a 5-minute mess. The rule is: import fewer clips than
you think. It is easier to add than to cut.
F.4 The timeline, the playhead and how video flows
The idea. The timeline reads left to right, like a sentence in a book. The first clip is on the far left; the last clip is on the far
right. The video plays from left to right.
The playhead. The white line shows where you are. Tap anywhere on the timeline to move the playhead there. Scrub (drag) the
timeline left and right to move through the video quickly.
Zoom. You can pinch or use the zoom control to make the timeline bigger so you can see frames more clearly, or smaller to see
the whole video at once. Wording may differ by version.
How a cut is made. A cut is simply the boundary between two clips. When clip 1 ends and clip 2 begins, that boundary is a cut.
Editing is mostly the art of deciding where those boundaries fall.
Real-life comparison. The timeline is like a trail of garri in a row of bowls. Each bowl is a clip. You can take garri out of a bowl,
move bowls around, or pour two bowls into one. The timeline is your row of bowls.
Important concept — the "talking head" trap. If your video is just you talking for 60 seconds with no cuts, the viewer gets
bored. Even in a talking video, we cut out the pauses so the energy stays high. Watch any popular Nigerian creator: they cut
every few seconds.
F.5 The five core actions — the heart of this session
These five actions do 90% of all editing. Learn them by heart.
Action 1 — TRIM (cut the start or end of a clip)
What it is. Removing a little from the beginning or the end of a single clip.
Why you do it. Every clip begins with you reaching for the phone or saying "are we recording?" and ends with you reaching to
stop. Trim removes that.
How (phone): Tap the clip to select it (a white border with handles appears). Drag the left handle to the right to cut off the
beginning, or the right handle to the left to cut off the end.
Diagram of the idea:
```text
Before trim:  [ "are we... umm... so today we show you..." ]
After trim:             [ "so today we show you..." ]
                         ^ you dragged the left handle to here
```
Worked example. A clip starts with Adaeze laughing for 3 seconds before she speaks. Trim the left handle forward 3 seconds so
the clip starts exactly when she says "Three reasons to love this dress."
Action 2 — SPLIT (cut one clip into two at the playhead)
What it is. Cutting a single clip into two separate pieces at the exact point of the playhead.
Why you do it. To remove a mistake in the middle of a clip, or to separate the good part from the bad part.
How (phone): Move the playhead to where you want to cut. Tap the clip to select it. Tap the Split icon (the scissors). The clip
becomes two clips.
How (desktop): Place the playhead and use the Split button, or press the keyboard shortcut if available (often shown next to
the button; wording/shortcut may differ by version).
Worked example. Adaeze says: "This dress is perfect for church." Then she stumbles: "For... for... for burial too." You split at the
end of "church" and split again at the start of "too". Now the stumble is a separate middle piece.
Action 3 — DELETE (remove the piece you split off)
What it is. Removing a clip or a piece from the timeline.
Why you do it. After you split out the mistake, you delete that piece so it is gone from the video.
How (phone): Select the piece → tap Delete (usually a trash-can icon) or swipe the clip off the timeline (gesture may differ by
version).
Worked example (continued). Select the middle "For... for... for burial" piece and delete it. Now the sentence reads: "This dress
is perfect for church." Clean.
Note about closing the gap: After deleting a middle piece, the clips usually close up automatically (CapCut "ripples"
them together). If a gap appears, trim the clips to meet, or use a "close gap" option if your version has one (wording may
differ).
Action 4 — REORDER (move clips into the right order)
What it is. Changing the sequence of clips.
Why you do it. To make the story follow your script. Your best hook clip might have been shot last.
How (phone): Press and hold a clip, then drag it left or right to a new position. A gap opens and the clip drops in.
Worked example. You shot the dress close-up before the shop-front wide shot, but your script wants the wide shot first. Long-
press the wide shot and drag it to the far left.
Action 5 — UNDO (put it back the way it was)
What it is. Reversing your last action.
Why you do it. Everyone makes mistakes. Undo is your safety net and the most-used button in editing.
How (phone): Tap the curved undo arrow at the top of the screen. There is usually a redo arrow beside it.
Real-life comparison. Undo is like writing with a pencil that has an eraser. You can try things freely because you can always
rub it out.
Rule to teach the class: Cut boldly, and when unsure, undo. You cannot break the original file, so experiment.
F.6 Matching clips to your script
The idea. Your Session 3 script is a shopping list, and editing is matching. For each line of the script, you place the right clip in
the right order.
The method (write this on the board):
1. Read the script Hook line. Find the clip that best shows that line. Put it first.
2. Read the Value section. Put the clips that explain the value next, in a sensible order.
3. Read the CTA line. Put the clip where you (or the product) invite action last.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion "3 reasons" script mapped to clips:
Script part Script words (sample) Clip chosen Length after trim
Hook "Stop! Before you buy that dress, look at this." Adaeze holding the dress, close 3s
Value 1 "Reason one: the fabric does not fade." Close-up of fabric 6s
Value 2 "Reason two: it fits all sizes." Dress on mannequin 6s
Value 3 "Reason three: it is perfect for church." Adaeze smiling to camera 6s
CTA "DM 'FASHION' to order yours today." Shop-front wide shot 7s
Why this matters. When clips match the script, the video makes sense even with the sound off — which is how many people
watch. This is the difference between a "random collection of clips" and a "video".
Common misconception. Learners think they must use every clip they shot. You do not. Unused clips are not wasted — they
are saved for future videos. A good edit uses only what the script needs.
F.7 Basic pacing — keeping the video alive
The idea. Pacing is the speed at which the video moves from one moment to the next. Fast pacing feels energetic; slow pacing
feels calm or boring.
Why it matters. On short vertical platforms, viewers decide in the first 1–3 seconds whether to keep watching. If the first
seconds are slow, they scroll away. Good pacing keeps them.
Rules of thumb for beginners:
1. The hook must be fast. Start within 1–2 seconds. No logos, no long "Hello everyone, welcome back to my channel." Put
the best thing first.
2. Remove all dead air. Any pause longer than about a second where nothing happens should usually be cut.
3. A cut every 3–7 seconds keeps a talking video alive. When you have no B-roll yet (that is next session), tighten the
talking clips instead.
4. End before they are tired. A 45-second video that holds attention beats a 90-second video that loses it.
Worked example — tightening a clip. Adaeze's sentence: "So, umm, this is the ... this is the Ankara dress that, err, we just
received." After trimming/splitting out the "umm", "this is the" repeat and the "err", it becomes: "This is the Ankara dress we
just received." Same meaning, half the time, twice the energy.
Limitation. Do not over-cut until the video feels like a jerky robot. A little breathing room is human. Pacing is a skill you build
with practice — watch your own edit twice and remove only what genuinely drags.
F.8 Auto-captions in CapCut
The idea. Captions (also called subtitles) are the words of the video written on the screen. CapCut can generate them
automatically from your audio — this is called auto-captions.
Why captions matter:
Many people watch videos with the sound off (in a bus, in a class, at work). Without captions, they do not understand your
video.
Captions keep attention and improve accessibility for deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers.
Captions reinforce your message: viewers read and hear at the same time.
How to add auto-captions (phone):
1. Tap Text on the toolbar (in some versions it may be under Captions).
2. Choose Auto captions / Auto generate.
3. Choose the language (for Nigerian English content, English; for pidgin, still often English because CapCut may not support
pidgin — options differ by version).
4. Tap Generate and wait. CapCut listens to your audio and writes the words on the timeline as caption clips.
5. Review every line. Auto-captions make mistakes, especially with names, places and local words.
Correcting captions — the golden rule: Never trust auto-captions without reading them. Tap a caption line to edit its text. Fix:
Names: "Adaeze" might become "A day zay".
Nigerian words and places: "Port Harcourt" might become "Port Harkot"; "Ankara" might become "Anka ra"; "naira" might
become "Nara".
Spelling and grammar.
Timing: if a caption appears before the speaker says it, adjust the caption clip's position.
Formatting captions for readability:
3–5 words per line. A wall of 10 words is hard to read.
Large enough font to read on a small phone screen.
High contrast: white text with a dark outline/stroke, or text on a dark background box, so it is readable over any video.
Keep captions clear of the bottom edge (where platform buttons sit) and clear of the top (where the username sits).
Common misconception. Learners think auto-captions are perfect. They are a first draft. Your job is to correct them. A
misspelled name is a spelling of your own business name wrong in public.
Worked example — a caption correction:
Auto-caption said Corrected to
"come and buy our new anka ra dress" "Come and buy our new Ankara dress"
"visit us at port harkot" "Visit us in Port Harcourt"
"message adaeze fashion" "Message Adaeze Fashion"
Where captions live on the timeline. Each caption is a small clip on a text track above your video. You can move them, split
them, or delete them just like video clips.
F.9 Exporting correctly for vertical platforms
The idea. Exporting is saving your finished edit as a single video file you can upload. If you export with the wrong settings, the
video can look blurry, play choppy, or be refused by the platform.
The correct settings for vertical platforms (Reels, TikTok, Shorts):
Setting Value Why
Resolution 1080 × 1920 (also written 1080p) The standard full-quality vertical size. Clear, not
blurry.
Aspect ratio 9:16 Fills the phone screen.
Frame rate 30fps Smooth standard speed; matches most phone
footage.
Format MP4 The format every platform accepts.
Bit rate Recommended / High (the default "Recommended" is
fine)
Higher bit rate = clearer video, bigger file.
How to export (phone): Tap the Export button (top right, often a small download/arrow icon). Choose the resolution and
frame rate. Tap Export again, and wait. The file saves to your gallery (CapCut may also save a copy inside its own app folder —
wording differs by version).
About watermarks / end cards. Depending on the version, CapCut may add a short end card or a small watermark. Check the
export screen for an option to turn this off, and check the exported video's last second. If your version does not offer the toggle,
keep the video tight and simply note it; do not let a watermark stop you from publishing a good video. (Free-tier behaviour
changes between versions.)
How to confirm the export is correct:
1. Play the exported file from your gallery.
2. Check the picture is sharp, not blocky.
3. Check the captions are readable and correctly timed.
4. Check the sound is present and not crackling.
5. Check the last frame — no unwanted end card.
Common misconception. Learners export at the lowest resolution to "save space" and the video looks soft on other people's
phones. For a 30–60 second video, 1080p is fine and worth the file size.
F.10 Copyright safety — say it plainly
The idea. Using someone else's song, video or photo without permission can get your account a copyright strike, take your
video down, or (for a business) bring trouble. It is illegal and it is also unnecessary.
Rules for this course:
Never re-upload someone else's video as your own. Editing a stolen clip does not make it yours.
For music, use the CapCut built-in music library (marked as free to use for CapCut), or royalty-free sources (see Section C
and next session). Do not record a popular song playing from another phone and lay it over your video.
For footage, use only what you shot, or what you have clear permission to use.
Professional connection. A business that uses a copyrighted song in an advert can be asked to remove it or pay. Using free
libraries protects the business and builds trust.
F.11 Summary of the session
Editing is choosing which seconds the viewer sees and in what order.
The CapCut screen has four zones: preview, timeline, playhead, toolbar.
Five core actions: trim, split, delete, reorder, undo.
Match clips to your script (Hook → Value → CTA).
Pacing keeps the video alive: fast hook, remove dead air, cut every few seconds.
Auto-captions must be corrected for names and Nigerian words; keep 3–5 words per line.
Export at 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4.
Do not use copyrighted music or footage.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner edits their own footage into a tight 30–60 second first cut
that follows their Session 3 script, adds corrected auto-captions, and exports it. Learners without footage use the sample clip
pack with the instructor's Adaeze script.
Starting materials: Your Session 2 footage on your phone; your Session 3 script; CapCut open. Sample clip pack available on
the machine.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Open CapCut → New project. Set ratio to 9:16.
2. Import only 6–12 clips that match your script. Tap Add.
3. Play the whole timeline once. Decide which clip is your hook (first 3 seconds). Pause and predict: "Which clip should come
first? Where is it now?"
4. Reorder so the hook clip is first (long-press and drag).
5. Trim the start and end of every clip so each one begins and ends on useful action. Remove "are we recording?" and the
reach-for-phone moments.
6. Split and delete any stumble or repeat in the middle of clips.
7. Watch the whole video. If it is longer than 60 seconds, find two more places to trim. If it is shorter than 30 seconds, that is
fine for now.
8. Pause and predict: "Read your video with the sound off. Does it still make sense?" (This is why clips must match the script.)
9. Tap Text → Auto captions, choose English, tap Generate.
10. Correct at least three caption errors. Check names (Adaeze, business name), places (Port Harcourt), and local words
(Ankara, naira). Edit each wrong line.
11. Check caption readability: about 3–5 words per line, large font, white text with a dark stroke.
12. Pause and predict: "Is any caption covering someone's face or the bottom buttons?" Move it if yes.
13. Tap Export. Choose 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4. Export.
14. Play the exported file from your gallery and check quality.
Expected result: A 30–60 second vertical video with a clear hook, no stumbles or dead air, readable corrected captions,
matching the script, exported at 1080p in MP4.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 6: "Did the video get shorter or longer? Why?" (Shorter — we removed material.)
After step 9: "What will the captions get wrong first?" (Names and local words.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Video is horizontal → set ratio to 9:16.
Captions far too long → split the caption clips or trim them; aim for 3–5 words.
Captions over the face → drag them higher on the screen.
Export greyed out → make sure the timeline is not empty and the device has storage.
App slow → close other apps; a long high-resolution project needs memory.
Audio missing after export → check the clip's Volume is not set to 0.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Project created in 9:16
Only script-matching clips imported
Hook clip is first
All clips trimmed (dead air removed)
At least one split + delete for a stumble
Video is 30–60 seconds
Auto-captions generated and at least 3 corrected
Captions are readable (3–5 words/line, high contrast)
Exported at 1080 × 1920, 30fps, MP4
Exported file plays correctly

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Sister Ngozi runs the media desk for a small church in Rumuokoro, Port Harcourt. Every Sunday she records the
pastor's 12-minute sermon on her phone and posts the whole thing to the church's Facebook page. The videos get very few
views, and members complain the video is "too long" and "the sound is not clear".
Relevant facts.
The full sermon is 12 minutes. Most viewers leave within the first 30 seconds.
The most powerful moment in the sermon is a 40-second story the pastor tells near the end.
Ngozi has a phone with CapCut. She has never used it.
She does not have a computer and does not want to pay for software.
Decision point. Should Ngozi keep posting the full 12-minute videos, or change her approach?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep posting the full video and hope viewers watch.
2. Post a 45–60 second highlight cut from the sermon — the pastor's best 40-second story plus a hook line at the start
and a CTA ("Full message in bio / Join us Sunday") — using CapCut on her phone.
3. Stop posting videos altogether because the response is poor.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. Short, captioned highlight clips are exactly what vertical platforms
reward, and Ngozi already has everything she needs (phone + free CapCut). The workflow is the one learned today: pick the
best 40 seconds, trim the rest, split out any stumbles, add captions, export vertical.
Reasoning / example workflow:
Find the story (the strongest 40 seconds — the "hook" of the whole sermon).
Trim so the clip starts with the first line of the story.
Split and delete the church announcements before and after it.
Add auto-captions and correct the pastor's name and Bible references.
Add a CTA end frame: "Watch the full message — link in bio."
Export at 1080 × 1920, MP4, and post as a Reel.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is what she already does; it is easy but gets little reach.
Approach 2 takes about 20 minutes per video but turns one 12-minute recording into several short clips — more content,
more reach, and the full video can still be posted separately for those who want it.
Approach 3 loses the church's reach and the members' access — not acceptable, as the media desk exists to serve them.
Professional connection. This "long video into short highlights" workflow is a paid job. Churches, coaches, and event
organisers pay freelancers to cut long recordings into short clips for social media. Learning to trim, split and caption is directly
the skill clients pay for — and it can be done from a phone.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live troubleshooting guide. When a learner hits an issue, do not give the answer immediately — point
them to the relevant row and let them check.
K.1 The video is horizontal / has black bars on the sides
Symptoms: The preview looks like a TV (wide) instead of a tall phone screen; black bars appear. Likely cause: The project ratio
was left at the default (often 16:9), or the footage was shot horizontal. How to investigate: Look at the top of the preview for
the ratio label; check the ratio menu. Corrective steps: Open the Ratio / Aspect ratio menu and set 9:16. If the footage itself
was shot horizontal, it will need to be scaled/cropped to fill; for best results, reshoot vertical next time. How to confirm
resolved: The preview is tall and fills the frame. Prevention: Always set 9:16 at the start of every vertical project.
K.2 Captions are missing or did not generate
Symptoms: You tapped Auto captions but no text appeared. Likely cause: (a) The language was set wrong. (b) The audio is too
quiet or noisy. (c) The download of the caption model failed (needs internet the first time). How to investigate: Check the
language setting; play your audio and listen if the speech is clear; confirm internet. Corrective steps: Re-run Auto captions with
the correct language on good Wi-Fi; if audio is too quiet, raise the clip's volume first, then regenerate. How to confirm
resolved: Caption clips appear on the text track. Prevention: Record clear audio (Session 3 skill) and generate captions while
online.
K.3 Captions are wrong (names, places, local words)
Symptoms: "Adaeze" shows as "A day zay"; "Ankara" as "Anka ra"; the business name is misspelled. Likely cause: Speech
recognition does not know Nigerian names, places or words. How to investigate: Read every caption line against the audio.
Corrective steps: Tap each wrong caption and type the correct text. Fix capitalisation too. How to confirm resolved: Every
caption matches what was said, with correct names. Prevention: Always budget time to proofread captions — never publish
unchecked.
K.4 A wall of text on screen / captions too long
Symptoms: One caption line holds 10 words and is hard to read. Likely cause: Auto-captions grouped long sentences into one
line, or the font/box is small. How to investigate: Pause on the caption and count the words. Corrective steps: Split the
caption clip into shorter pieces (3–5 words) and delete extras; increase font size; add a stroke/outline. How to confirm
resolved: Each line is short and readable at a glance. Prevention: Set a style with a readable size before generating, and split
long lines as you correct them.
K.5 Caption sits on someone's face or under the platform buttons
Symptoms: Text covers a face, or the bottom app buttons hide it. Likely cause: Default caption position. How to investigate:
Preview the video as if on the final platform. Corrective steps: Drag the caption up or down to a clear area; keep it clear of the
very top and very bottom. How to confirm resolved: Text is fully visible and covers nothing important. Prevention: Check
caption position on a phone-sized preview before export.
K.6 Deleted the wrong clip and lost your place
Symptoms: A good part disappeared. Likely cause: You deleted the wrong piece, or split at the wrong point. How to
investigate: Look at the timeline around the gap. Corrective steps: Tap Undo immediately (and repeatedly) until the piece
returns. Then split more carefully. How to confirm resolved: The clip is back and the video flows. Prevention: Move the
playhead slowly and watch the preview before splitting; undo is always available.
K.7 Export fails or takes forever
Symptoms: Export stuck, fails, or the app closes. Likely cause: Low storage, low memory, too many apps open, or very high
export settings. How to investigate: Check free storage; close background apps. Corrective steps: Free up space; close apps;
export at 1080p (not 4K) for short videos; try again. If still failing, restart the phone. How to confirm resolved: Export completes
and the file appears in the gallery. Prevention: Keep at least 5–8 GB free before editing; export at 1080p.
K.8 Exported video has no sound
Symptoms: The video plays but is silent. Likely cause: A clip's Volume was set to 0, or the original clip was muted. How to
investigate: Select the clip → Volume and check the level. Corrective steps: Raise the volume back to normal (100% or as
recorded). How to confirm resolved: Sound plays on the exported file. Prevention: Play your edit once before exporting,
watching for the sound icon/meter.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: In every row above, the likely cause becomes confirmed only after you check it (see the
language setting, run the volume check, look at the storage number). Teach learners to confirm before changing things.
