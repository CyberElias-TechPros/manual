# Copywriting — Hooks, Captions, CTAs and Hashtags

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 4: Copywriting and Repurposing

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (writing, not software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The edited video from Session 5 (or any short video the learner has). Learners must be able to type (or dictate) a few sentences. No prior marketing knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** You have a finished video and a thumbnail. A video with no caption is like a shop with no salesman — people walk past. Today you write the words that make people stop, watch and act: the hook line, the caption, the CTA and the hashtags.

**Preparation before class:** Print good/bad caption examples for the opening; a whiteboard or projector for live rewriting; the Session 5 Adaeze Fashion video ready to use as the running example; learners' phones with a notes app or Google Docs for typing; the Lab Pack "caption template" printed.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain the three parts of a caption: the hook line, the value, and the CTA.
2. Write scroll-stopping hooks using at least three formulas (question, bold claim, number/list, mistake, story).
3. Write a complete caption for their own video, following the hook–value–CTA structure.
4. Choose the right CTA for a given goal (get comments, get orders, get DMs, get followers).
5. Explain how hashtags work and select 3–10 relevant hashtags instead of stuffing 30.
6. Adapt the same message for different platforms — Instagram, TikTok, YouTube Shorts and WhatsApp Status.
7. Repurpose one video into multiple posts with different captions and formats.
8. Use a reusable caption template to speed up writing.
9. Write three captions with hooks and CTAs for their edited video, plus a hashtag set.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show the same 30-second video (e.g., the Adaeze Fashion Reel) three times, each with a
different caption underneath. Do not explain which is "right" yet.
Caption 1 (weak): "Our new dress is available. Check our page. #fashion #dress #style #Naija #Ankara #PortHarcourt
#trending #viral #fyp #love #instagood"
Caption 2 (weak): "Hello everyone, welcome back to our page. Today we want to show you one of our new products.
Please watch the video and let us know your thoughts in the comment section below. Thank you so much for watching."
Ask for a show of hands: "Which of these two captions would make you stop and watch?" Most pick neither enthusiastically.
Then show:
Caption 3 (strong): "Stop before you buy another ankara dress ðŸ‘€ — here are 3 reasons ours never disappoints. Reason 3
will save you money at the tailor. ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order yours today. #AdaezeFashion #PortHarcourtFashion
#AnkaraDress"
What the instructor says:
"The video was exactly the same in all three. The only thing that changed was the words below it. Caption one is a list of
tags with no message. Caption two is polite but boring — nobody reads three lines of 'hello everyone'. Caption three has
a hook ('stop before you buy'), gives value ('3 reasons'), and tells you exactly what to do ('DM FASHION'). That is
copywriting."
Expected learner response: Learners will point at caption three and say "it tells me to do something", "it is interesting", "it is
short". Accept all answers and draw out the three parts: hook, value, CTA.
Then reveal the hook: Show the hashtag block in Caption 1 and ask, "Do you think ten random hashtags like #love and
#instagood bring customers from Port Harcourt to this dress?" Explain that today they will learn which hashtags actually help.
Why this opener works: It isolates the words as the variable (same video, different captions), proving that copywriting — not
the video — changes the result, and it introduces the three-part structure before any explanation.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the three parts of your Session 3 script, and what does each do?"
Expected answer: Hook (stops attention), Value (gives the reason/benefit), CTA (tells the viewer what to do).
Common gap: learners separate the video script from the caption as if they were unrelated. Clarify: the caption repeats and
reinforces the script, using the same three-part logic in words.
Question 2: "What is a thumbnail for, and name one thing that makes it clickable."
Expected answer: a thumbnail drives clicks; a face/emotion, 3–5 big words, high contrast.
Common gap: some learners think the thumbnail is the same as the caption. Clarify: the thumbnail is the picture that gets
the click; the caption is the words that get the watch and the action. They work as a team.
Question 3: "Why do most people watch with the sound off, and how did we handle that in the video?"
Expected answer: they watch in the bus, at work, in class; we handled it with captions.
Common gap: learners forget captions are for everyone, not just the deaf.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your video is the meal. The thumbnail is the signboard. The caption is the salesman at the door — the one who says
'come in, taste this, and here is how to order'. Today you become that salesman, in writing. A businessman with a good
product and no salesman will still be hungry. Let us learn to sell with words."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the three parts of a caption, spend 5 extra minutes writing one caption together on
the board, labelling Hook / Value / CTA, before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What copywriting is
The idea. Copywriting is writing words that persuade someone to stop, watch, and take an action. In content creation, the
"copy" is your caption, your hook, your CTA and your hashtags.
Plain-English definition. Copywriting is selling with words — making a person want to click, watch, buy, comment, or follow,
by choosing the right words in the right order.
Why it exists. You can have the best video in Port Harcourt, but nobody will watch it if the first words do not grab them and the
last words do not tell them what to do. Copywriting exists because attention is scarce and choices are many: people scroll past
hundreds of posts a day. Words are how you win one of those moments.
What it does:
Stops the scroll (the hook).
Keeps them watching (the value).
Tells them what to do next (the CTA).
Reaches the right people (the hashtags).
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader in Port Harcourt. She does not stand silently behind her tomatoes. She calls
out: "Fresh tomatoes! Ripe ones! Five hundred naira! Come and buy!" That is a hook ("fresh tomatoes"), value ("ripe"), and a
CTA ("come and buy"). Copywriting is doing exactly that — but in writing, on a phone screen.
Important limitation / misconception. Copywriting cannot sell a bad product or a misleading video. The words must be
honest — they should promise what the video and product actually deliver. Exaggeration that fails to deliver burns trust and
reduces future reach.
How a learner recognises good copy. Read a caption and ask three questions: Did it make me want to stop? Did it tell me
something useful? Did it tell me what to do? If yes to all three, it is good copy.
F.2 The anatomy of a caption
The idea. A caption is not a random paragraph. It has three jobs, usually in this order:
Job Example (Adaeze Fashion)
1. Hook line
The first line — stop the scroll "Stop before you buy another ankara dress ðŸ‘€"
2. Value
Give the reason/benefit — why keep watching/reading "Here are 3 reasons ours never disappoints…"
3. CTA
Tell them exactly what to do "ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order yours today."
4. Hashtags
Help the right people find you "#AdaezeFashion #PortHarcourtFashion"
The first line is the most important. On most platforms, the caption is cut off after the first line or two with a "more"
button. That means your hook line is the only line many people will ever read. If the first line is weak, the rest does not matter.
Length guidance for beginners: aim for roughly 40–80 words for a short video. Short enough to be read in a few seconds,
long enough to give real value. Use line breaks (blank lines) to make it easy to scan — nobody reads a solid block of text on a
phone.
Real-life comparison. A caption is like the back of a product packet. The first line is the big claim ("Whitens in 7 days"), the
middle is the benefits, the end is the instruction ("Use morning and night"). You read it top to bottom because each part earns
the next.
Common misconception. Learners write captions that describe the video ("In this video I will show you…"). Do not describe the
video — speak to the viewer and give them a reason to care. Nobody is moved by "in this video I will show you"; many are
moved by "this one mistake is costing you money".
F.3 Writing scroll-stopping hooks
The idea. A hook is the very first thing the viewer reads or hears — the line that decides whether they stop scrolling or keep
going. You have about 1–3 seconds to win them.
Why it matters. On TikTok, Reels and Shorts, the hook is the difference between 200 views and 200,000 views. The hook is the
"market trader's shout" that makes a passer-by stop.
Five beginner-friendly hook formulas (with Nigerian examples):
1. The Question Hook. Ask a question the viewer wants answered.
"Still buying cheap ankara that fades after two washes?"
"Do you know why your food business is not getting orders?"
"Want your hair to still look fresh on Sunday evening?"
2. The Bold Claim Hook. State a strong, honest claim.
"This is the only charger you need in Port Harcourt."
"One caption changed my shop's sales. Here is the exact one."
"This is the best jollof on the east side — come and prove me wrong."
3. The Number / List Hook. Promise a specific count.
"3 reasons our dresses never disappoint."
"5 things to check before you buy a phone charger."
"2 mistakes that kill your church flyer."
4. The Mistake / Warning Hook. Warn them about a common error.
"Stop making this mistake before you send money to a tailor."
"Do not post another video until you fix this."
5. The Story Hook. Open a small story that makes them curious.
"I lost ₦18,000 on one cake order — learn from my mistake."
"A customer walked in for one dress… and left with five."
How to test a hook: Read it and imagine a Port Harcourt stranger scrolling. Would they stop? If the first line could appear on
any post from any brand, it is too generic. Make it specific.
Limitation / misconception. Do not make false claims to hook people. "This will make you a millionaire in one day" is a lie and
will damage your account. Hook with truth, not tricks.
F.4 CTAs — telling people what to do
The idea. A CTA = Call To Action. It is the line that tells the viewer the exact next step. Without a CTA, people who enjoyed your
video often do nothing — because you never asked.
Why it exists. People are busy and unsure. A clear CTA removes the decision: it tells them precisely what to do, and where to do
it. A video with no CTA is a shop with the door closed.
Match the CTA to your goal:
Goal CTA examples
Get orders "ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order." / "Call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order."
Get comments "Comment 'AVOCADO' if you have done this before."
Goal CTA examples
Get followers "Follow for more Port Harcourt food spots."
Get DMs / enquiries "Send 'PRICE' to our DM for the price list."
Get shares/saves "Save this post before you forget."
Get visits "Visit us at 24/26 Ebony Road, Port Harcourt."
Rules for strong CTAs:
1. Make it specific. "DM 'FASHION'" is better than "Message us." A single word is easy to obey.
2. Make it easy. One clear action, not three.
3. Make it urgent (honestly). "Only a few left this week" — only if it is true.
4. Put it where they are. For DMs, the CTA says DM; for calls, give the number; for a shop, give the address.
Worked example. Weak: "Hope you like it. Thanks for watching." Strong: "ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order yours today — same-
day delivery in Port Harcourt."
Common misconception. A strong video does not automatically produce action. Even happy viewers need to be told what to
do. Always end with a CTA.
F.5 Hashtags — how they work and how many
The idea. A hashtag is a word or phrase with a # in front of it (e.g., #PortHarcourt ). On Instagram and TikTok, hashtags help
group content and help people discover posts. On YouTube Shorts, hashtags also appear with the video and can help
discovery.
How they work (simple understanding). When someone taps or searches a hashtag, they see posts using it. If your post uses a
relevant hashtag, it can be shown to those interested people. Hashtags help discovery; they do not guarantee views. Relevance
matters far more than quantity.
Relevance over volume — the key lesson:
3–10 good, relevant tags beat 30 random tags. (Some creators use more; for beginners, 3–10 focused tags is the safe,
honest rule.)
Good tags are specific to your location, niche and audience.
Bad tags are generic #love #instagood #photooftheday #fyp #viral — they bring random traffic that will never buy, and
some platforms even warn against them.
A good hashtag set for a Port Harcourt small business (example):
Location: #PortHarcourt #PortHarcourtFashion #PHFood
Niche: #AnkaraStyle #NaijaFashion #MadeInNaija
Business/brand: #AdaezeFashion
That is a focused, relevant set of around 5–8 tags. Much better than 30.
The business (brand) hashtag tip. Create a hashtag for your own business (like #AdaezeFashion ) and use it on every post. Over
time, it becomes a gallery of your work that customers can browse.
Common misconception. "More hashtags = more views." No. Random hashtag stuffing can look spammy, attract the wrong
audience (who never buy), and on some platforms hurt your credibility. Focused and relevant wins.
Limitation. Hashtags are only one discovery tool. Strong hooks, watchable content and a clear CTA matter more.

Why this matters. A caption perfect for Instagram may be too long for WhatsApp Status and too formal for TikTok. Adapting
shows professionalism and reaches more people from the same video.
Common misconception. Learners think they must create brand-new content for each platform. They do not — they
repurpose the same video with adapted words (see F.7).
F.7 Repurposing — one video, many posts
The idea. Repurposing is using one piece of content (a video) to make several posts across platforms and formats, instead of
creating from scratch every time.
Why it exists. Content is hard work. A small business cannot shoot and edit a new video every day. Repurposing multiplies the
value of one video, saves time, and keeps you consistent.
Ways to repurpose one 30-second video:
1. Post the video to Instagram Reels, TikTok and YouTube Shorts with adapted captions.
2. Take 3 screenshots from the video to make 3 photo posts (Carousel) with the same message.
3. Write a short text status (WhatsApp) summarising the video's tip.
4. Turn one B-roll shot into a standalone teaser with a different hook.
5. Save the best-performing clip and reuse it next month with a new hook.
Worked example. Adaeze's one "3 reasons" video becomes: a Reel, a TikTok, a Short, a WhatsApp Status text, and a 3-image
carousel — five posts from one shoot.
Limitation / misconception. Repurposing does not mean posting the identical caption everywhere. It means adapting the
message to each platform (see F.6). Identical copy-paste is lazy and performs worse.
F.8 A reusable caption template
The idea. A template is a fill-in-the-blank structure you can reuse for every post, so you never face a blank screen. Here is a
simple, proven one.
The CEA Caption Template:
```text
[HOOK LINE — stops the scroll. A question, bold claim, number or warning.]
[blank line]
[VALUE — 2–4 short lines. Give the reason, benefit or tip. Use a list if helpful.]
[blank line]
[CTA — one clear action: DM 'WORD' / Comment / Call / Visit / Follow.]
[blank line]
[HASHTAGS — 3–10 relevant tags: location + niche + brand.]
```
Filled example (Adaeze Fashion):
```text
Stop before you buy another ankara dress ðŸ‘€
```
```text
Here are 3 reasons ours never disappoints:
1. The fabric does not fade after washing.
2. It fits all sizes, XS to XXL.
3. It is perfect for church and weddings.
```
```text
ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order yours — same-day delivery in Port Harcourt.
```
```text
#AdaezeFashion #PortHarcourtFashion #AnkaraStyle #NaijaFashion
```
How to use it: copy the template into your notes, fill in the blanks for each video, then adapt slightly per platform. In time, the
structure becomes automatic.
Common misconception. A template is a starting point, not a cage. Change the wording to fit the video. The structure stays;
the words stay fresh.
F.9 Summary of the session
Copywriting = selling with words: stop, watch, act.
A caption has three parts: hook line, value, CTA — plus 3–10 relevant hashtags.
Hooks use formulas: question, bold claim, number/list, mistake/warning, story.
CTAs must be specific, easy and match your goal (DM 'WORD', comment, call, follow).
Hashtags: relevance over volume; 3–10 focused tags (location + niche + brand), not 30 random ones.
Adapt for each platform (Instagram, TikTok, Shorts, WhatsApp Status).
Repurpose one video into several posts.
Use the reusable caption template to write faster.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
On the projector: a weak caption for the Adaeze Fashion video (from the opening activity).
A blank caption template on the board/screen.
A notes/Docs document open for live writing.
The Session 5 video playing so the class can see what they are writing about.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — dissect and label (4 minutes)
Say: "Before we write, let us see why the weak one fails."
Show the weak caption:
"Hello everyone, welcome back to our page. Today we want to show you one of our new products. Please watch the video
and let us know your thoughts in the comment section below. Thank you so much for watching."
Label it on the board out loud:
Hook? "Hello everyone" — weak; it does not stop anyone.
Value? "one of our new products" — vague; no reason to care.
CTA? "let us know your thoughts" — weak and vague.
Hashtags? None, or random.
Say: "This caption does not know what it wants. It is polite, but it does not sell and does not help. Now watch me rebuild it."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — write a hook live (4 minutes)
Say: "The video gives 3 reasons to love the dress. So what is our hook?"
Write several options on the board and discuss each:
Question: "Still buying ankara that fades?"
Bold claim: "This is the only dress you need this season."
Number: "3 reasons our ankara dress never disappoints."
Warning: "Stop buying ankara until you watch this."
Ask the class to vote on the strongest hook, and explain why the number + "stop" combination usually works: it promises
value and creates urgency honestly.
Expected result: The class chooses a hook (usually the number or warning). Write the winner at the top of the blank template.
G.4 Demonstration 3 — build the full caption live (5 minutes)
Fill the template on the projector, narrating each choice:
```text
Stop before you buy another ankara dress ðŸ‘€
```
```text
Here are 3 reasons ours never disappoints:
1. The fabric does not fade after washing.
2. It fits all sizes, XS to XXL.
3. It is perfect for church and weddings.
```
```text
ðŸ‘‰ DM 'FASHION' to order yours — same-day delivery in Port Harcourt.
```
```text
#AdaezeFashion #PortHarcourtFashion #AnkaraStyle #NaijaFashion
```
Point out each part:
Hook: first line.
Value: the numbered list (the reasons match the video).
CTA: specific ("DM 'FASHION'"), easy (one word), with a true benefit (same-day delivery).
Hashtags: 4 relevant tags (brand + location + niche). Say: "Four focused tags, not thirty random ones."
Ask: "Is this caption honest? Does the video really give 3 reasons?" → Yes. Say: "The words must match the video, or trust dies."
G.5 Demonstration 4 — adapt for two platforms (2 minutes)
Take the same caption and adapt it on the spot:
TikTok: "wait till reason 3 ðŸ˜³ | DM FASHION to order #ankara #nigeriantiktok"
WhatsApp Status: "New ankara dresses landed ✨ Call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order. Same-day delivery in PH."
Say: "Same video. Same message. Different clothes for different platforms."
G.6 Questions to ask during the demo
"Which line is the hook?" (The first line.)
"Why is 'DM FASHION' better than 'message us'?" (Specific and easy to obey.)
"Why only four hashtags?" (Relevant beats random; 3–10 focused tags.)
"What would make this caption dishonest?" (Claiming 3 reasons if the video gave fewer, or promising something
undelivered.)
"Can we post this same caption on WhatsApp Status?" (No — adapt it; too long, hashtags not needed there.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes one complete caption (hook + value + CTA + hashtags) for their
own Session 5 video, using the template, then adapts it for a second platform.
Starting materials: Your edited video; a notes app or Google Docs; the printed caption template.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Open your notes app. Copy the caption template (Hook / Value / CTA / Hashtags).
2. Watch your own video once. Pause and predict: "What is the single best reason someone should care about this video?"
Write that as the value.
3. Write three different hooks for the same video — one question, one number/list, one warning. (Worksheet 6.1.)
4. Choose your strongest hook and put it on the first line. Pause and predict: "Would a Port Harcourt stranger stop for this
line?"
5. Write the value in 2–4 short lines. Use a list if you have reasons.
6. Write a specific CTA that matches your goal (order? comments? follow?). Use a single word in quotes if you want DMs (e.g.,
DM 'FASHION').
7. Add 3–10 relevant hashtags: location + niche + your brand.
8. Read the whole caption aloud, slowly. Fix anything vague, dishonest or long.
9. Pause and predict: "Which platform is this caption best for?" Now adapt it for a different platform (e.g., WhatsApp Status
— shorten it and drop hashtags).
10. Save both versions.
Expected result: One complete caption (hook + value + CTA + 3–10 hashtags) plus one adapted version for a second
platform.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 3: "Which of your three hooks would you personally stop for, and why?"
After step 6: "If a viewer follows your CTA, what exactly happens next?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Caption is a solid block → add blank lines; split into hook / value / CTA.
Hook is "hello everyone" → replace with a question, number or warning.
No CTA → add one specific action.
20+ hashtags → cut to 3–10 relevant ones.
CTA and value disagree → align them (if you ask for a DM, make sure the value makes the DM worth it).
Caption promises something the video does not show → fix the words or the video.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Hook line is a question, number, claim or warning (not a greeting)
Value section gives 2–4 clear reasons/benefits
CTA is specific and matches a goal
3–10 relevant hashtags (location + niche + brand)
Blank lines used for easy reading
Caption is honest (matches the video)
A second, platform-adapted version written

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task (new task, done alone): Write a full caption for a different business and video than your own, and a hashtag set, without
copying the instructor's Adaeze example.
Scenario. Mama Chika's Kitchen (from Session 5) has a 30-second video showing jollof rice frying, steam rising, and a
customer smiling at the first bite. The business is in Port Harcourt, sells fresh jollof daily at ₦1,500, and delivers within the
city. Their goal is more orders by phone call.
Requirements:
1. Write a caption with all four parts: hook, value, CTA, hashtags.
2. The hook must use one of the five formulas (question, bold claim, number, warning, story).

## J. Real-World Case Study

Situation. Emeka runs a small phone-accessory shop in Port Harcourt. He posts the same video to Instagram and TikTok every
week, always with the caption: "Quality phones and accessories. Call us." His videos get a few views, and he rarely gets orders
from social media. Meanwhile, a competitor, Blessing, posts similar videos but with captions like "Your phone dies by 2pm?
This power bank fixes that — DM 'CHARGE' to order" and gets steady DMs.
Relevant facts.
Emeka's videos are actually fine — clear product shots and good light.
Emeka's caption never changes and gives no reason to care, no specific action, and no hashtags.
Blessing's caption uses a question hook (pain point: phone dying), a value (the power bank solves it), a specific CTA (DM
Emeka has the same products and similar prices.
Decision point. What should Emeka change — his videos, his products, or his captions?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy new equipment and reshoot better videos.
2. Rewrite his captions using hook–value–CTA and relevant hashtags.
3. Copy Blessing's exact captions word for word.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. The problem is not the video or the product — it is the words. Emeka's

Reasoning / example rewrite for Emeka's power-bank video:

Value: "This 20,000mAh power bank charges your phone 4 times — take it anywhere in PH."

template forever.
Approach 3 is dishonest copying and will not fit Emeka's products or brand; it also risks looking identical to a competitor.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 The hook is a greeting ("Hello everyone, welcome back")
Symptoms: Very few people stop; view counts stay low. Likely cause: The first line is polite but not interesting; it gives no
reason to stop. How to investigate: Read only the first line. Would a stranger stop? Corrective steps: Replace it with a
question, number, bold claim or warning. How to confirm resolved: The first line raises a question or promise. Prevention:
Never start a caption with a greeting; start with the hook.
K.2 No CTA (or a vague one)
Symptoms: People watch but do not order, comment or follow. Likely cause: The caption never told them what to do, or said
"message us" with no specifics. How to investigate: Find the last line. Is there one clear action? Corrective steps: Add a
specific CTA matching the goal — "DM 'FASHION'", "Call 0803-XXX-XXXX", "Comment 'YES'". How to confirm resolved:
There is exactly one clear action. Prevention: End every caption with a CTA.
K.3 Hashtag stuffing (20–30 random tags)
Symptoms: Random traffic, wrong audience, sometimes reduced reach; looks spammy. Likely cause: A belief that more
hashtags = more views. How to investigate: Count the tags and ask if each relates to the post. Corrective steps: Cut to 3–10
relevant tags: location + niche + brand. How to confirm resolved: Every remaining tag is relevant. Prevention: Save a small
set of proven relevant hashtags and reuse it.
K.4 Caption is an essay (a solid block of text)
Symptoms: Nobody reads past the first line; the message is buried. Likely cause: No line breaks, too many words. How to
investigate: Look at it on a phone — is it scannable? Corrective steps: Break into hook / value / CTA with blank lines; cut to
~40–80 words. How to confirm resolved: It reads easily in a few seconds. Prevention: Use the template; keep short lines.
K.5 Caption does not match the video (clickbait)
Symptoms: Viewers leave quickly; comments accuse you of misleading them; trust drops. Likely cause: The hook promises
something the video does not deliver. How to investigate: Watch the video, then re-read the hook. Corrective steps: Change
the words to match the video, or re-edit the video to deliver the promise. How to confirm resolved: The caption and video tell
the same story. Prevention: Write the caption after watching the final video.
K.6 Same caption copy-pasted on every platform
Symptoms: It feels off — too long for WhatsApp, too formal for TikTok. Likely cause: No adaptation. How to investigate:
Check the platform's style (length, tone, hashtags). Corrective steps: Adapt length and tone per platform; drop hashtags on
WhatsApp Status. How to confirm resolved: The caption fits the platform. Prevention: Keep one master caption, then tailor a
short version per platform.
K.7 The value is vague
Symptoms: "Quality products", "Best service" — no one is moved. Likely cause: Generic words instead of specific benefits.
How to investigate: Ask "so what?" after each line. Corrective steps: Replace vague claims with specifics: "charges your phone
4 times", "delivery in 2 hours", "₦1,500 a plate". How to confirm resolved: Each value line gives a concrete reason. Prevention:
Always answer "what is the benefit for the viewer?"
K.8 CTA and value contradict
Symptoms: You ask for a phone call but the video is about a free tip; or you ask for a DM but give no detail to DM about. Likely
cause: The CTA was chosen before the message. How to investigate: Read the value then the CTA. Corrective steps: Align
them — the CTA should be the natural next step after the value. How to confirm resolved: The action makes sense right after
the value. Prevention: Decide the value first, then choose the CTA that follows from it.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Check before fixing — count the hashtags, find the CTA line, watch the video against the
hook. Confirm the cause, then correct.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner's hook line is a question/claim/number/warning, not a greeting, without being told.
Learner's CTA is specific ("DM 'FASHION'") rather than vague ("message us").
Learner selects a small, relevant hashtag set instead of stuffing.
Learner adapts the caption for a second platform (shorter for WhatsApp Status).
Learner checks the caption against the video for honesty.
Learner uses line breaks to make the caption scannable.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"More hashtags = more views." → No; relevance beats volume.
"A good video sells itself; no caption needed." → Even happy viewers need a hook and a CTA.
"I should write whatever I feel." → Write for the viewer's benefit, not your mood.
"I can copy my competitor's caption." → Adapt the structure, not the content; copying is dishonest and off-brand.
"Exaggerating gets more clicks." → Clickbait that fails to deliver destroys trust and reach.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Hook rewrite drill: give five weak first lines ("Hello everyone", "We sell quality") and ask the learner to rewrite each as a
question/number/warning.
2. CTA matching drill: give four goals and four CTAs; ask the learner to match and justify.
3. Hashtag sort drill: give 20 hashtags (some relevant, some random) and ask the learner to keep only the 5–8 relevant ones.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a caption template with the value already filled in; learner writes only the hook and the CTA.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full caption pack for three posts.
Level 3 (challenge): write a second hook version for each post as an A/B test (two hooks for the same video), and add a
short carousel caption that turns the video's 3 reasons into 3 slides; explain which hook you expect to perform better and
why.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners writing their own captions (Sections H and the assignment). If the room is slow,
shorten Section J to a 3-minute mention and limit the platform-adaptation demo (G.5). Protect writing time — a learner who
leaves without a written caption has no copy deliverable.
