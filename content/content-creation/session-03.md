# Clear Audio and Simple Scripts

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: See and Plan (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 (niche, audience, hooks) and Session 2 (stable, lit, framed phone footage, shot lists). A learner should have shot a short clip and completed a shot list. If a learner missed Session 2, give the two-minute catch-up: "Face the light, hold steady, use the rule of thirds." Connection to previous session In Session 2 learners learned to capture a clean picture. Today we fix the other half of video — sound — and then give the content structure by writing a script (hook–body–CTA), a storyboard and a shot list. The 30-second script and 3-panel storyboard produced today are the plan for the first capstone video.

**Preparation before class:** Prepare one clip with good picture but bad audio (recorded in a noisy room, phone far away) and one with clear audio (quiet room, close mic); have both ready to play. Ensure earphones with a built-in mic are available (learners' own are fine). Have a lavalier (lapel) mic for demonstration if the academy has one. Print Worksheet 3.1 ("My 30-Second Script") and Worksheet 3.2 ("Three-Panel Storyboard"). Find a quiet corner/room for the audio recording practice.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why audio quality often matters more than picture quality for whether a video is watched.
2. Identify common audio problems — background noise, wind, hum, echo and distance — and how they happen.
3. Record clear speech using the phone mic, an earphone mic or an external mic, with the mic close to the speaker in a quiet
space.
4. Write a 30-second script using the hook–body–call-to-action (CTA) structure.
5. Draw a three-panel storyboard matched to a script.
6. Build a shot list that supports the script and storyboard.
7. Read a script aloud naturally (not robotically) and adjust it to fit a time limit.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware

Earphones with a built-in microphone (the standard ones that come with phones). Learners should bring their own if

A lavalier (lapel) mic for instructor demonstration if available.
A quiet room or corner for recording.
Optional: a soft cloth or foam windscreen for outdoor audio (or simply record indoors).
Software
The phone's built-in camera/voice recorder app. No editing software needed today.
Accounts
None required today.

Instructor prepares two clips: one with good picture + bad audio; one with good picture + clear audio. These anchor the

An example 30-second script and its matching 3-panel storyboard on the projector (use Adaeze Fashion).
Worksheets
Worksheet 3.1 — "My 30-Second Script" (one page with a hook / body / CTA structure and a word-count guide).
Worksheet 3.2 — "Three-Panel Storyboard" (one page with three large boxes for drawing).
Tools
Whiteboard and markers.
A timer or phone stopwatch for the 30-second read.
Internet
Not required. Wi-Fi optional for looking up free, royalty-free music later (introduced properly in Session 5).

Learners without earphones record with the phone mic held close, or use the phone's voice recorder. All script and

reads the script.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Play the two prepared clips behind a black/blank screen or with the phone screen hidden, so learners only
hear the clips. Clip A has very noisy audio (a busy market or two people talking over each other). Clip B has clear audio (a quiet
room, close mic).
What the instructor says:
"I am going to play two clips. Do not look at the screen — just listen carefully. For each one, I want you to decide: would
you keep watching this if it appeared on your feed?"
(Play Clip A first, then Clip B. Ask for a show of hands for "would keep watching".)
"Both clips have the same clear picture. The only difference was the sound. Most of you said you would leave the noisy one
— and you never even saw the picture. That is the lesson of today: bad audio makes people leave faster than bad
picture. People will forgive a slightly soft picture, but they will not forgive sound they cannot understand."
Concrete hook (do this): Ask the class to cover their ears and hum while the instructor speaks a sentence. Then ask what the
instructor said. (Almost no one knows.) Say:
"You could see my mouth moving perfectly — but the sound was breaking it. That is what a noisy video does to a viewer."
Expected learner response: Learners say the noisy clip was "stressful", "hard to hear", "I would scroll past". Confirm their
instinct and reveal that the one they rejected had the same visual quality as the one they liked.
Why this opener works: It proves, with the learners' own reaction, that audio is the hidden make-or-break factor — and it
primes them to value today's quieter, less glamorous skill.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run a quick oral round. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "Name two of the four factors that matter more than the camera brand."
Expected answer: stability and light (or framing and audio).
Common gap: learners forget audio. Perfect — today is about audio.
Question 2: "What is the rule of thirds, and why does the empty side of the frame matter?"
Expected answer: 3×3 grid, subject on a line; the empty side is for text/captions.
Common gap: confusion between grid lines and headroom. Re-state briefly.
Question 3: "From your Session 2 assignment, what is one hook line you will build a script around today?"
Expected answer: a learner's own hook.
Common gap: no hook. Pair the learner with someone who has one, or ask them to write a quick hook now using the C-P-
C pattern from Session 1.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your pictures are clean now. But a clean picture with muddy sound is still a failed video. Today we fix the sound, and we
give your hook a structure — a script and a storyboard — so your video has a clear beginning, middle and end instead of
rambling."
If more than a third cannot answer Question 1 or 2: spend 10 extra minutes on a combined recap of Session 1 (hook) and
Session 2 (stability/light/framing) before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why audio matters more than video
The idea. Audio is the sound in your video: the voice, the background noise, the music. When viewers watch a phone video, the
sound they hear must be clear and comfortable.
Plain-English definition. "Clear audio" means the voice is loud enough, easy to understand, and not buried under noise, wind,
echo or hum.
Why audio matters so much. Viewers are surprisingly forgiving of a slightly soft or slightly dark picture, but they will leave
immediately if the sound is painful or hard to follow. They often watch short videos with the sound on first — and if it hurts,
they scroll. Good audio also builds trust: a clear voice sounds professional and confident.
The two enemies of audio:
1. Distance — the farther the mic is from the speaker, the more the voice fades and the room noise rises.
2. Noise — anything else in the room: traffic, fans, generators, wind, other people, echo from bare walls.
Analogy. Think of a phone call. If the person is far from their phone in a noisy room, you struggle. If they hold the phone to their
mouth in a quiet room, you hear them perfectly. Video audio works the same way.
Common misconception. "The picture is what matters; sound is a bonus." In reality, viewers abandon bad audio far more often
than bad picture. Audio is not a bonus; it is half the video.
Limitation. Some audio problems cannot be fully fixed after recording. A noisy room recorded badly cannot be cleaned in a
simple editor. That is why we capture it cleanly at the source — the same "get it right when you shoot" principle as stability and
light.
F.2 Recording clear audio — the golden rule
The idea. The golden rule of audio is simple: get the microphone close to the speaker, in a quiet place. Almost every other
technique follows from this.
Why it works. Sound gets quieter and noisier with distance. Moving the mic from 2 metres to 20 centimetres makes the voice
dramatically louder and the room noise much quieter relative to the voice. This is called improving the "signal-to-noise ratio":
the voice (signal) becomes strong compared to the background (noise).
Plain-English definition. Signal-to-noise ratio just means "how much louder is the good sound (the voice) compared to the
bad sound (the noise)". We want the good sound much louder.
How to record clear audio (step by step):
1. Choose a quiet place. A small room with soft furnishings (curtains, a bed, a rug) absorbs echo. Avoid bare-tiled rooms,
busy corridors, generators, fans and main roads.
2. Turn off noise-makers. Fans, air conditioners and generators off if possible, or move away from them.
3. Get the mic close. Hold the phone (or the earphone mic) about 15–30 cm (a hand-span) from the speaker's mouth,
slightly to the side so breath does not hit the mic.
4. Use the right mic: the phone's built-in mic works if close; an earphone mic (the little one on your earphone cable) is
usually clearer; a lavalier (lapel) mic clips to the collar and is the best simple option when available.
5. Speak at a normal volume, slightly slower than usual, and pause between sentences.
6. Record a few seconds of "room tone" (silence) at the start — it helps when editing to smooth gaps.
7. Listen back with earphones before you move on. If you cannot understand every word, re-record.
8. Avoid wind outdoors. Wind destroys phone audio. Record indoors, or shelter the mic, or use a foam windscreen.
9. Avoid handling noise. Do not rub the phone case or touch the mic cable while recording; this creates rumbles.
Analogy. Recording audio well is like serving food hot. You cannot "reheat" a badly recorded conversation in editing into
something appetising — it is much easier to serve it right the first time.
Worked example. Adaeze records her voiceover in her bedroom in the evening: fan off, door closed, phone's earphone mic
held a hand-span from her mouth, speaking slowly. The result is clean voice, no echo. Later she records the same lines in her
shop in the afternoon with the fan on and the phone on a table two metres away — the result is hollow, humming and hard to
follow. The words were identical; the setup decided everything.
Common misconception. "I can remove the noise later." Simple tools like CapCut have a "reduce noise" feature, but it cannot
fully rescue badly noisy audio — and it often makes the voice sound robotic. Capture it clean.
Limitation. In very loud real-world settings (a busy market, a live event), clean voice audio may be impossible without special
gear. In those cases, choose a different approach: record the voice separately in a quiet place, add music, or use on-screen text
instead of speech. This is a real professional skill — knowing when not to rely on live sound.
F.3 The video script — hook, body, call-to-action
The idea. A script is a written plan of what will be said (and often shown) in your video. A short video script follows a simple
three-part structure: hook → body → call-to-action (CTA).
Why it exists. Without a script, people ramble, forget their point, and run over time. A script keeps the video tight, purposeful
and on message — and it saves a lot of re-shooting.
The three parts:
1. Hook (first 3 seconds/one sentence). Grab attention. Uses the C-P-C pattern from Session 1.
2. Body (the middle). Deliver the value: the tip, the product detail, the story, the demonstration. Keep it to one main idea.
3. Call-to-action (CTA) (last line). Tell the viewer exactly what to do next: "Order now", "Save this video", "Send us a message",
"Follow for more".
Analogy. A script is a recipe. A recipe has a start (the idea), a middle (the steps) and a finish (the dish served). A video with no
script is cooking without a recipe — it might work, but it usually doesn't.
Worked example — a 30-second Adaeze Fashion script:
Hook (0–3s): "This ₦8,000 dress made a bride's sister cry at her wedding." Body (3–24s): "Here's why. It's made from
a soft cotton blend that doesn't wrinkle, so it still looks fresh after hours of sitting and dancing. It comes in three colours,
and the cut flatters both tall and petite frames. I styled it with simple sandals here, but it also works with heels for church."
CTA (24–30s): "Tap the link in our bio to order yours — limited colours this week."
Notice: one idea (one dress), one clear structure, one action.
Common misconception. "A script means reading stiffly like a newsreader." No — a script is a guide. Write it in your natural
speaking voice, then say it naturally. You can also write bullet points instead of full sentences for a looser, more relaxed delivery.
Limitation. For some types of content (a spontaneous vlog, a reaction) a full script would feel fake. In those cases, write a simple
outline (hook + key points + CTA) and improvise the middle. The structure still holds.
Word-count guide for 30 seconds: In normal, slightly-slow speech, people say roughly 70–80 words in 30 seconds. So keep
a 30-second script to about 75 words. Beyond that, it will not fit.
F.4 Storyboards — seeing the video before you shoot it
The idea. A storyboard is a set of simple drawings (or rough sketches) showing the main shots of your video in order, each with
a caption of what happens and what is said. It is the video drawn as a comic strip.
Why it exists. A storyboard lets you "see" the video before you shoot it, so you plan your shots, avoid missing key moments,
and save time. It also helps you match the words (script) to the pictures (shots).
How to make a simple three-panel storyboard:
1. Divide the page into three boxes (beginning, middle, end).
2. In each box, draw a rough stick-figure sketch of what the camera will show.
3. Under each box, write:
the shot type (wide / medium / close-up / over-the-shoulder),
the words being said at that point (from the script),
any text on screen.
Analogy. A storyboard is like an architect's plan before building a house. The architect draws the rooms before anyone lays
blocks. You draw the shots before anyone presses record.

Limitation. A storyboard is a plan, not a prison. If a better shot appears while shooting, take it. Update the board when you get
home.
F.5 The shot list — the practical bridge to shooting
The idea. A shot list (introduced in Session 2) is the practical, written list of every shot you need. The storyboard shows the idea;
the shot list tells you exactly what to capture and how.
How they work together.
The script says what will be said.
The storyboard shows roughly what each part looks like.
The shot list spells out each shot (type, description, notes) so the shoot runs smoothly.
Worked example — the shot list behind the Adaeze storyboard:
# Type Description Notes
1 Close-up Hand pulls dress from the rack By window, tap-lock focus on fabric
2 Medium Adaeze holds and explains the dress Tripod, eye level, face the window
3 Medium Adaeze smiles and points toward the link End with on-screen text "Order in bio"
Analogy. If the storyboard is the recipe card, the shot list is the shopping list that makes sure you have every ingredient.
Common misconception. "If I have a storyboard I don't need a shot list." The storyboard is visual and general; the shot list is
specific and practical. Professionals use both.
Limitation. For very simple one-shot videos, a full shot list may be overkill — a storyboard line or two is enough. Match the
planning effort to the size of the job.
F.6 Reading naturally and keeping to time
The idea. A script is only good if it is spoken naturally and fits the time. Two skills: sounding human, and being brief.
How to sound natural:
Write the way you talk (use contractions: "it's", "you're", "don't").
Read it aloud while writing; if a sentence feels long, shorten it.
Speak slightly slower than normal; pause at the end of each sentence.
Smile while you speak — it changes the tone of your voice.
Practise two or three times before recording.
Keeping to time:
Aim for about 75 words per 30 seconds.
Use a stopwatch. Read the script and time it. If it is too long, cut words — do not speak faster.
If it is too short, the video may need a B-roll moment (a cutaway shot); note it for editing.
Analogy. Speaking naturally is like talking to a friend, not reciting in an assembly hall. Carry that tone into the recording.
Common misconception. "Faster is better to fit more in." Fast speech is hard to understand and sounds nervous. Cut content
instead; keep the delivery calm.
Limitation. Some people simply speak faster or slower naturally. Adjust the word target to your own pace after one timed read.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
Two clips (good picture + bad audio; good picture + clear audio) for the opener.
A quiet corner of the room.
Earphones with mic; a lavalier mic if available.
Adaeze Fashion script and storyboard on the projector.
A stopwatch.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — record clear audio, then ruin it (8 minutes)
Say: "Watch me record one clean line, then break it on purpose so you can see exactly what causes bad audio."
Step by step:
1. In the quiet corner, hold the phone (or earphone mic) a hand-span from the instructor's mouth. Record: "This ₦8,000
dress made a bride's sister cry at her wedding." Play it back. It is clear.
2. Now move the phone two metres away onto a table. Record the same line. Play it back. It sounds distant and hollow.
3. Now turn a fan on (or create a hum). Record again. Play it. The voice is buried.
4. Now hold the phone but rub the case while recording. Play it. Rumbling handling noise.
5. Now hold the earphone mic close in the quiet corner. Record. Play it. Noticeably clearer than the phone's built-in mic.
Expected result: learners can hear the difference between close/quiet and far/noisy, and between mic options.
Deliberately break it: This is the demonstration of breaking it. Say: "Every one of these mistakes is easy to avoid — check
these three things every time: quiet room, mic close, listen back."
Errors to watch and correct:
Speaker too close to a bare wall (echo) → move away or add soft furnishings.
Breath popping into the mic → angle the mic slightly to the side of the mouth.
Waiting to "fix it later" → re-record now, not later.
Questions to ask: "Which clip could you understand without effort?" "What changed — the voice or the room?" "Could an
editor fully remove that fan noise without harming the voice?" (Not really.)
G.3 Demonstration 2 — build a script and storyboard live (7 minutes)
Say: "Now I will turn a hook into a full 30-second script and storyboard, live."
Step by step on the projector:
1. Start with the hook: "This ₦8,000 dress made a bride's sister cry at her wedding."
2. Write the body in three short sentences (the dress's qualities and how it can be styled).
3. Write the CTA: "Tap the link in our bio to order yours — limited colours this week."
4. Count the words aloud with the class. If over ~75, cut together.
5. Time a read with the stopwatch. If over 30 seconds, trim.
6. Draw the three-panel storyboard beside the script, one panel per part, with shot types.
Expected result: a complete script + storyboard from a single hook, built in front of the class.
Error to show and correct: the instructor initially writes a long, wordy body ("This dress is made from cotton and polyester and
it has these buttons and these seams..."). Ask the class to help cut it to one clear idea. Say: "One video, one idea. More ideas
belong in more videos."
Questions to ask: "Does the hook make you want to hear the body?" "Does the CTA tell a viewer exactly what to do?" "Does the
storyboard match the script?"

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes a 30-second script (hook–body–CTA) and draws a 3-panel
storyboard for their own chosen business/hook.
Starting materials: Worksheet 3.1 (My 30-Second Script), Worksheet 3.2 (Three-Panel Storyboard), Session 1 hook, Session 2
shot list, a pen.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners work; instructor walks the room):
1. Write the hook at the top of Worksheet 3.1. Use the C-P-C pattern. Keep it to one spoken sentence.
2. Write the body in 3–4 short sentences. Deliver one main idea only (one product, one tip, one story).
3. Write the CTA as one clear line telling the viewer what to do next.
4. Count the words. Target about 75 words. Cut anything unnecessary.
5. Pause and predict: before reading, ask learners: "Will this fit 30 seconds? Guess now." Then time it.
6. Time a read with a stopwatch (or ask a neighbour to time). Adjust by cutting words, not by speeding up.
7. Draw the 3-panel storyboard on Worksheet 3.2: one panel for the hook, one for the body, one for the CTA. In each box,
sketch the shot and write the shot type and the words.
8. Match check: confirm the storyboard order matches the script order.
9. Write a short shot list (3 shots) on the back of the storyboard to match the panels.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 4: "Is this 75 words or 120? What should we cut?"
After step 7: "Which panel needs a close-up, and why?"
Expected result: each learner has a complete 30-second script, a 3-panel storyboard and a 3-shot list for their own content.
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Script too long → cut to one idea; drop adjectives; aim for 75 words.
No clear CTA → ask "What exactly should the viewer do after watching? Write that."
Storyboard does not match script → line them up side by side and fix.
Learner can't draw → reassure with stick figures; the drawing is for planning, not display.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Hook written as one spoken sentence.
Body delivers a single clear idea.
CTA tells the viewer a specific action.
Script is about 75 words and fits ~30 seconds.
Three storyboard panels drawn with shot types.
Three-shot list matches the panels.

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task: On your own, write a 30-second script and 3-panel storyboard for a different business, then record the voiceover with
clear audio.
Scenario: Pastor Ken's Youth Ministry at a Port Harcourt church wants a 30-second clip inviting young people to a weekend
youth fellowship. You will write the script, storyboard it, and record the invited voiceover cleanly.
Requirements:
1. Write a 30-second script using hook → body → CTA:
Hook: something that makes a young person stop scrolling (curiosity or a relatable problem).
Body: the key details (what, when, where, why they should come).
CTA: a clear action (e.g., "Bring a friend this Saturday").
2. Keep it to about 75 words.
3. Draw a 3-panel storyboard with shot types matching the script.
4. Record the voiceover in a quiet spot with the mic close (phone mic, earphone mic or lav mic). The recording must be
understandable without effort.
5. Listen back with earphones and note one thing you would improve.
Constraints:
One main idea (the fellowship invitation).
No background noise audible in the recording.
Mic close to the speaker (hand-span).
Time: 15 minutes total (write, storyboard, record).
Success criteria:
Script follows hook–body–CTA and is about 75 words.
Storyboard has three panels with shot types.
The voiceover is clear: every word understandable, no wind/hum/echo dominating.
The learner can say one improvement they noticed on playback.
Hints available if stuck:

Hint 2: Body must answer "what, when, where" in two or three short sentences.
Hint 3: If the room echoes, record inside a room with curtains/beds, or speak closer to the mic and away from bare walls.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ifeoma sells handmade soaps on Instagram from her home in Port Harcourt. Her products photograph beautifully,
and she films short videos of the soaps in her kitchen. But her videos always do poorly. When a friend watched one closely, the
problem became obvious: Ifeoma records while her generator is running outside, her phone sits on the counter two metres
from her, and her small tiled kitchen makes her voice echo. Viewers comment "can't hear you" and leave.
Relevant facts.
The picture is good; the audio is the failure.
The noise sources are the generator, the distance and the echo.
Ifeoma speaks quickly and does not listen back before posting.
She has earphones with a mic but has never used the mic for recording.
Decision point. Should Ifeoma (a) keep posting and hope, (b) give up on videos and post only photos, or (c) change where and
how she records the audio?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep posting the same way. The same cause keeps the same result.
2. Give up on video. Photos are fine, but video builds much more connection and trust — and she can fix this easily.
3. Fix the audio. Turn off the generator while recording, move to a room with curtains (less echo), hold the earphone mic a
hand-span from her mouth, speak a little slower, and listen back before posting.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Nothing needs to be bought; the tools she already has (her phone, her earphones, a
quieter room) are enough.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 repeats the mistake.
Approach 2 sacrifices the reach and trust that video brings.
Approach 3 asks Ifeoma to change her routine (quieter room, closer mic, listen-back habit), but it is free and directly fixes
the cause.
Professional connection. Professional video makers treat audio as a first-class discipline. On real film sets there is a whole job
title — the sound recordist — whose only concern is clean audio, because directors know that audiences forgive a lot except
bad sound. Ifeoma is doing, at a small scale, exactly what professionals do.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
K.1 The voice is hard to understand
Symptoms: Viewers cannot hear the words clearly. Likely cause: Mic is too far away, or the room is noisy. How to investigate:
Listen back with earphones; identify whether the voice is quiet or buried under noise. Corrective steps: Move the mic closer
(hand-span), move to a quieter room, speak slightly louder and slower. How to confirm resolved: Every word is
understandable on playback. Prevention: Always record close and quiet; listen back before finishing.
K.2 Loud background noise (fan, generator, traffic)
Symptoms: A steady hum or rumble under the voice. Likely cause: A running appliance or location noise. How to investigate:
Listen carefully; identify the sound and its source. Corrective steps: Turn off fans/generators, close windows, move rooms, or
record at a quieter time. How to confirm resolved: The voice stands out with no competing sound. Prevention: Do a 5-second
"silence test" before recording — if you hear noise in silence, fix it first.
K.3 Wind noise outdoors
Symptoms: A harsh "whooshing" or rumbling sound. Likely cause: Wind hitting the mic. How to investigate: Note whether
recording is outdoors or near a breeze. Corrective steps: Move indoors; shelter the mic behind your body; use a foam
windscreen; angle away from wind. How to confirm resolved: No whooshing on playback. Prevention: Prefer indoor
recording; never point the mic at the wind.
K.4 Echo / hollow sound
Symptoms: The voice sounds like it is in a bathroom. Likely cause: A bare, hard room (tiles, bare walls) with no soft materials.
How to investigate: Clap once; a long ring means echo. Corrective steps: Record in a room with curtains, a bed or a rug;
move closer to the mic; add a soft cloth behind the speaker. How to confirm resolved: The voice sounds tight and clear.
Prevention: Choose soft-furnished rooms for voice recording.
K.5 Handling noise
Symptoms: Rumbles or taps under the voice. Likely cause: Rubbing the phone, touching the mic cable, or bumping the stand.
How to investigate: Listen for the noise lining up with hand movements. Corrective steps: Hold still; do not touch the phone
or cable during recording; use a stand. How to confirm resolved: No rumbles on playback. Prevention: Set the phone down or
hold it firmly and motionless.
K.6 Script does not fit 30 seconds
Symptoms: The video runs long, or the reading feels rushed. Likely cause: More than one main idea; too many words (over
~75). How to investigate: Count words and time a read. Corrective steps: Cut to one idea; delete adjectives and repeats; do
not speed up. How to confirm resolved: A comfortable read fits about 30 seconds. Prevention: Write to ~75 words from the
start.
K.7 Delivery sounds robotic
Symptoms: The presenter sounds like a newsreader reading a page. Likely cause: Reading full sentences stiffly; not rehearsing.
How to investigate: Listen to the playback for flat, fast or stiff delivery. Corrective steps: Rewrite in natural speech; use bullet
points; rehearse two or three times; smile. How to confirm resolved: The delivery sounds like a real person talking. Prevention:
Write the way you talk; practise before recording.
Likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after checking — for example, doing a silence test, clapping
for echo, or listening to the playback. Confirm before correcting.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. Why do viewers often leave a video with clear picture but bad audio?
Q2. Multiple choice. The best way to record clear voice audio is: a) Put the phone on a table two metres away b) Record in a
busy market for realism c) Keep the mic close (about a hand-span) in a quiet room d) Shout from across the room
Q3. Explain in your own words. What does "signal-to-noise ratio" mean, in simple terms?
Q4. Practical identification. Name the three parts of a short-video script and say what each does.
Q5. Error / scenario. A learner records a voiceover in a tiled room with a ceiling fan on, phone on the table. List two problems
this causes and one fix for each.
Q6. Short answer. Roughly how many words fit in a 30-second script, and why does this matter?
Q7. Explain in your own words. What is a storyboard, and how is it different from a shot list?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. Because audio that is hard to understand, painful or noisy makes people uncomfortable and they scroll away fast — and
they may judge the content as unprofessional. Clear audio is essential for understanding the message.
A2. (c) Keep the mic close (about a hand-span) in a quiet room.
A3. "Signal-to-noise ratio" means how much louder the good sound (the voice) is compared to the unwanted sound
(background noise). We want the voice much louder than the noise, which is why we get close and stay quiet.
A4. (1) Hook — grabs attention in the first seconds. (2) Body — delivers the main value or message (one idea). (3) Call-to-
action (CTA) — tells the viewer what to do next.
A5. Problems and fixes: (1) echo from the tiled room → record in a room with curtains/soft furnishings, or get closer; (2) hum
from the ceiling fan → turn the fan off while recording (and place the phone closer, not on a table far away — that is a third
valid problem). (Accept any two with sensible fixes.)
A6. About 70–80 words (roughly 75) fit in 30 seconds. It matters because going over the limit makes the video too long or
the delivery rushed, which loses viewers; you should cut words, not speed up.
A7. A storyboard is a set of simple drawings showing the main shots in order (the video as a comic strip) with the words and
shot types. A shot list is a written list of each shot to capture (type, description, notes) without drawings. The storyboard shows
the idea visually; the shot list is the practical capture plan. They work together.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner records a voiceover that is understandable on the first listen, with the mic close and noise controlled.
Learner does a silence test and listens back before finishing.
Learner writes a script with a clear hook, single-idea body and specific CTA.
Learner keeps the script to about 75 words and times the read.
Learner's storyboard matches the script order and includes shot types.
Learner can explain why a noisy recording cannot simply be "fixed later".
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Sound is a bonus; picture matters most." → Correct: viewers leave bad audio fast.
"I'll clean the noise later in editing." → Correct: it cannot be fully removed; record clean.
"A script makes me sound stiff." → Correct: write like you talk; use bullet points; rehearse.
"I can't draw, so I can't storyboard." → Correct: stick figures are enough.
"Faster speech fits more content." → Correct: cut words instead; fast speech loses viewers.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Close vs far drill: record the same line at 1 metre and at a hand-span; compare with earphones.
2. Silence test habit: have learners sit quietly and record 5 seconds; identify every noise they hear and how to remove it.
3. Script slimming: give a 120-word script and ask learners to cut it to 75 words without losing the main idea or the CTA.
4. Storyboard match: give a script and three blank panels; have learners fill the panels so they match exactly.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially written script with the hook done and blanks for the body and CTA; pair the learner
for the recording.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise and assignment as written.
Level 3 (challenge): write two alternative hooks for the same video and record both; choose the stronger one and explain
why. Optionally, write a "bullet-point outline" version and perform it unscripted, then compare the natural delivery to the
scripted take.
Timing guidance. Audio is today's highest-value skill, so protect Demonstration 1 (clear audio vs broken audio) and the
recording part of the practical. If time is short, shorten the knowledge check and reduce the independent exercise to writing the
script and storyboard only (record the voiceover as part of the assignment). Never cut the guided practical — it produces the
script and storyboard that drive Week 2's editing.
