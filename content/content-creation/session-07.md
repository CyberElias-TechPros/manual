# One-Week Calendar and Branding Consistency

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Publish, Measure and Package

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (planning and design, not advanced software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Everything from Sessions 1–6: niche, avatar, pillars and hooks (S1); stable, well-lit shooting (S2); clear audio and a simple script (S3); a first CapCut cut (S4); B-roll, music and a Canva thumbnail (S5); hooks, captions, CTAs and hashtags (S6). A free Canva account must exist. Connection to previous session In Session 6 you wrote the words for one video. But one good post is not a business. Posting "whenever you feel like it" is exactly why most small pages stay invisible. Today you turn your single video and copy into a planned week: a one-week content calendar, a simple batch workflow, and a locked-in brand look (colours, fonts, logo placement, tone) so every post looks and sounds like one professional business.

**Preparation before class:** Print the blank one-week calendar (Worksheet 7.1) and the brand-kit worksheet (Worksheet 7.2), one set per learner. Have the Adaeze Fashion example week on the projector and the Adaeze Canva brand colours on the board. Open Canva with a simple post and a 1280 × 720 thumbnail ready. Confirm every learner has their Session 6 video, thumbnail and captions. Whiteboard and markers ready; Wi-Fi on for Canva. Assessment link This session delivers LO9 (plan and publish consistently). It is assessed through the Session 7 practical, Assignment 7 (build a one-week calendar), and the capstone — specifically Criterion 8 "One-week content calendar" (2 marks) and Criterion 7 "Graphics — 2 posts + 1 thumbnail" (5 marks). Checkpoint (end of S7): every learner must have a working calendar draft, Video 1 finished and one thumbnail.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why a planned content calendar beats random posting, in their own words.
2. Build a realistic one-week content calendar with all seven columns filled: date, platform, format, pillar, hook, caption and
status.
3. Choose a varied content-pillar mix for the week (educate / promote / entertain / proof), avoiding an all-promo page, and
include at least one lighter or rest day.
4. Explain and apply a batch workflow — shooting several clips in one session, editing in another, writing captions in
another — and say why it saves time.
5. Define a simple brand look — one primary colour and one secondary colour (with hex codes), one or two fonts, a logo
placement rule, and a tone of voice.
6. Assemble a brand kit in a simple, free way (a saved Canva design, or a written brand sheet with hex codes) without a paid
subscription.
7. Check posts for consistency using a short checklist, and correct off-brand colours, fonts, logo placement or tone.
8. Plan a week that is realistic for one person who has a job or business, rather than an impossible "three posts a day"
schedule.
9. Produce a one-week content calendar and a brand kit for their chosen business, ready to feed the capstone.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two small business Instagram grids side by side (printed screenshots or prepared
mock grids).
Grid A ("Uche's Boutique"): every post is a pink sale flyer — same pink, same font, every day — but all of them say "BUY
NOW".
Grid B ("Tega's Boutique"): the posts look like they belong to different businesses — red font on white today, blue on
green tomorrow, a different logo size in each. The content itself is good.
Ask for a show of hands: "Which page looks more professional?" Most pick Grid A (the consistent one). Then say:
"Grid A looks tidy — but every post is shouting 'BUY!', so it tires fast. Grid B has variety but looks like five different shops
on one page. The perfect page is the middle path: one clear look on every post and a mix of content that is not all selling.
Today you build both — a one-week plan that varies your content, and a brand look that stays the same."
Expected learner response: Learners will point out the mismatched colours and fonts in Grid B and the repetition in Grid A.
Accept all answers and draw out the two ideas: consistency (look the same) and variety (say different things).
Then reveal the hook. Ask, "How many posts would you need to keep this up for one week?" Then show Adaeze's completed
week and say:
"Seven posts. Not thirty. Not zero. A real plan, made in one sitting. By the end of today, each of you will have a week like
this — and a brand kit so your whole page looks like one business, not seven."
Why this opener works: It creates the exact tension of the session — consistency versus variety — before any explanation,
and shows the finished deliverable immediately, so learners know what they are building.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the three parts of your Session 3 script, and what does each do?"
Expected answer: Hook (stops attention), Value (gives the reason/benefit), CTA (tells the viewer what to do). The
calendar schedules these same parts across the week; the structure does not change, it is just planned ahead.
Question 2: "Name three parts of a strong caption, and say how many hashtags a beginner should use."
Expected answer: hook line, value, CTA (plus hashtags); 3–10 relevant hashtags, not 30. Today's calendar will hold these
captions, so they must already be right.
Question 3: "You made one finished video and one thumbnail. Is that enough to grow a page?"
Expected answer: No. One post is not a plan; reach grows from showing up regularly and consistently, not from one
perfect post.
Question 4: "What are the four content pillars, and give one example of each for Adaeze Fashion."
Expected answer: educate (a care tip), promote (a new-arrival offer), entertain (a light skit), proof (a customer
testimonial). Forgetting that not every post should sell is exactly the mistake today's pillar mix fixes.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your video is the meal, your caption is the salesman, your thumbnail is the signboard. But a restaurant that opens only
when the cook feels like it still starves. Today you become the restaurant manager: you plan the menu for the whole week,
decide which days to serve what, and make sure every plate comes out looking like it came from the same kitchen. That is a
calendar and a brand."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the four pillars, spend 4 extra minutes listing them on the board before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why a calendar beats random posting
The idea. A content calendar is a simple plan that shows what you will post, on which day, in what format, about what, with
which hook and caption, and whether it is done yet. It turns "posting" from a daily panic into a settled decision made once a
week.
Plain-English definition. A calendar is your posting timetable. Instead of waking up each morning asking "what do I post
today?", you open your plan and execute. The thinking is already finished.
Why it exists. Random posting fails for three predictable reasons:
1. Inconsistency. You post three days in a row, then disappear for two weeks. The audience never learns to expect you, so they
stop looking.
2. Decision fatigue. Making a fresh decision every day is tiring, so you either skip posting or post low-effort content.
3. Imbalance. Without a plan, you post whatever mood you are in — usually all sales posts, which wear the audience out.
A calendar removes all three problems at once. The plan decides; you simply do.
What a calendar does for a small business:
Keeps you consistent — you post on set days, not on mood.
Balances your content — you can see, at a glance, that you are not selling every day.
Saves mental energy — one planning sitting replaces a week of daily stress.
Lets you batch — because you know the whole week, you can shoot, edit and write in blocks (see F.4).
Shows progress — the "status" column lets you see what is done, edited, shot or still an idea.
Worked example. Two Port Harcourt tailors with the same skill: Tailor A posts by ear — five days, then ten days' silence,
almost all "come and sew" adverts, and his follower count barely moves. Tailor B posts every Monday, Wednesday and Friday,
teaches, shows work, promotes and shares customer proof, and grows steadily. Same tailoring skill. The difference is the plan.
Common misconception. "Planning makes me rigid and boring." The opposite is true. Once the week is planned, you are free
to be spontaneous on top of it — a plan is a floor, not a ceiling.
Limitation. A calendar is only useful if it is realistic. A beautiful plan that you cannot execute is worse than no plan, because it
teaches you that planning fails. Section F.7 fixes this.

F.3 The content-pillar mix — variety that still sells
The idea. Your content pillars are the two to four themes you rotate. The pillar mix is the balance of those themes across the
week. A good mix teaches, promotes, entertains and proves — it does not only sell.
Why the mix matters. Imagine walking into a shop where the shopkeeper shouts "BUY! BUY! BUY!" the moment you enter,
every time. You would leave. An all-promo page does the same to followers: they feel used and unfollow. Content that gives
(teaches, entertains, proves) earns the right to ask (promote).

The rule of thumb for beginners: for every one promote post, aim for at least one or two posts that give value (educate,

actually land.
Common misconception. "If I do not sell every day, I waste a post." No — a post that teaches or entertains keeps followers
interested enough to see the posts that do sell. Value posts are not wasted; they are the reason the promo posts work.
Limitation. Pillars should not be so rigid that you miss a golden moment (a viral trend, a festival). Pillars guide the balance; they
do not forbid an opportunistic post.
F.4 The batch workflow — how to make a week without burning out
The idea. A batch workflow means you group similar tasks together instead of doing a little of each every day. Instead of
shooting, editing and writing captions all in one exhausting day, you shoot on one day, edit on another, and write captions on
another.
Why it exists. Switching between different kinds of work is slow. Every time you change task — from shooting to editing to
writing — your brain has to "warm up" again. Batching removes those warm-ups and the constant switching, so the same
amount of work takes far less time.
Why one shoot day works so well. Setting up light, a tripod and a clean background is most of the effort. If you set up once
and shoot five clips, you pay that setup cost once instead of five times. The same clothes, the same light, the same background
also make the videos look consistent — which feeds straight into branding (F.5).
A worked Adaeze batch plan:
Day Batch task Time Output
Sunday evening Plan the week 45 min Calendar with all seven rows filled
Monday evening Shoot 60 min 4 video clips + 6 photos for the week
Tuesday evening Edit 90 min 3–4 finished videos (captions, B-roll, music)
Wednesday evening Write 45 min All captions for the week
Thursday evening Design 60 min 2 posts + 1 thumbnail in Canva
Then Publish a few min/day Post on the set days
That is about 5 hours spread across a week — realistic for someone with a job, and far better than a panic on each posting
day.
Common misconception. "Batching means I must spend one whole day on content." No — you can batch across evenings, as
the example shows; the point is grouping, not a marathon. Not every business can shoot a week ahead (a restaurant's "today's
special" changes daily): batch everything that can be pre-made, and leave only the daily item to a quick phone shot.
F.5 Branding consistency — the brand kit
The idea. Your brand is how your business looks, sounds and feels everywhere — the same colours, the same fonts, the same
logo position, and the same tone of voice. A brand kit is a short list of these decisions, written down so you reuse them every
time.
Why branding exists. People recognise things by their look long before they read a word. You recognise a Coca-Cola bottle, an
MTN yellow, a particular shop's signboard. If every one of your posts uses different colours and fonts, no one ever learns to
recognise you — you look like a different business each day. Consistency builds recognition; recognition builds trust; trust
builds customers.
The key parts of a beginner brand kit:
1. Primary colour — the main colour of your brand (with its hex code, e.g., #7B1E3A , a deep burgundy). This is the colour
people will learn to associate with you.
2. Secondary colour — a supporting colour (e.g., cream #F5EFE0 ) used for backgrounds or text, so the primary does not
overwhelm.
3. An accent colour (optional) — a small pop of colour for buttons or highlights (e.g., gold #D4A017 ).
4. Heading font — the font for big words and titles (e.g., Playfair Display, a font available free in Canva).
5. Body font — the font for smaller text (e.g., Montserrat, also free in Canva). Beginners should use one or two fonts only.
6. Logo placement — one rule for where your logo or business name always sits (e.g., "bottom-centre on every post, top-left
on stories").
7. Tone of voice — a short note on how you speak (e.g., "warm, encouraging, honest, simple English; speaks to young
working women as 'you'; never exaggerates").
Hex codes in plain English. A hex code is a six-character label for an exact colour ( #7B1E3A ). Using the exact code — instead of
"that reddish colour" — guarantees the colour is identical on every post, on every device. You can find a colour's hex code in
Canva by clicking the colour swatch.

Now every Adaeze post — a Reel caption, a Canva graphic, a WhatsApp Status — uses burgundy and cream, Playfair Display
for headings and Montserrat for text, and the same friendly tone. The page looks like one business. A brand kit is like a school
uniform: the same colours and badge, so anyone can tell in a crowd which school (or business) someone belongs to.
How to keep a free brand kit (no paid subscription). Canva's saved "Brand Kit" is a paid Pro feature. On the free plan you stay
consistent by writing your hex codes and font names on Worksheet 7.2 and reusing them, or by keeping one saved Canva
design and duplicating it for every new post.
Common misconception. "A brand needs a fancy logo and a designer." No — for a beginner, a consistent name in a
consistent colour and font is already a brand. Branding is consistency, not sameness: every post can be about something
different and still be on-brand.
F.6 Checking posts for consistency
The idea. Before you post, you run a consistency check: a quick scan to confirm that this post looks and sounds like the rest of
your page.
Why it matters. Consistency is easy in a plan and easy to break at the moment of posting, especially under time pressure. A 30-
second check before each post protects the whole page.
The six-point brand consistency check (Worksheet 7.3):
1. Colours — Are the colours exactly my brand colours (same hex codes)?
2. Fonts — Did I use only my chosen heading and body fonts?
3. Logo — Is my logo/business name in the same place as always?
4. Tone — Does the caption sound like me (same warmth, same honesty, same simple English)?
5. Readability — Is the text big, short and readable at small size?
6. Honesty — Do the words and image match the actual video/product?
If all six are "yes", the post is on-brand.
Common misconception. "Consistency means every post must look identical." No — the system is identical (colours, fonts,
logo, tone); the content varies. Same uniform, different students. Consistency is a habit, not a one-time fix: it only holds if you
run the check every time.
F.7 Realistic planning for one person with a real job
The idea. A plan that ignores your real life will fail. Realistic planning means building a week you can actually complete as one
person — with a job, a family and limited time.
Why it exists. The biggest reason small businesses stop posting is not laziness — it is that they planned something impossible.
"Three posts a day, every day" is a plan for a team of six, not for one tailor with a shop to run. When the plan collapses, the
owner blames themselves and gives up.
How to plan realistically:
Start smaller than you think. If you are new, plan 3 posts a week (e.g., Monday, Wednesday, Friday), not 15.
Choose platforms you can maintain. One or two — not four. Every extra platform doubles the work.
Use a batch plan (F.4) so the week fits into a few evenings.
Include a lighter or rest day — one easy Status post, or a genuine day off.
Reuse what you have and match the plan to your free time (if evenings are yours, batch in the evenings).

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Obi runs a small barbershop in Port Harcourt. He cuts well and his shop is neat, but for two years he posted
"whenever he remembered". His page was a jumble — a red poster one day, blue the next, sometimes a random photo with no
logo, sometimes "Back soon, come and cut". He had about 150 followers and almost no bookings from the page; all his
customers came by word of mouth. His neighbour Jide posts every Monday, Wednesday and Friday, always with the same
look — navy background, white bold text, shop name always at the bottom — and a varied plan: one before/after cut, one tip,
one happy-customer post each week. Jide grew to 1,200 local followers in a year and now gets bookings almost every day from
people who found him online.
Relevant facts.
Obi and Jide have similar cutting skill, similar prices, and neither pays for ads.
Obi posted more often on some weeks than Jide — but irregularly, and every post looked different.
Obi's content was almost all selling ("come and cut"); Jide's was balanced (proof, tip, and a gentle promo).
Jide did his week in one batch each Sunday evening; Obi did each post separately and often gave up.
Decision point. What should Obi change — his barbering, his camera, his posting plan, or his brand look?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a better phone and a light, hoping better pictures fix the problem.
2. Copy Jide's exact colours and captions word for word.
3. Build his own realistic three-day-a-week calendar with a balanced pillar mix, and lock down his own simple brand kit (one
colour, one font, one logo position, one tone) — then batch the work on one evening a week.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 3. Obi's barbering and prices are already fine; his camera is adequate. The
real problems are inconsistency, an all-promo page, and no recognisable look. A simple, balanced plan plus a consistent brand
fixes all three for free.
Reasoning / example plan for Obi.
Brand kit: primary black #111111 , secondary white #FFFFFF , one bold heading font, shop name always bottom-centre,
tone "sharp, friendly, confidence-building".
Weekly calendar (3 posts): Monday — proof (before/after cut); Wednesday — educate ("How to describe the cut you
want"); Friday — promote ("Fresh cut for the weekend, ₦X").
Batch plan: shoot and edit all three on Sunday evening; write captions in the same sitting.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 costs money and time but does not fix inconsistency — a better camera on a mismatched page still looks
scattered.
Approach 2 is dishonest copying and will not fit Obi's shop or voice.
Approach 3 is free, quick to start, and directly fixes the three real problems. It also repeats forever with almost no new effort.
Professional connection. Social media managers are paid specifically to build calendars and brand kits for businesses like Obi's.
Planning a balanced, consistent week is one of the most hireable skills in this course — exactly what the capstone's calendar
and graphics criteria measure.

## N. Reflection and Recap
