# Customer Service and Communication

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 4 — Module 4: Professional Practice

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (people skills, role-play heavy)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–9 — the diagnostic workflow, all hardware and software repair skills, and OS installation. Learners can now fix machines; today they learn to serve the people who own them.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 you learned the five-step diagnostic workflow, which begins with asking the customer good questions. Today you turn that single step into a full professional skill: greeting, listening, capturing the fault accurately, explaining plainly, quoting honestly and handling difficult situations.

**Preparation before class:** Print role-play scenario cards (one set per pair); print Customer Intake Forms (one per learner); arrange the room so pairs can sit face to face; set up a "shop counter" table for realistic role play; have two example estimates ready (one good, one bad) to display. Professional note This session has no dangerous hardware steps, but it carries a confidentiality duty: customer data and private information must never be shared, gossiped about or copied. Treat every role play as though it were a real customer.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Greet a customer professionally and create a calm, trustworthy first impression at a repair shop counter.
2. Capture a fault accurately in the customer's own words, using the Customer Intake Form, without leading or interrupting.
3. Ask good diagnostic questions (what, when, how, what changed) that move a vague complaint toward a real fault.
4. Explain faults and fixes in plain language, avoiding jargon or translating it into everyday Nigerian English.
5. Manage expectations about time, cost and uncertainty honestly, including saying "I don't know yet" professionally.
6. Quote a repair and obtain written approval before starting any billable work.
7. Handle an unhappy or angry customer calmly using listen → empathise → solve → boundary.
8. Protect customer confidentiality, in the shop and outside it.
9. Build trust and repeat business through honesty, follow-up and consistent service.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Write two sentences on the whiteboard, side by side.
Sentence A: "The motherboard's SATA controller is failing, causing the HDD to be undetectable by the BIOS, so the POST
sequence returns a no-bootable-device error."
Sentence B: "The part inside your computer that connects your hard drive is faulty, so the computer cannot find your files. We
need to replace it."
What the instructor says:
"Read both. They mean the same thing. Which one would you say to a market woman who brought in her shop computer?
… Exactly. Sentence B.
Here is the hard truth about this trade: you can be the best technician in Port Harcourt, but if you talk to customers in
Sentence A, you will have no customers. Nobody pays a person who makes them feel stupid.
Yesterday you learned to fix the machine. Today you learn to fix the relationship. You will learn how to greet a customer,
take down their complaint in their own words, explain what is wrong in plain language, give an honest price, and stay
calm when someone is shouting at you.
This is the skill that turns a one-time repair into a customer who comes back for years — and tells their friends about you."
Expected learner response: Learners will find Sentence B easier and may laugh at Sentence A. Use the moment to name the
skill: translation.
Then demonstrate the hook: Ask a volunteer to act as a frustrated customer who says only: "My computer is doing me
somehow." The instructor (as technician) asks three good questions and shows how a vague complaint can be turned into useful
information within one minute. Say:
"Notice what I did: I did not guess, and I did not talk down to her. I asked good questions and let her talk. That is where
every good repair begins."
Why this opener works: It immediately shows the gap between technical language and customer language, and it proves that
good questioning yields real information — the exact skills of today.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five steps of the diagnostic workflow?"
Expected answer: observe, ask questions, form a hypothesis, test, resolve/verify.
Common gap: learners remember "test" and "fix" but forget "ask questions". Emphasise that questions to the customer
are step two and often solve the case before you open the case.
Question 2: "Why is it important to ask the customer what they were doing when the fault started?"
Expected answer: it gives clues to the cause (for example a fault after a power cut, a drop, a spill, or an update).
Common gap: learners think they can find everything from the machine alone. Clarify that customer context is free
evidence.
Question 3: "What should you always do before starting a billable repair?"
Expected answer: get the customer's approval of the cost and scope (an estimate).
Common gap: learners skip straight to fixing. Reinforce that surprise bills destroy trust.
Question 4: "In Session 9, why did we say data safety comes first?"
Expected answer: a clean install or wipe destroys data, so we back up and confirm with the customer first.
Common gap: learners forget to tell the customer what will happen. Today's confidentiality and consent theme builds on
this.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Look how much of today is already in what you know. The diagnostic workflow starts with asking. OS installation taught
you to get the customer's consent before destroying anything. Today we simply turn these into a professional habit —
because a repair shop lives or dies on how it treats people."
Gap handling: If more than a third of the class cannot name the intake/approval idea in Question 3, pause and act out a 60-
second "surprise bill" scene: technician fixes without quoting, then presents ₦60,000, customer is furious. Ask the class what
went wrong. Then proceed. This makes the rest of the session land harder.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why customer service is a technical skill
The idea. Customer service is the set of behaviours that make a customer feel respected, informed and confident — before,
during and after a repair.
Plain-English definition. Customer service is how you treat the person while you do the job. It is greeting them, listening,
explaining, quoting honestly, keeping your word and being reachable.
Why it exists. A customer cannot see the skill in your hands. They cannot tell if your soldering is neat or your diagnosis is sharp.
What they can judge is how you treated them. That judgement decides whether they come back, whether they recommend
you, and whether they trust your price.
What it does.
Makes a frightened customer feel safe (their data, their money).
Prevents disputes by keeping everyone informed and getting approval.
Builds a reputation that brings repeat and referred business.
Reduces your own stress — a calm, informed customer is far easier to deal with.
Everyday analogy. Think of a good barber. He greets you, remembers your name, checks what style you want before he cuts,
tells you the price, and does not start until you nod. He might cut hair no better than the next man, but you keep going back
because he makes you feel attended to. A repair shop is the same.
Realistic scenario (Port Harcourt). Two technicians in Rumuola both fix a laptop for ₦15,000. One greets, explains and follows
up. The other grunts, snatches the machine and ignores the customer. Within a year, the first has a queue and a WhatsApp list
of loyal customers; the second wonders why business is slow.
Common misconception. "Customer service is only for receptionists or big companies." Wrong. In a small Nigerian repair shop,
the technician is the business. Your people skills are part of your product.
Limitation / honesty. Good service cannot turn a bad repair into a good one. Service and skill must go together. This session
assumes you are also doing the technical work well.
F.2 The first impression — greeting and setting the tone
The idea. The first thirty seconds set the mood of the whole transaction.
What good looks like.
Stop what you are doing and turn to face the customer (or politely say "one moment, sir/ma").
Greet appropriately: "Good afternoon, welcome. How can I help you today?"
Introduce yourself: your name, and the shop's name if they are new.
Smile and use a calm voice. Do not look distracted or annoyed.
Offer a seat if they must wait, and be honest about how long.
Never start with the price or with a sigh.
Analogy. Imagine you walk into a church office and the secretary does not look up. You feel unwelcome before anyone speaks.
Then imagine she stands, greets you by name and offers you a chair. Same information — completely different feeling.
Greeting is that powerful.
Realistic scenario. A school principal's laptop is failing on the day of a PTA meeting. She is already stressed. A warm "Good
afternoon, ma. Sit down, let me attend to you now" lowers her panic instantly and makes her a patient, cooperative customer.
Common misconception. "Nigerians don't do small talk; just get to business." Respectful greeting is not small talk — in most
Nigerian businesses it is the expected opening. Skipping it can read as rudeness.
F.3 Capturing the fault accurately — in the customer's own words
The idea. Before you diagnose, you record exactly what the customer experiences, in their words, not your technical guess.
Why their words matter. The customer's exact description often contains the clue to the fault. If you "translate" too early, you
may lead yourself astray. Example: a customer says "it dey show blue screen and restart" — that is different from "it won't
come on at all". Both could be described as "not working", but they point to completely different faults.
How to capture it well.
1. Let them speak first without interruption.
2. Write the complaint as close to their wording as possible on the intake form.
3. Use their phrases in quotes: "It makes a rattling noise like something loose."
4. Then ask your structured questions (see F.4).
5. Read it back: "So what I understand is…, is that correct?"
Useful intake questions (the "good questions" list).
What exactly happens? What do you see on the screen?
When did it start? Was it sudden or gradual?
What were you doing when it started?
Has anything changed recently — a drop, a spill, a power cut, an update, new software?
Does the problem happen every time, or sometimes?
Does anyone else use the machine?
Is there important data on it that must be saved? (Always ask this.)
An analogy. You are a detective taking a witness statement. You write what the witness saw, not what you think happened.
The conclusion comes later, after evidence.
Realistic scenario. A church secretary says, "The computer is slow." A careless technician hears "slow" and assumes a virus.
Good questioning reveals: "It became slow only after the generator was changed; now it sometimes beeps and won't start." That
points toward RAM or power, not a virus. The customer's own words saved hours of wrong work.
Common misconception. "The customer does not know anything; just look at the machine." The customer may not know the
cause, but they know the symptoms and timing better than anyone. Use them.
Limitation. Customers can be mistaken or vague. Capture their words accurately but verify with tests before you commit to a
diagnosis.
F.4 Explaining faults and fixes in plain language
The idea. You translate technical findings into words the customer can understand and act on.
The rules of plain language.
1. No unnecessary jargon. If you must use a technical word, explain it once and simply.
2. Use analogies to everyday things (this is the most powerful tool).
3. Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
4. Check understanding: "Does that make sense?" or "Should I explain that part again?"
5. Be honest and specific, not vague or scary.
The translation table.
Technical term Plain English
RAM The computer's short-term memory; it forgets when you switch off
Hard drive / HDD / SSD Where your files are stored permanently
Motherboard The main board that connects all the parts
CPU The computer's brain
PSU The power supply — it feeds electricity to the parts
GPU / graphics card The part that draws what you see on the screen
Driver A small program that lets Windows talk to a piece of hardware
Operating system / Windows The main software that runs the whole computer
Malware / virus Bad software that harms or slows the computer
Reinstall Wipe the system clean and install it again
Boot To start up
Partition A section of the hard drive
Corrupted Damaged / spoiled
Diagnostics Tests we run to find the cause
Analogy bank (use these freely).
RAM is a tabletop: small, fast, but cleared at the end of the day. The hard drive is a filing cabinet: bigger, slower, but keeps
things forever.
The CPU is the brain; RAM is the short-term memory; the hard drive is the long-term memory or diary.
The PSU is the heart that pumps electricity; if it weakens, everything else misbehaves.
A driver is an interpreter between Windows and a device that speaks another language.
Overheating is like a person working in a sealed room with no air — performance drops and eventually they collapse.
Realistic scenario. A trader asks, "Wetin dey worry am?" The technician says: "Madam, the memory that the computer uses to
run your programs has gone bad. That is why it keeps restarting. We will change it — the same part, small cost — and it will
work well again." Clear, honest, calm.
Common misconception. "Using big words makes me look professional." The opposite is true. Big words make customers feel
confused and suspicious, and they hide behind them when they do not understand.
Limitation. Some findings genuinely are complicated (for example data recovery from a physically failing drive). When the truth
is complex, be honest about the uncertainty rather than pretending certainty.
F.5 Managing expectations — time, cost and uncertainty
The idea. Tell the customer the truth about what you know, what you do not know, and what it will cost and take — before,
not after.
The three expectations to manage.
1. Time. "Diagnosis will take a few hours / one day." Give a realistic window, not an optimistic lie. It is far better to say
"tomorrow afternoon" and deliver at midday than to promise "in one hour" and disappoint.
2. Cost. Explain the difference between the diagnosis fee and the repair cost, and give a range when the fault is not yet
confirmed.
3. Uncertainty. It is professional to say: "I cannot give the exact price until I diagnose it. I will test it, then call you with a written
estimate before we start any work. Nothing will be done without your approval."
Why honesty about uncertainty matters. A technician who says "I am not sure yet, let me test it" is more trustworthy than one
who guesses a price on the spot. Customers respect honesty; they resent surprises.
Realistic scenario. A customer's PC does not power on. It could be a ₦2,000 loose cable or a ₦25,000 failed PSU. A good
technician says: "The test will tell us. It might be small, it might be the power supply. I will know by tomorrow and call you
before I spend any money."
Common misconception. "If I say I'm not sure, they will go to another shop." In reality, most customers prefer a careful
technician over a confident guesser. Honesty wins the job.
Limitation. Occasionally a fault changes during repair (a second problem reveals itself). If that happens, stop and contact the
customer before spending more of their money. Never silently increase a bill.
F.6 Quoting and getting approval before work
The idea. Before you do billable work, you give the customer a clear estimate and get their agreement — ideally in writing (or
a WhatsApp message they reply to).
What an estimate should contain.
Diagnosis fee (if you charge one).
Parts — the replacement component(s), with a price each.
Labour — your time.
Total — the figure the customer will pay.
Timeline — when it will be ready.
Warranty — what is covered and for how long.
Approval line — where the customer agrees (signature, or "Yes, proceed" on WhatsApp).
Estimates in the Nigerian shop context.
Written or WhatsApp estimates are best because both sides keep a copy. WhatsApp is widely used and perfectly
professional when worded clearly.
Give a range when unsure, and state clearly what would push it to the higher end.
State whether the diagnosis fee is waived if they proceed with the repair.
Get approval before starting. "Nothing will be done without your approval" protects both customer and technician.
Analogy. A good tailor measures you and tells you the price and when to collect before cutting the cloth. Imagine a tailor who
sews first and demands any price later — you would never return.
Realistic scenario. A customer approves ₦18,000 for a PSU replacement. Mid-repair the technician finds the PSU failure also
damaged the motherboard. The professional stops, calls the customer, explains plainly, gives a new estimate, and only
continues if approved.
Common misconception. "Verbal is fine; we trust each other." Verbal agreements are easily disputed. A written or WhatsApp
estimate with a clear "Yes, go ahead" protects the technician if a customer later claims they never agreed.
Limitation. You cannot always know the full cost upfront. That is why estimates can be revised — but only with fresh
customer approval.
F.7 Handling unhappy or angry customers
The idea. An upset customer is not your enemy. They are a person with a problem, and your calm professionalism can turn
them into a loyal customer.
The four-step method.
1. Listen. Let them finish. Do not interrupt, do not defend, do not argue. Take a breath.
2. Empathise and acknowledge. "I understand this is frustrating." "I can see why you are upset." This costs nothing and
lowers the temperature fast.
3. Solve. Focus on what can be done: "Let me see what I can do to help." Offer realistic options.
4. Set boundaries. Be firm but polite. If a customer becomes abusive, you may calmly end the conversation: "Sir, I want to
help you, but please do not shout at me. Let us talk calmly." You are allowed to protect your dignity and that of your staff.
Escalate to a manager if needed.
What NOT to do.
Do not match their emotion or raise your voice.
Do not blame them ("You caused this").
Do not lie to make them happy — a lie today becomes a bigger fight tomorrow.
Do not promise what you cannot deliver.
The "broken promise" moment. Sometimes the fault is genuinely your shop's (you scratched the casing, or a repair failed).
The professional response is to own it honestly, apologise, and fix it at your own cost where reasonable. This builds more trust
than any smooth talk. Hiding a mistake and hoping the customer will not notice is how shops lose their name.
Realistic scenario. A customer shouts because a repair is taking three days. The technician listens, says "I understand, sir; the
part had to be ordered from Lagos and I should have told you," explains what is happening today, gives a firm pickup time, and
follows up. The customer calms down and returns next time.
Common misconception. "An angry customer is always wrong." Often the customer is simply scared — about their data, their
money or their image. Address the fear and the anger usually drops.
Limitation. Some situations cannot be resolved to full satisfaction. In those rare cases, be honest, offer options, and if needed let
them take the matter to the owner or a fair third party. Never let a dispute turn into a fight.
F.8 Confidentiality of customer data
The idea. Anything on a customer's device is private and must be treated as a secret entrusted to you.
What confidentiality means in practice.
Do not open, read or copy the customer's files except as needed for the repair.
Do not take photos of their documents, photos or messages.
Do not gossip about what you found on a customer's machine.
Do not leave customer data on a shared or personal drive after the job.
Do not use a customer's data, accounts or files for any personal purpose.
Store intake forms and job records securely.
Why it matters.
It is ethical. The customer trusted you with their private life.
It is professional. Reputation spreads fast, especially in a close city like Port Harcourt.
In serious cases, misuse of a customer's data can be a legal matter, not just a moral one.
Analogy. A customer's device is like a sealed envelope handed to you to deliver. You carry it, you do not open it, and you do
not tell the neighbourhood what was inside.
Common misconception. "If they left it on the machine, it's not private." It is still private. They did not give you permission to
browse it.
F.9 Building trust and repeat business
The idea. The goal of every job is not just a fixed computer — it is a customer who trusts you and returns.
How trust is built.
Keep your word on time and price.
Be reachable. Answer calls and WhatsApp messages, even to say "still working on it".
Follow up. A short "How is the computer working now?" a few days later is powerful and costs nothing.
Give honest recommendations — including "you do not need to replace this yet".
Be transparent about what you did (this connects to the service report in Session 11).
Thank them, and invite them back.
Realistic scenario. After replacing a laptop screen, a technician sends a WhatsApp: "Good evening, ma. Hope the screen is fine?
Please let me know if anything comes up." The customer replies, then tells two friends. That one message becomes three jobs.
Common misconception. "Once they pay and leave, the job is finished." The relationship, not the transaction, is the real
business.
Limitation. Trust is slow to build and fast to lose. One dishonest act can undo years of good service. Protect your name above a
single sale.
F.10 Summary of the session
Customer service is a technical skill in this trade — it wins and keeps business.
Greet warmly; listen and capture the fault in the customer's own words.
Ask good questions (what, when, how, what changed, important data?).
Explain plainly — no jargon, use analogies, check understanding.
Manage expectations honestly about time, cost and uncertainty.
Quote and get approval before work; revise only with fresh approval.
Handle anger with listen → empathise → solve → boundary.
Protect confidentiality; build trust with honesty and follow-up.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
What the instructor prepares:
The "shop counter" table and two chairs.
Scenario card: "My laptop will not charge and the battery was full this morning. I need it for work tomorrow."
A printed Customer Intake Form on the projector or whiteboard.
The good and bad estimate examples.
G.1 Demonstration 1 — The intake, done well (6 minutes)
Say: "Watch everything I do, and listen to how I speak as much as what I say."
The instructor acts as the technician. A volunteer plays the customer with the scenario above.
1. Greet: "Good afternoon, welcome. My name is Elias. Please sit down. How can I help you today?"
2. Let them speak without interrupting. Write their exact words on the form: "My laptop will not charge and the battery was
full this morning."
3. Ask good questions: When did it start? What changed? Do you use the original charger? Does the charging light come on?
Is there important data on it?
4. Read back: "So the laptop works on battery but will not charge since this morning, and you have work due tomorrow. Have
I understood correctly?"
5. Set expectations: "I will test the charger and the charging port first — that is the small possibility — then the battery.
Diagnosis will take about one hour. I will call you with a written estimate before any repair."
6. Mention data safety: "Is anything on it you cannot afford to lose? Good — I will not wipe anything without asking you."
Then say: "Did you notice I asked before I concluded? Did you notice I quoted a process, not a fake price? That is the whole
skill."
G.2 Demonstration 2 — Explaining the fault plainly (4 minutes)
Say: "Now I have a finding. Watch me translate it."
Technical truth: "Battery cell degradation; the battery no longer accepts charge."
Plain version: "Ma, your laptop's battery has reached the end of its life. It is like a phone battery that no longer holds charge.
The charger is fine. We can replace just the battery and it will hold charge again."
Check understanding: "Does that make sense? Any questions?"
Ask the class: "What did I do to make that clear?" → Used an everyday analogy (phone battery), avoided the word
'degradation', checked understanding, and told her what would happen next.
G.3 Demonstration 3 — The angry customer (3 minutes)
The instructor plays a customer who is angry that a repair is late. Narrate the four steps as you do them:
1. Listen — let them finish, no interrupting.
2. Empathise — "I understand, sir. This has delayed you and I am sorry."
3. Solve — "Here is exactly where we are and what I can do today."
4. Boundary — if shouting continues: "I want to help you, but please let us talk calmly."
Expected result: The "customer" visibly calms.
Error to watch: If the learner disagrees, defends or raises their voice, stop the scene and reset. Point out that arguing always
makes it worse.
G.4 Demonstration 4 — Good vs bad estimate (2 minutes)
Show the two examples on screen.
Bad: "Laptop repair — maybe ₦20k, come back Friday." (vague, no breakdown, no approval).
Good: a clear table with diagnosis fee, parts, labour, total, timeline, warranty and an approval line.
Ask: "Which protects the customer? Which protects you?" → Both. A clear estimate is protection for everyone.
Questions to ask throughout the demo:
"Why did I write the customer's exact words?" → Their words contain clues and show I listened.
"Why did I say 'I don't know yet' about the price?" → Honesty about uncertainty builds trust and prevents surprises.
"Why is a WhatsApp 'Yes, proceed' useful?" → It is written proof of approval.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: In pairs, learners role-play a full customer intake and a plain-language fault explanation, using a real scenario and
the Customer Intake Form.
Confidentiality reminder before starting: everything said in role-play stays in the room. When a real customer is
involved, their data and details are private.
Setup: Pairs sit face to face. One is the technician, one is the customer. The instructor hands each pair a scenario card. The
technican holds the Intake Form; the customer holds the card.
Procedure (instructor calls each numbered step; pairs follow; instructor walks the room and listens):
1. Decide who is technician and who is customer. The customer reads their scenario card silently.
2. Greet the customer warmly and offer a seat. (Instructor watches for warmth and eye contact.)
3. Let the customer speak first. The technician writes the fault in the customer's own words on the form.
4. Ask at least four good questions from the list (what, when, how, what changed, important data).
5. Pause and predict: before writing a diagnosis, the technician says out loud what the likely area of fault is (for example
RAM, storage, power, software) and why.
6. Read the intake back to the customer and ask, "Have I understood correctly?"
7. Explain the likely fault in plain language, using at least one analogy. Check understanding.
8. Set expectations: time, a note that a written estimate will follow, and that nothing is done without approval.
9. Offer an estimate using the template (diagnosis fee, parts range, labour, total, timeline, warranty, approval line). Get a
verbal or written approval.
10. Switch roles and repeat with a different scenario card.
11. Give each other feedback using the three questions below.
Expected result: A completed Intake Form that accurately captures the fault in the customer's words, a plain-language
explanation the "customer" understood, and an approved estimate.
Scenario cards (use and reuse):
Card 1. "My computer is very slow and takes forever to start. I have a presentation tomorrow."
Card 2. "My computer is making a loud noise and I am scared it will lose my photos."
Card 3. "I poured water on my laptop and now it will not come on. Please fix it quickly."
Card 4. "A blue screen appears and the computer restarts by itself. I don't know what it means."
Card 5. "The screen is cracked and sometimes goes black. I use it for my POS business."
Card 6. "The computer works but there is no sound at all. I use it for church programmes."
Pause-and-predict checkpoints: after step 3 ("What is the likely fault area?"), after step 7 ("Did the customer understand? Ask
them to repeat it back.").
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Technician interrupts the customer → gently stop the scene; remind them step 3 is "let them finish".
Technician uses jargon ("SATA", "BIOS") → ask them to say the same thing to a market woman.
No approval obtained → pause and show why the estimate and "yes" matter.
Customer is too vague → prompt the technician to ask "What exactly do you see on the screen?"
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Warm greeting given
Fault captured in the customer's own words
At least four good questions asked
Fault area predicted with a reason
Intake read back and confirmed
Plain-language explanation with an analogy, comprehension checked
Expectations set (time, cost, uncertainty)
Estimate offered with an approval line
Both roles played (two scenarios)
Feedback given and received

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chidi runs a small repair corner in a Port Harcourt plaza. He is a good technician but a poor communicator. A
customer brings a laptop that will not start. Chidi takes it, grunts, and says "I will check it." Two days later he calls: "It's your
board; ₦45,000." The customer is shocked — no warning, no explanation, no approval. She collects the machine and takes it
elsewhere. The other shop diagnoses a faulty power button cable and fixes it for ₦6,000. She tells everyone at her office about
Chidi "who wanted to spoil her".
Relevant facts.
Chidi's diagnosis may even have been partly right, but nobody trusts a surprise bill.
The second shop greeted her, explained plainly, gave a written estimate and got approval.
Same fault. Two very different outcomes — decided by customer service, not by hands-on skill.
Decision point. Where did Chidi lose the customer?
Possible approaches.
1. Blame the customer for being "unreasonable".
2. Learn to greet, explain plainly, quote honestly and get approval before work.
3. Keep doing the same but hope for the best.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. If Chidi had (a) greeted her, (b) explained what he suspected in plain language, and (c)
sent a written estimate of ₦45,000 with a clear reason before doing anything, the customer would either have approved it or
declined politely — either way, no damage to his name. He also lost the chance to compete: the second shop found the real,
cheaper fault.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 feels good but changes nothing; the shop stays empty.
Approach 2 takes only minutes per customer but transforms reputation and repeat business.
Approach 3 is the road to closed doors.
Professional connection. In a repair shop, communication is part of the repair. The intake, the plain explanation, the honest
estimate and the approval are not extra chores; they are the product the customer is paying for, alongside the fix.
Transfer question for the class: "Chidi charged ₦45,000; the second shop charged ₦6,000 for the same-looking problem.
What does that teach you about the value of a correct diagnosis plus honest communication?"

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table live. When a learner hits an issue in role-play, point them to the relevant row.
K.1 Using jargon with the customer
Symptoms: Customer looks confused; keeps asking "wetin that mean?" Likely cause: The technician used words like SATA,
BIOS, motherboard, corrupt. Safe corrective steps: Translate to plain English; use an analogy; check understanding.
Prevention: Practise your analogy bank until it is automatic.
K.2 Interrupting or not listening
Symptoms: Customer repeats themselves; technician misses key details; wrong fault area predicted. Likely cause: Rushing to
solve before understanding. Safe corrective steps: Let the customer finish; write their words; read back. Prevention: Treat the
customer as a witness — capture first, conclude later.
K.3 No estimate / no approval
Symptoms: Customer angry at the bill; dispute; loss of trust. Likely cause: Work started without written cost or consent. Safe
corrective steps: Apologise, provide the breakdown, and agree a fair resolution; change your process going forward.
Prevention: Always quote and get approval (signature or a clear "Yes, proceed") before billable work.
K.4 Overpromising time or outcome
Symptoms: Customer returns furious; reputation damaged. Likely cause: Giving an optimistic guess instead of a realistic
window. Safe corrective steps: Own it, apologise, communicate the true status, give a firm new time. Prevention: Under-
promise and over-deliver. Say "I will confirm after diagnosis".
K.5 Matching the customer's anger
Symptoms: The situation escalates into an argument. Likely cause: Taking the anger personally and reacting. Safe corrective
steps: Pause, lower your voice, listen, empathise, set a calm boundary. Prevention: Remember the customer is frustrated with
the situation, not necessarily with you.
K.6 Breaching confidentiality
Symptoms: Customer learns their private files or situation were discussed or copied. Likely cause: Curiosity, gossip, or careless
handling of data and records. Safe corrective steps: Stop immediately, secure the data, and never repeat it; where serious, this
can become a legal matter. Prevention: Treat every device as a sealed envelope; access data only for the repair.
K.7 Hiding a shop mistake
Symptoms: Customer discovers the damage later; trust destroyed. Likely cause: Embarrassment and hope the customer will not
notice. Safe corrective steps: Own it, apologise, fix it at your cost where reasonable. Prevention: Honesty is the cheapest long-
term policy.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: "Likely cause" is your best guess; you confirm it by listening carefully and, when relevant,
checking the customer's facts. Teach learners to ask before assuming.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that a repair shop is a people business. You practised greeting a customer warmly;
capturing their fault in their own words; asking good questions (including the all-important "is there important data?");
explaining faults and fixes in plain language with analogies; managing expectations about time, cost and uncertainty; quoting
and getting approval before work; handling angry customers calmly; protecting confidentiality; and building trust for repeat
business. None of this replaces good technical work — it wraps around it.
Five important takeaways:
1. Greet and listen first; capture the fault in the customer's own words.
2. Explain plainly — no jargon, use analogies, check understanding.
3. Manage expectations honestly; it is fine to say "I don't know yet".
4. Quote and get approval before doing billable work.
5. Stay calm and protect confidentiality — trust is your real business.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I run a full customer intake and fill the form without prompting?
Can I explain a fault to a non-technical customer and have them understand it?
Can I write a clear estimate with an approval line?
Can I stay calm with an angry customer and still solve the problem?
Do I treat every customer's data as private?
Connection to the next session: You have now served the customer well and agreed the work. But a professional shop also
records the work. Next session you learn service reports and documentation: what a proper report must contain, how to write
it clearly and honestly, and how it protects both you and the customer. The report is also the evidence that becomes your Final
Project deliverable.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner greets warmly and naturally, without a frozen script.
The learner lets the customer finish and writes their exact words.
The learner asks focused questions and follows up on vague answers.
The learner explains a fault with an analogy and checks understanding.
The learner is honest about uncertainty and never invents a price.
The learner produces a clear estimate and asks for approval.
The learner stays calm in the angry-customer scene.
The learner treats confidentiality and data safety as non-negotiable.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Customers should understand technical words." → It is your job to translate, not theirs to learn.
"An angry customer is always wrong." → Often they are frightened; address the fear.
"Verbal estimates are fine." → Put it in writing/WhatsApp; it protects everyone.
"If I don't know the price, I should guess." → Honest uncertainty builds more trust than a wrong guess.
"If they left the file on the machine, it is not private." → It is still private and must be protected.
"Customer service is for receptionists." → In a small shop, the technician is the face of the business.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Translation drills: hand out ten technical sentences; ask the learner to say each one to a "market woman" in one or two
simple sentences.
2. Question-building drill: give a one-line complaint; ask the learner to write five good questions and pick the best three.
3. Calm-under-pressure drill: the instructor plays a mildly difficult customer; the learner practises listen → empathise →
solve → boundary. Increase difficulty gradually.
4. Estimate-completion drill: give a partially filled estimate; ask the learner to complete the total, timeline, warranty and
approval line.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a scripted role-play the learner can follow word for word, and a heavily guided intake form;
let them start as the customer and build confidence.
Level 2 (standard): complete both role-plays and the written exercise as written; produce the playbook.
Level 3 (challenge): handle a two-part difficult scenario (angry customer plus a revised estimate mid-repair); or, with
instructor supervision, conduct a real intake with a genuine customer or staff volunteer; write a full script for a price-
objection situation.
Timing guidance (105 min):
Opening 6, prerequisite review 7, lecture 35, demonstration 15, guided role-play 18, independent written exercise 15, case
study folded into reflection 3, reflection/recap + knowledge check 6. If the room is animated in role-play, protect the role-
play and the plain-language practice above all — those are the priority. The case study can be reduced to a two-minute
mention if time is short.
