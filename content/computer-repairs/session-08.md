# Cleaning and Maintenance

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module: Repair and Maintenance

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (hands-on, safety-critical)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–7: components, safety and tools, disassembly/reassembly, the diagnostic workflow, hardware and software diagnostics, and component replacement (including thermal paste and safe handling). If a learner cannot demonstrate the two safety must-dos, run the 10 minute refresher in Appendix 8A before this session.

**Connection to previous work:** Session 7 taught you to replace parts. This session teaches you to prevent faults — keeping a machine cool, clean and protected so it lasts longer and costs the customer less. Cleaning is the cheapest repair of all.

**Preparation before class:** A deliberately dusty practice desktop (or one that can be made dusty for demonstration); canned compressed air; a soft anti-static brush (or a clean, soft paintbrush); isopropyl alcohol (90%+); lint-free/microfiber cloths; cotton buds; thermal paste; a small screwdriver and anti static wrist straps (one per learner); HWMonitor installed; a surge protector and, if available, a UPS to show; an old keyboard and an old monitor/phone screen for safe cleaning practice; printed Cleaning Checklist and Maintenance Plan Template (one per learner). Optional: a copy of a PC cleaning video is not required — keep it live. A note on what cleaning means here. Cleaning a computer is not just making it look nice. It is a maintenance action that removes the main causes of overheating (dust, blocked airflow, dried thermal paste) and protects the machine from environmental risks (humidity, power surges). Done wrong, cleaning can damage a computer with static electricity or liquid; done right, it extends a machine's life by years.

Institution

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain how dust, heat and blocked airflow damage a computer, and how these lead to slowness, shutdowns and part
failure.
2. List the correct cleaning tools and state which tools are unsafe (e.g., ordinary vacuum cleaners).
3. Perform a safe internal clean — powering off, unplugging, using ESD care, and using compressed air, brushes and
isopropyl alcohol correctly.
4. Clean fans, heatsinks, the case interior, keyboard, ports and screens safely and appropriately for each surface.
5. Explain when to re-apply thermal paste and how often, and recognise the signs (rising temperatures, dust-clogged
heatsink).
6. Describe the environmental threats to a computer — dust, humidity, heat and power surges — and how a surge
protector and a UPS help.
7. Create a practical preventive maintenance schedule for a home, a small office and a cyber café, including who does
what and how it is documented.
8. Produce a written maintenance recommendation for a real machine after cleaning and a cooling check.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the bench, place the dusty practice desktop with its side panel removed so the class can see the inside.
Beside it, place a clean, dust-free machine (or a clean fan/heatsink) for contrast. Have HWMonitor ready on the dusty machine
(or a note of its earlier temperature).
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Look inside this computer. What do you see? Dust. A grey blanket of dust sitting on the heatsink, in the fan
blades, in every corner.
Now here is the important part. Dust does not just look bad. That dust blanket is a heat trap. Heat cannot escape. The
CPU gets hot, so the computer slows itself down on purpose — that is why a dusty machine feels slow. If it gets hotter still,
it shuts itself off to save its own life. And year after year, that heat quietly shortens the life of every part inside.
In Nigeria, in Port Harcourt, with our heat and harmattan dust, this is one of the most common reasons a computer dies
young. But here is the good news: cleaning is the cheapest repair there is. No parts to buy. Just proper tools, proper care,
and a habit of doing it regularly.
Today you will learn to clean a computer safely — because done wrongly, cleaning can damage a computer with static or
liquid — and you will learn to build a maintenance schedule so the computer never gets this bad again."
Expected learner response: Learners often say "just blow it with the mouth" or "use a cloth" or "use a vacuum cleaner". Accept
these and note them on the board. Say:
"By the end of today you will know exactly why a vacuum cleaner is dangerous, why blowing with your mouth is a bad
idea (moisture), and what to use instead."
Then demonstrate the hook: on the compressor or with a canned air, give one short, controlled puff across the dusty heatsink
over a bin or outside airflow. The cloud of dust that rises is the moment the lesson lands.
Why this opener works: It shows the real, physical enemy (dust) instead of describing it, it names a very common Nigerian
reality (heat + dust), and it challenges several common bad habits (vacuuming, blowing) with a promise to teach the correct
tool.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "Name the six major components."
Expected answer: Motherboard, CPU, RAM, storage, PSU, GPU.
Common gap: forgetting the PSU. Probe and move on.
Question 2: "What are the two safety must-dos before opening a computer?"
Expected answer: Power off and unplug (press power to discharge); wear an anti-static wrist strap.
Common gap: forgetting the discharge step. Correct it — cleaning involves moving air and cloths, so static and loose
power are real risks.
Question 3: "What is ESD, and why does it matter when cleaning?"
Expected answer: Electrostatic discharge — a tiny static shock from your body or a cloth that can silently damage
components. Cloths and brushes rub and build static, so we must ground ourselves and use anti-static tools.
Common gap: learners think ESD is only a concern when replacing parts. Make clear it is especially relevant to cleaning,
because rubbing a cloth on a case builds static.
Question 4: "When do we re-apply thermal paste, and what amount do we use?"
Expected answer: when the CPU and cooler are separated, or when temperatures rise despite clean fans; use a pea-sized
amount. (From Session 7.)
Question 5: "A computer shuts down after 30 minutes and the case is hot. What are two likely causes?"
Expected answer: overheating (dust, a failed fan, dried thermal paste) and possibly a failing PSU. This bridges directly into
today.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Session 5 taught you to find overheating with tools. Session 7 taught you to replace parts. Today, you learn to prevent the
overheating in the first place. A surgeon can operate; a good doctor keeps you from needing the operation."
Gap handling: If learners cannot state the two safety must-dos or explain ESD (Questions 2–3), stop and run the Appendix 8A
refresher now. Cleaning involves rubbing and air, which build static, so this session cannot proceed safely without it.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why dust and heat destroy computers
The idea. A computer produces heat when it works and must get rid of that heat. Dust blocks the path heat takes to escape.
Block the path, and the heat builds up — and heat is the slow killer of electronics.
Plain-English definitions.
Dust is a mixture of tiny particles — skin, cloth fibres, and in Nigeria especially harmattan dust and road dust. It settles on
every surface inside a computer.
Heat is a by-product of electricity moving through components. It must be carried away by the cooling system (fans,
heatsinks, airflow) and out of the case.
A heatsink is a block of metal fins attached to a hot chip (like the CPU). The metal absorbs heat from the chip, and the fins
give a large surface area for a fan to blow air across and carry the heat away.
Why dust causes damage — the chain of events:
1. Dust settles on fan blades and between heatsink fins.
2. The dust "felt" blocks airflow and insulates the metal, so heat cannot escape.
4. The computer slows itself down on purpose to make less heat (this is called thermal throttling) — so a dusty computer
feels slow.
5. If it gets hotter still, the computer shuts itself off suddenly to protect the chip (a thermal shutdown) — which looks like
random restarts or shutdowns.
6. Over months and years, the constant high heat shortens the life of the chip, the drive and other parts.
Analogy. Imagine doing hard labour in Port Harcourt midday heat while wrapped in a thick blanket. You cannot work well, you
feel weak, and if you keep going you collapse. The dust blanket does exactly this to a computer.
Realistic Port Harcourt scenario. A cyber café near Mile 1 has machines that keep shutting down during busy evenings. The
café owner thinks the machines are "old and spoiled". A technician opens one and finds the CPU heatsink completely packed
with dust — the fins look like a carpet. After cleaning, the machines run cool and no longer shut down. The owner was about
to buy new computers; he only needed a cleaning.
Important misconception. "Cleaning is just to make it look nice." No — the purpose is cooling and reliability. A machine can
look clean on the outside and be clogged inside.
Limitation. Cleaning cannot reverse damage already done. If a fan bearing is worn out or thermal paste has dried and
temperatures are already very high, cleaning helps a lot but may need to be combined with a fan replacement or re-pasting
(Session 7). And cleaning will not fix a computer whose real problem is software (Session 6) or a failing part (Session 5).
F.2 Safe cleaning tools — and the dangerous ones
The idea. Not every cleaning method is safe. Some tools can destroy a computer. Know which is which.
SAFE tools:
Compressed air (canned air or a compressor with a moisture trap) — for blowing loose dust out of heatsinks, fans and the
case. Hold the can upright; short bursts; keep the nozzle a little distance away; never let it blow liquid.
Antistatic brush / soft paintbrush — for gently loosening dust from fan blades and heatsink fins, and for brushing the
motherboard surface.
Isopropyl alcohol (90%+) on a lint-free cloth or cotton bud — for stubborn grime and for cleaning old thermal paste. It
evaporates quickly and does not conduct electricity when dry.
Microfiber / lint-free cloths — for wiping the case interior, screens and exterior.
Cotton buds — for small crevices and ports.
Anti-static wrist strap — always, to protect against ESD.
DANGEROUS / UNSAFE tools:
Ordinary vacuum cleaner — never use inside a computer. The plastic nozzle and the moving air build static electricity,
which can silently destroy components. A vacuum can also physically suck small parts and jumpers off the board. (Special
anti-static vacuums exist for professional use; the ordinary household one does not.)
Wet cloths / water — water conducts electricity and causes corrosion. Never use water inside a computer.
Your own mouth / blowing with breath — your breath carries moisture (which can cause corrosion) and saliva droplets;
use compressed air instead.
Sharp metal tools — can scratch circuit traces and short connections.
Household cleaning sprays (e.g., strong surface cleaners, bleach solutions) — can leave residues that damage components
and screens. Use only approved screen cleaners or a barely-damp microfiber cloth.
Why compressed air can is safer than a vacuum. A can of compressed air is a sealed, non-sparking source; it does not
generate static the way a vacuum's plastic nozzle does. Keep the can upright so it does not spray its liquid propellant onto the
parts.
Important misconception. "A vacuum cleaner is fine if I do not touch the parts." The static builds on the nozzle through the
moving air and can discharge onto the board even without contact. The safe rule is: compressed air inside, vacuum only
outside and never near open electronics.
Limitation. Compressed air can push dust deeper into crevices if used carelessly. Blow dust outward, toward the exit, and
work in a well-ventilated place, or ideally take the machine outside or near a window so the dust leaves the room.
F.3 The safe internal cleaning procedure
Safety first — read aloud and follow every time:
Power off, unplug the power cable, then press the power button for a second to discharge leftover electricity.
Wear an anti-static wrist strap.
Use only safe tools (F.2).
Work in a well-ventilated area; dust will fly.
Never open the PSU.
Handle components by the edges; do not spin fans with the air jet.
If anything smells burnt or you see a swollen battery, stop and call the instructor.
Step-by-step procedure:
1. Prepare the workspace. Unplug everything. Move the machine to a hard, flat surface in a ventilated area (outside if
possible). Put on the wrist strap.
2. Open the case. Remove the side panel (method depends on the case; the instructor demonstrates on the practice
machine).
3. Loosen, then blow. Use the brush first to gently loosen the dust "carpet" on the heatsink fins and fan blades. Then use
compressed air (short bursts, can upright) to blow the loosened dust out of the case.
4. Clean the heatsink and fans. Hold each fan blade steady with a finger or a soft object so it does not spin while you blow
— spinning a fan with air can damage its bearing and can generate a tiny reverse current. Brush and blow the fins clean.
5. Clean the case interior. Wipe surfaces with a microfiber cloth; use a brush for corners. Clean the air filters if the case has
them (many can be removed and rinsed and dried — only refit when fully dry).
6. Clean the connectors and slots lightly. Use a brush or a short gentle burst of air on RAM slots and expansion slots. Do not
push dust into them.
7. Inspect while you are in there. Look for: a fan that does not spin when the machine runs; bulging capacitors; a swollen
laptop battery; burnt smells; loose cables.
8. Reassemble. Replace the side panel, reconnect only the power cable and needed cables, and power on.
9. Verify and record. Open HWMonitor and record the CPU/GPU temperatures. Compare with the "before" reading. A drop
of several degrees is a clear success.
Analogy. Cleaning the inside is like clearing a blocked drainage channel before the rainy season. You do not wait for the flood;
you clear the channel so the water (heat) can flow away freely.
Important misconception. "I should spin the fan with the air jet to clean it faster." No — forced spinning damages the fan
bearing. Hold the blade steady.
Limitation. Some heatsinks are so clogged that air alone cannot clear them; the cooler may need to be removed to clean
properly (and then thermal paste re-applied on refitting — Session 7). Some laptops require deeper disassembly to reach the
fan; that is more delicate work and should be supervised.
F.4 Cleaning keyboards, ports and screens
The idea. Not everything can be cleaned the same way. Blowing compressed air into a keyboard's electronics area, or spraying
liquid on a screen, can cause damage. Match the method to the surface.
Keyboard (external cleaning, safe for learners):
Power off the computer.
Tip the keyboard upside down and gently shake out crumbs.
Use short bursts of compressed air along the rows of keys, at a slight angle, to lift dust and crumbs out.
Wipe the key tops and the frame with a barely-damp microfiber cloth (dampened with a little isopropyl alcohol or plain
water — not dripping). Never spray liquid directly onto the keyboard.
Between keys, use a cotton bud. Do not force it under keys and do not bend key mechanisms.
Removable-key keyboards: the instructor may show removing a keycap to clean beneath; do not remove keys on a laptop
keyboard (they break easily).
Ports (USB, HDMI, charging ports):
Power off and unplug.
Use short bursts of compressed air to blow dust out of the port. Do not insert sharp metal objects, which can bend the
internal contacts.
For stubborn dust, a soft dry brush or a wooden toothpick used very gently along the edge — never force anything into
the port.
Never put liquid into a port.
Screens (monitors, laptop screens, phone screens):
Power off the screen (this makes dust easier to see and is safer).
Use a microfiber cloth, dry for light dust, or very slightly dampened with plain water or an approved screen cleaner for
marks.
Never spray liquid directly on the screen — spray onto the cloth, not the screen, and never let liquid run into the edges.
Use gentle, circular motion with light pressure. Do not press hard — screens are fragile.
No paper towels (they scratch), no household glass cleaner (ammonia can damage screen coatings), no alcohol on screens
unless the manufacturer says it is safe for that screen (alcohol can damage some anti-glare coatings).
Analogy. Different surfaces need different care, like cleaning a pair of glasses versus cleaning a car engine — same idea
(remove dirt), completely different method.
Important misconception. "More liquid cleans better." For screens, less is more. Excess liquid is the main cause of screen and
keyboard damage during cleaning.
Limitation. Some marks (scratches, screen-print wear) cannot be cleaned away. On laptops, deep cleaning behind the keyboard
requires disassembly and is not a beginner task.
F.5 When to re-apply thermal paste
Plain-English definition. Thermal paste (thermal compound) is the grey paste between the CPU and its heatsink (cooler). It fills
the microscopic gaps between the two metal surfaces so heat passes efficiently from the chip into the cooler.
When to re-apply:
Whenever the CPU and cooler have been separated (for example, during a deep clean where the cooler is removed, or a
cooler replacement).
When temperatures have crept up over the years despite clean fans and good airflow.
On a machine that has run hot for a long time and shows signs of dried, cracked paste.
How often (a guide, not a strict rule): many technicians recommend re-pasting roughly every 1–2 years, or whenever the
cooler is removed. For a well-cooled home PC it may last longer; for a hot, dusty cyber-café machine that is opened regularly,
more often. Judge by temperature readings, not only by a calendar.
Signs the paste needs replacing:
CPU temperatures are high even after cleaning dust and confirming fans spin.
The old paste, when the cooler is lifted, looks dry, cracked or powdery (instead of soft and grey).
Fan noise is high and the machine still runs hot.
The steps (recap from Session 7):
1. Power off, unplug, discharge, wrist strap on.
2. Remove the cooler carefully (do not twist if the CPU is on an older socket design where the CPU may come away stuck to
the cooler — ask the instructor).
3. Clean old paste from CPU and cooler base with isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth/cotton bud until both are clean and
dry.
4. Apply a pea-sized dot of new paste in the centre.
5. Refit the cooler, tightening screws evenly (a little each, in turn).
6. Reconnect the fan cable.
7. Power on and verify temperatures with HWMonitor (should be normal or lower).
Analogy. Thermal paste is like a thin layer of oil between a pot and a hot plate. The pot and plate do not touch perfectly; the oil
fills the tiny gaps so heat moves evenly. Too little and heat does not travel; too much and it makes a mess.
Important misconception. "Thermal paste lasts forever." It dries out over the years and loses effectiveness. Also, "more is
better" is false — a pea-sized amount is correct.
Limitation. Not every high temperature is a paste problem. Sometimes the real issue is a worn-out cooler, a failed fan, blocked
vents or an underpowered case airflow. Diagnose the whole cooling system, not just the paste.
F.6 Environmental threats — dust, humidity, heat and power
The idea. Cleaning protects the inside of the computer. But the environment around the computer — dust, humidity, heat and
unstable electricity — can also destroy it. Managing the environment is part of maintenance.
Dust (already covered). Keep computers off the floor where dust gathers, away from windows where harmattan dust blows in,
and out of the kitchen area (cooking smoke and oil stick to fans and make dust worse).
Humidity and moisture.
High humidity (common in Port Harcourt during the rainy season) can allow moisture to condense on cold components,
causing corrosion and short circuits over time.
Low humidity / dryness (common in the harmattan) makes static electricity build up more easily — increasing ESD risk.
Practical measures: keep machines in dry, ventilated rooms; avoid placing them where they get wet; allow a cold machine
(e.g., brought in from the rain) to reach room temperature before powering it on; use a dehumidifier in severe cases (a
professional/household solution, not essential for beginners).
Heat.
Ambient (room) heat directly raises component temperatures. Keep computers out of direct sun, away from hot spots, and
ensure the room is ventilated.
Ensure the case has clear intake and exhaust vents (not against a wall or curtain) so air can flow.
Power problems — surges, sags and outages.
Surge: a sudden spike in voltage (e.g., when power returns after an outage, or from lightning). A surge can destroy the PSU
and components instantly.
Sag/brownout: voltage dropping below normal (common where supply is weak). Can cause crashes, restarts and data
corruption.
Outage: sudden loss of power — can corrupt files, especially during updates.
Protection devices:
A surge protector (a power strip with surge protection) absorbs sudden voltage spikes. It helps protect against surges but
does not keep the computer running during an outage.
A UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply) is a battery backup box that keeps the computer running for a few minutes during
an outage, giving time to save work and shut down safely. It also helps with sags/brownouts. It is especially valuable for
cyber cafés, offices and any machine doing important work.
Note: a surge protector must be replaced after a serious surge; many have an indicator light that shows when protection is
lost.
Analogy. Electricity is like water coming through a pipe. A surge is a pressure burst that can burst the pipe; a sag is a drop in
pressure that stalls the machines; an outage is the water being cut. A surge protector is a shock absorber; a UPS is a small
reserve tank.
Realistic Port Harcourt scenario. A cyber café loses power suddenly while a customer is midway through printing a
document and another is saving a school project. Without a UPS, the print job is spoilt and the saved project may be corrupted.
With a UPS on the key machines, there is time to save and shut down.
Important misconception. "A surge protector also keeps the computer on during a blackout." No — that is a UPS's job. A
surge protector only guards against spikes.
Limitation. No device guarantees 100% protection — lightning can overwhelm any protector. Good practice also includes
regular backups (so a power problem can never destroy the only copy of the customer's work) and properly grounded wall
sockets.
F.7 Preventive maintenance schedules
The idea. Instead of waiting for a machine to fail, we perform small tasks on a schedule. This keeps computers cool, healthy
and reliable — and it is a service a technician can sell.
Plain-English definition. A preventive maintenance schedule is a plan listing what to check, how often, and who is
responsible, so that small problems are caught before they become big ones.
Why it exists. Most computer failures are slow, not sudden: dust builds, paste dries, drives age, disks fill up. A schedule catches
these while they are still cheap to fix.

Analogy. A maintenance schedule is like a car service book. You do not wait for the engine to seize; you change the oil on time.
The computer is the same.
Important misconception. "If it is working, it does not need maintenance." Working machines are exactly the ones worth
protecting — prevention keeps them working.
Limitation. Schedules must match the environment. A cyber café in a dusty area needs far more frequent cleaning than a
home office in a sealed, air-conditioned room. A schedule copied blindly from the internet will be wrong; adapt it.
F.8 Summary of the session
Dust + heat cause slowness (thermal throttling), random shutdowns (thermal shutdown) and early part failure.
Safe tools: compressed air, antistatic/soft brush, isopropyl alcohol, microfiber cloth, cotton buds, wrist strap. Unsafe:
vacuum cleaner (static), water, breath (moisture), harsh sprays.
Internal clean: power off, unplug, discharge, strap on; loosen with a brush, blow outward with compressed air; hold fans
steady; inspect; verify with HWMonitor.
Keyboards/ports/screens: air for keys/ports; barely-damp microfiber for screens, never spray on the screen; never liquid in
ports.
Thermal paste: re-apply when the cooler is removed or temperatures creep up (roughly every 1–2 years); pea-sized
amount.
Environment: manage dust, humidity, heat and power; use a surge protector for spikes and a UPS for outages/sags.
Preventive schedules for home, office and café; document every task.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
The dusty practice desktop, opened on the bench.
Compressed air, antistatic brush, isopropyl alcohol, microfiber cloths, cotton buds.
An old keyboard and an old monitor/phone screen for cleaning practice.
A surge protector and a UPS.
HWMonitor ready; a note of the dusty machine's "before" temperature.
The Cleaning Checklist and Maintenance Plan Template on the projector.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — Safe internal clean (6 minutes)
Say: "Watch the order: loosen, then blow outward, and never let the fan spin."
Steps:
1. Power off, unplug, press power to discharge, put on the wrist strap.
2. Remove the side panel. Point out the dust on the heatsink and fans.
3. Use the brush to gently loosen the dust on the heatsink fins and fan blades. Say: "I loosen it first. Dry dust sticks; brushing
frees it, then the air can carry it away."
4. Take the can of compressed air, hold it upright, and give short bursts to blow the dust outward (toward the open side /
out of the case). Say: "Upright — if I tilt it, it can spray cold liquid onto the parts. Short bursts, not one long blast."
5. Hold a fan blade steady while blowing it. Say: "If I let the air spin the fan, I can damage the bearing. Free-wheel spinning is
bad for fans."
6. Wipe the case interior with a microfiber cloth; point out the air filter and how to clean it.
Expected results: visible dust removed; heatsink fins visibly clearer.
Reasoning to say aloud: "Loosen, blow outward, protect the fans — that is the whole technique."
Common errors to show and correct:
Tilting the can (show the warning) → liquid risk.
Blowing dust into the machine instead of out.
Spinning the fan with air.
Using a vacuum cleaner — hold it up and say loudly: "Never this, inside a computer — static!"
Questions: "Why brush before blowing?" (Loosen it so air can carry it away.) "Why hold the fan?" (Prevent bearing damage.)
G.3 Demonstration 2 — Re-pasting a CPU (4 minutes)
Say: "Now the paste — small is powerful."
Steps:
1. Show a cooler base with dried, cracked old paste. Say: "This is what paste looks like when it has failed — dry and cracked.
It can no longer pass heat."
2. Clean the CPU and cooler base with isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth until both are clean. Say: "Clean and dry, both
surfaces."
3. Apply a pea-sized dot in the centre. Say: "Small. Too much squeezes out and can make things worse."
4. Show how the cooler presses down and spreads the paste. Refit and reconnect the fan cable.
5. Verify: point to HWMonitor and describe the expected temperature drop.
Expected results: clean surfaces, a pea-sized paste dot, cooler refitted, fan cable reconnected.
Common errors to show and correct:
Using too much paste.
Forgetting the fan cable.
Twisting the cooler (risk of pulling a stuck CPU — warn).
Questions: "How do you know the paste needs replacing?" (Dry/cracked, or high temps despite clean fans.)
G.4 Demonstration 3 — External cleaning and protection devices (5 minutes)
Steps (keyboard/ports/screen):
1. Power off. Show short bursts of compressed air along the keyboard rows; shake crumbs out first.
2. Show wiping key tops with a barely-damp microfiber cloth — never spraying onto the keyboard.
3. Show a cotton bud for between-keys cleaning.
4. On the old screen, show a dry microfiber cloth for dust, and a slightly damp cloth for marks, sprayed on the cloth, not the
screen. Say: "No paper towels, no glass cleaner, no hard pressing."
Steps (surge protector and UPS): 5. Hold up the surge protector. Say: "This absorbs sudden voltage spikes — like when
power returns after an outage. It does NOT keep the computer on during a blackout." 6. Hold up the UPS. Say: "This is a battery
backup. It keeps the computer alive for a few minutes so you can save and shut down safely. This is what a cyber café needs
on its key machines." 7. Point to the surge protector's indicator light. Say: "When this light goes out, the protection is spent —
replace it."
Expected results: learners see correct external cleaning technique and understand the difference between a surge protector and
a UPS.
Common errors to show and correct:
Spraying liquid directly on a screen or keyboard.
Confusing a surge protector with a UPS.
Questions: "Which device protects against a blackout?" (UPS.) "Which protects against a spike?" (Surge protector.)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi runs a small hair salon in Rumuola that also has a computer for bookings, customer records and the salon's
social-media page. The computer is on the floor near the door, where dust from the street and hair clippings collects. For
months it has been getting slower and louder, and twice recently it shut down in the middle of work. Ngozi assumes it is "old"
and plans to buy a new one.
Relevant facts.
The machine is only about three years old.
It sits on the floor, close to the open door.
It has never been opened or cleaned.
The room is hot, and the computer shares a power strip with a hair dryer (a high-power appliance) on the same socket.
Customer records are stored on the machine with no backup.
The decision point. Buy a new computer, or clean and protect the existing one?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a new computer (expensive; the new one will also get dusty and hot in the same spot).
2. Clean the current machine (internal dust + thermal paste if needed), then fix the setup: move it off the floor, away from the
door, onto a surge protector, separate from the hair dryer, and start a cleaning and backup routine.
3. Ignore it and hope it lasts.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. Internal cleaning clears the dust blanket (the likely cause of the
slowdown and shutdowns); moving the machine off the floor and away from the door reduces future dust; a surge protector
(and separating it from the hair dryer) protects it from power problems; setting up a backup for the customer records and a
monthly cleaning routine prevents the problem returning. If temperatures stay high after cleaning, re-apply thermal paste. This
costs a fraction of a new computer.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is expensive and does not fix the environment — the new machine will face the same dust, heat and power
problems and may also die young.
Approach 2 is affordable, treats the actual cause, and adds lasting protection; it requires a small amount of regular effort.
Approach 3 lets the machine die, likely losing the customer records.
Professional connection. This is a core professional insight: most "old" computers are not old — they are neglected. A
technician who cleans, protects and documents earns repeat business and referrals, because the customer sees real value (a fast
machine and saved records) at low cost. The certificate's promise of "honest assessments and recommendations" is exactly this.
Transfer exercise (ask the class): "Ngozi asks, 'Why did my computer shut down in the middle of work?' Give her a plain
answer and one prevention step."
Suggested answer: "Dust had clogged the cooling inside, so the machine got too hot and shut itself off to protect the parts. We
will clean it, and if you keep it off the floor and clean it every month, it will stay cool and stop shutting down."

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Dust will not come off the heatsink / temperatures stay high after cleaning
Symptoms: cleaning done, but CPU still hot.
Likely causes: the heatsink is so clogged that air cannot clear it; dried thermal paste; a failed or slow fan; blocked vents; the
room is too hot.
How to investigate: check fan speeds (HWMonitor); check the fan spins; inspect the vents; check the paste condition.
Safe corrective steps: remove the cooler for a proper clean and re-apply thermal paste (Session 7, supervised); replace a
failed fan; unblock vents; improve room airflow.
How to confirm resolved: temperatures drop and stay normal during use.
Prevention: clean on schedule before it clogs; maintain airflow.
K.2 A fan does not spin
Symptoms: one fan is stationary while the machine runs.
Likely causes: the fan is clogged and stalled; the fan cable is disconnected; the fan bearing has failed; the fan is not getting
power.
How to investigate: check its cable is connected to the right header; clear dust; test.
Safe corrective steps: clean it; reseat its cable; if it still does not spin, replace the fan.
How to confirm resolved: the fan spins freely and quietly.
Prevention: regular cleaning; replace worn fans promptly.
K.3 Machine will not start after cleaning
Symptoms: no power after reassembly.
Likely causes: a cable was knocked loose (24-pin, CPU power, SATA, fan); a component was dislodged; the side panel
trapped a cable.
How to investigate: re-open and check all connectors and component seating; look for trapped cables.
Safe corrective steps: reseat connections; free trapped cables; power on.
How to confirm resolved: the machine POSTs and boots.
Prevention: photograph connections before cleaning; work gently; check before closing.
K.4 Static damage after cleaning
Symptoms: the machine behaves strangely or dies after a clean, with no visible cause.
Likely cause: ESD — static from a cloth, brush or body discharged into a component.
How to investigate: there may be no visible sign; ESD damage can be invisible. This is why prevention is essential.
Safe corrective steps: ESD damage is often permanent and part-level; identify and replace the affected part (diagnostic
repeat, Session 5).
How to confirm resolved: the machine runs normally after replacing the damaged part.
Prevention: always wear an anti-static wrist strap; use antistatic tools; never use a vacuum cleaner inside.
K.5 Screen damage during cleaning
Symptoms: streaks, smears or damage after wiping a screen.
Likely causes: liquid sprayed onto the screen; harsh glass cleaner; hard pressing; paper towels.
How to investigate: inspect for fluid marks or surface damage.
Safe corrective steps: for smears, gently wipe with a clean, dry microfiber cloth; surface damage may not be repairable
(may require replacing the panel).
How to confirm resolved: the screen is clear and undamaged.
Prevention: screen cloth barely damp, liquid on the cloth not the screen, gentle pressure, no harsh chemicals.
K.6 Power-related damage or corruption
Symptoms: crashes, restarts, or corrupted files, especially after outages or when power returns.
Likely causes: surge, sag or outage; no surge protection; no UPS.
How to investigate: ask about the power supply history; check for a surge protector; check for corrupted files.
Safe corrective steps: fit a surge protector; use a UPS on critical machines; back up; repair corrupted files (Session 6,
sfc ).
How to confirm resolved: stable operation and no further corruption.
Prevention: surge protector on everything, UPS on critical machines, regular backups.
K.7 Distinguishing likely from confirmed
As always, a likely cause becomes confirmed by evidence: a temperature reading that drops after cleaning, a fan observed
spinning (or not), a paste visibly dry, a machine that will not POST only after a specific event. Record observations honestly and
never claim "cleaned and fixed" without a before/after temperature reading and a working test.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. In one or two sentences, explain how dust makes a computer slow. Q2. Multiple choice. Which of these
must you never use to clean the inside of a computer? a) Compressed air b) An antistatic brush c) An ordinary vacuum cleaner
d) A microfiber cloth Q3. True or false, with a reason. "You should spray screen cleaner directly onto the monitor screen." Q4.
Explain in your own words. When does thermal paste need to be replaced, and how much do you use? Q5. Short answer.
What is the difference between a surge protector and a UPS? Q6. Scenario. A cyber café's computers keep shutting down
during busy evenings, and the room is not air-conditioned. Give two maintenance actions that would help, and explain why.
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. Dust settles on fan blades and between heatsink fins and blocks airflow, so heat cannot escape. The CPU gets hot and the
computer slows itself down on purpose (thermal throttling), so it feels slow; if it gets hotter still, it shuts off to protect itself.
A2. (c) An ordinary vacuum cleaner. Its plastic nozzle and moving air build static electricity that can destroy components (and
it can suck off small parts). Use compressed air inside instead.
A3. False. Never spray liquid directly onto a screen — it can run into the edges and damage the screen. Spray (or dampen) the
cloth, then wipe gently.
A4. Replace thermal paste whenever the CPU and cooler are separated, or when temperatures creep up despite clean fans
(roughly every 1–2 years). Use a pea-sized amount in the centre of the CPU — not too much.
A5. A surge protector absorbs sudden voltage spikes (e.g., when power returns after an outage); it does not keep the
computer running during a blackout. A UPS contains a battery and keeps the computer running for a few minutes during an
outage and steadies sags/brownouts, allowing a safe save and shutdown.
A6. Any two sensible actions, e.g.: (1) Clean the internal dust regularly (monthly in a dusty, non-air-conditioned room) —
dust blocks airflow and causes overheating and shutdowns. (2) Improve cooling/airflow — ensure vents are clear, all fans
work (replace failed fans), and the room is ventilated; add a surge protector + UPS if the shutdowns relate to unstable power.
(Also acceptable: re-apply thermal paste if temperatures stay high; move machines out of direct heat.)
Marking: award for correct concept; deduct for unsafe answers (vacuum inside, spraying liquid on screens, opening the PSU).

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that cleaning is not about looks — it is about cooling and reliability. Dust blocks
airflow, so heat cannot escape; the computer then slows itself down (thermal throttling) and can shut itself off (thermal
shutdown), and over time the heat shortens the life of every part. You learned the safe tools (compressed air, antistatic brush,
isopropyl alcohol, microfiber cloth, cotton buds, wrist strap) and the dangerous ones (vacuum cleaner, water, your breath,
harsh sprays). You practised the safe internal clean (power off, unplug, discharge, brush, blow outward, hold fans steady,
inspect, verify with HWMonitor) and external cleaning of keyboards, ports and screens. You learned when to re-apply thermal
paste (a pea-sized amount) and how to protect a computer from its environment — dust, humidity, heat and power
surges/sags/outages — using a surge protector and a UPS. Finally, you learned to build preventive maintenance schedules
for homes, offices and cyber cafés, and to document every task.
Five important takeaways:
1. Dust + heat = slowdowns, shutdowns and early death. Cleaning is the cheapest repair.
2. Compressed air (upright) and an antistatic brush inside; never a vacuum cleaner inside.
3. Hold fans steady; never spin them with air.
4. Re-apply thermal paste when the cooler is removed or temperatures creep up — pea-sized.
5. Protect from power: a surge protector for spikes, a UPS for outages — and always back up.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I clean a computer safely using the right tools?
Can I record before/after temperatures and show the improvement?
Can I clean a keyboard, ports and screen without damaging them?
Can I explain a surge protector versus a UPS?
Can I write a maintenance schedule tailored to an environment?
Safety self-check (tick honestly):
I powered off, unplugged and discharged before opening the machine.
I wore my anti-static wrist strap and used antistatic tools.
I never used a vacuum cleaner inside the computer.
I never opened the PSU.
I kept liquid away from the inside of the machine and off screens.
I recorded a before/after temperature reading and verified the machine worked.
Connection to the next session. You can now diagnose, replace and maintain hardware. The final major technical skill is the
software foundation itself: next session, Session 9 — Operating System Installation, you will learn to prepare for a Windows
install, install Windows on a machine (including one with a new SSD), install drivers, run updates, and activate — turning a
blank or freshly-repaired machine into a working computer ready for the customer. Maintenance kept the machine healthy;
installation gives it its brain.

## O. INSTRUCTOR EVALUATION NOTES (instructor-only)
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner sets up correctly: power off, unplug, discharge, wrist strap — every time.
The learner uses the correct tools and refuses the vacuum cleaner inside.
The learner uses compressed air upright and holds fans steady while blowing.
The learner records before/after temperatures and makes an honest comparison.
The learner cleans screens/ports appropriately (no direct spraying, no liquid in ports).
The learner's maintenance schedule is tailored to the environment, not copied.
The learner explains the difference between a surge protector and a UPS correctly.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Cleaning is only cosmetic." → It is about cooling and reliability.
"A vacuum cleaner is fine." → Static risk; never inside.
"Spin the fan with air to clean it." → Damages the bearing.
"More liquid cleans better." → Excess liquid damages screens/keyboards.
"A surge protector keeps the computer on in a blackout." → That is a UPS.
"If it is working, no maintenance is needed." → Working machines are the ones worth protecting.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Tool-sorting drill: give cards for tools (compressed air, brush, vacuum, cloth, water, alcohol) and have the learner sort them
into "safe" and "unsafe" with reasons.
2. Technique rehearsal: practise on a spare fan/heatsink: brush-then-blow, can upright, hold the fan steady — without
powering anything on.
3. Schedule-adaptation drill: give three environments (home, office, café) and have the learner adjust cleaning frequency
and justify each change.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a step-by-step cleaning card and a partially filled Maintenance Plan Template; pair with a
stronger learner for the internal clean.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): perform the full clean and remove the cooler to re-apply thermal paste (supervised), verify with a
before/after temperature graph, and design a maintenance schedule for a cyber café with a per-machine logbook.
Timing guidance: This session is hands-on and can be messy. Protect the guided practical and the before/after temperature
exercise — that is the core learning. If time is short, trim the case study to a two-minute mention and demonstrate external
cleaning briefly. Ensure adequate ventilation and time to tidy up. Do not rush the safety steps; a rushed clean with a vacuum or
a tilted can can destroy a machine.
APPENDIX 8A — 10-MINUTE SAFETY REFRESHER (only if needed)
If learners cannot demonstrate the two safety must-dos and ESD awareness, run this before Section D.
1. The two must-dos — the class chants: "Power off and unplug. Wear the anti-static wrist strap."
2. Discharge — press the power button after unplugging to drain leftover electricity.
3. ESD in cleaning — say: "Rubbing a cloth or brush builds static. Static silently kills components. That is why we ground
