# Component Replacement

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module: Repair and Maintenance

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (hands-on, safety-critical)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6: components, safety and tools, disassembly/reassembly, the diagnostic workflow, hardware diagnostics, and software diagnostics. If a learner cannot demonstrate safe case-opening and ESD precautions, give the 10-minute safety refresher in Appendix 7A before this session.

**Connection to previous work:** Sessions 5 and 6 taught you to find the fault. This session teaches you to fix it by choosing compatible parts and physically replacing them, then verifying the machine works.

**Preparation before class:** One or more practice desktops that may be opened; matching known-good replacement RAM (same generation as the practice machines) and a spare known-good stick; at least one 2.5 inch SSD and a matching SATA cable + power cable, or a spare HDD; a known-good PSU of suitable wattage for the practice machine; thermal paste; cleaning cloths; anti-static wrist straps (one per learner); screwdrivers with the correct heads; a known-good USB or SATA cable; a bootable Windows USB (for the OS-reinstall branch) and, if available, a cloning tool (a program that copies one drive to another) pre-installed on a bootable USB or on the running machine. Printed Compatibility Check Sheet and Replacement Service Log (one per learner). Important wording used in this session. A component is a physical part of the computer (RAM, drive, PSU, GPU, CPU cooler). To verify a replacement means to power on the machine and confirm that the new part is detected and working correctly — never assume it just because it fitted.

Institution

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why a replacement part must be compatible with the machine, and check compatibility for RAM (generation,
speed, size), storage (interface) and PSU (wattage and connectors).
2. State the difference between a hard disk drive (HDD) and a solid-state drive (SSD), and between SATA and NVMe, and
choose the correct one for a given machine.
3. Upgrade RAM correctly — match the type, seat the stick by the notch, and confirm the new capacity is recognised.
4. Replace a storage drive by removing the old one, fitting the new one, connecting the correct cables, and either cloning
the old drive or installing Windows on the new drive.
5. Explain "clone" and "fresh install", including when each is appropriate and why backing up comes first.
6. Replace a power supply safely using a known-good unit of suitable wattage, reconnecting every cable and double-
checking before power-on.
7. Re-apply thermal paste correctly (clean, apply a small amount, refit the cooler) and confirm improved cooling.
8. Verify a machine after any replacement using the tools from Sessions 5 and 6 (POST, Windows, Task Manager,
HWMonitor).
9. Follow safety and ESD procedures at every step, and know when a task should be handled by a qualified technician.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the bench, place two objects side by side: an old mechanical hard drive (HDD) and a solid-state drive
(SSD) of the same size. Also write on the board the price of a typical small SSD versus the cost of a new budget computer (use
realistic Port Harcourt market figures the academy has prepared — do not invent exact prices).
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. This week you stop watching and start doing. Last week you learned what is wrong. Today you make it
right.
Look at these two things. One is an old spinning hard drive. The other is a solid-state drive — an SSD. Same size, similar
price. But this one [SSD] can wake an old, tired computer up so well that the owner will swear you replaced the whole
machine. Customers love this job more than almost any other, because it is cheap and the change is dramatic.
Here is the question for today: How do you know the right part to buy? Buy the wrong RAM and it will not fit. Buy the
wrong SSD and it will not connect. Replace a power supply wrongly and you can damage the whole computer — or hurt
yourself.
Today you learn to choose compatible parts, to replace them safely, and to verify that the machine is truly fixed. This is
the day you become a technician, not just a diagnoser."
Expected learner response: Learners will be interested in the SSD speed claim; some may ask "how much does an SSD cost?" or
"can my laptop take one?". Accept the questions and say they are exactly what a technician must learn to answer for customers.
Why this opener works: It promises a satisfying, high-value repair (the SSD upgrade), it immediately raises the real professional
skill of choosing the right part, and it sets the safety tone for a hands-on session where mistakes have real consequences.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the two ESD safety steps before you touch any internal component?"
Expected answer: Power off and unplug the machine; wear an anti-static wrist strap (or touch a bare metal part of the
case first).
Common gap: learners remember the strap but forget to unplug. Say: both, every time.
Question 2: "In what order did we remove components during teardown?"
Expected answer: a sensible order — e.g., remove power cables first, then the GPU, then RAM, then storage, and the CPU
cooler only when needed (or the reverse for reassembly).
Common gap: forgetting to disconnect power first. Emphasise: power first, always.
Question 3: "What is the five-step diagnostic workflow?"
Expected answer: Observe → Ask → Hypothesise → Test → Resolve (then document).
Question 4: "A machine is slow and CrystalDiskInfo shows a healthy drive, RAM passes, and temperatures are normal. What
might make it fast again without replacing it?"
Expected answer: cleaning up software (startup programs, full disk, malware) from Session 6 — and, if the drive is an old
HDD, replacing it with an SSD (today's topic) even though it is not "faulty".
Question 5: "Why must we never open a PSU, and how do we test one?"
Expected answer: lethal stored charge; test by swap with a known-good PSU, never by opening.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Diagnosis is detective work. Replacement is surgery. Before a surgeon operates, they check that the new part matches the
patient — blood type, size, and so on. Today, 'matching' means RAM generation and speed, storage interface, and PSU
wattage. Get the match wrong and the operation fails."
Gap handling: If more than a third of the class cannot state the ESD steps in Question 1, stop and run the Appendix 7A safety
refresher now. This session must not proceed with learners who cannot work safely.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why compatibility matters — the foundation of replacement
The idea. Not every part fits every computer. Before buying or fitting any part, you must confirm it is compatible with the
machine.
Plain-English definition. Compatible means the part is of the correct type and size to work with that specific motherboard, case
or system. An incompatible part will not fit, will not connect, or will fit but not work (or even damage something).
Why it matters. Customers will lose faith if you order a part that does not fit. Worse, a wrong part (for example, the wrong
voltage or a badly matched power supply) can damage the computer. Checking compatibility first is what separates a
professional from a guesser.
Analogy. Buying a replacement part is like buying shoes: the right brand is not enough — you need the right size. A beautiful
shoe that is the wrong size is useless. Compatibility is the "size" of a computer part.
What we always check, by part:
RAM: the generation (DDR3, DDR4 or DDR5), the size (GB per stick), and ideally the speed (MHz) and whether it is a
desktop (DIMM) or laptop (SO-DIMM) stick.
Storage: the interface (SATA or NVMe) and the physical size/format (2.5-inch, 3.5-inch, M.2).
PSU: the wattage (must be enough and not excessive) and the connectors the machine needs.
GPU: the slot type (PCIe), the physical length that will fit the case, and whether the PSU can supply enough power.
Laptop parts: model-specific; always match the exact laptop model.
Important misconception. "Any RAM will fit any computer." False. DDR3, DDR4 and DDR5 are different shapes and are not
interchangeable. A DDR4 stick will not go into a DDR3 slot, and forcing it will damage the slot.
Limitation. Even compatible parts can differ in quality; a cheap part of the right type may fail early. Compatibility is necessary,
not sufficient — quality matters too.
F.2 Choosing RAM — generation, speed and size
Plain-English definition. RAM (Random Access Memory) is the fast working memory that holds whatever the computer is
doing right now. It comes in sticks. More RAM lets you run more programs at once before the machine slows down.
The three things to match:
1. Generation: DDR3, DDR4 or DDR5. These are different physical shapes and cannot be mixed. Check the motherboard's
manual or look up the model to know which generation the machine uses.
2. Size: how many gigabytes (GB) per stick, and the total the machine supports. A 32-bit version of Windows can only use
about 4 GB, so extra RAM is wasted there; 64-bit Windows uses more.
3. Speed: measured in MHz (e.g., 2666 MHz, 3200 MHz). Ideally use the same speed as the existing RAM. If you mix speeds,
the machine usually runs all sticks at the slowest speed — it should still work, but matching is best.
Desktop vs laptop. Desktops use DIMM (longer) sticks; laptops use SO-DIMM (shorter) sticks. They are not interchangeable.
Running in pairs (dual-channel). Many motherboards run faster when two matched sticks are installed in the correct paired
slots (often the same colour slots). This is optional for beginners but good practice.
Realistic Port Harcourt scenario. A shop owner's desktop with 4 GB RAM slows to a crawl when he opens his accounting
program and a browser with many tabs. The technician checks the motherboard is DDR4, buys one matching 8 GB DDR4 stick of
the same speed, installs it, and the machine runs smoothly. The cost is a fraction of a new computer.
Analogy. RAM is the size of a trader's work-table. A small table cluttering up with goods slows every task; a bigger table lets you
spread out and work faster. But the table must fit your shop — you cannot buy a table bigger than the room.
Important misconception. "More RAM always makes it faster." It helps when the machine is short of memory (many programs
open, disk light always busy). If the real problem is a slow HDD or a virus, adding RAM will not fix it.
Limitations of upgrading RAM. Some laptops have RAM soldered to the board and cannot be upgraded. Some machines have
a maximum capacity (e.g., 16 GB) beyond which extra RAM is not recognised. Always check first.
F.3 Choosing storage — HDD vs SSD, SATA vs NVMe
Plain-English definitions.
HDD (Hard Disk Drive): stores data on spinning metal platters with a moving head. Cheap per gigabyte, but slower and
mechanical (it can click and wear out).
SSD (Solid-State Drive): stores data in memory chips with no moving parts. Much faster, quieter and more shock-
resistant. More expensive per gigabyte, but the speed difference is huge.
SATA: a common connection type used by both 2.5-inch/3.5-inch HDDs and 2.5-inch SSDs. It uses a flat SATA data cable
and a SATA power cable. Typical speed: around 6 Gb/s (the interface limit), though a mechanical HDD is far slower than
that in real use.
NVMe: a newer, much faster connection used by M.2 SSDs that slot directly onto the motherboard, with no data or power
cable. NVMe drives can be several times faster than SATA SSDs.
Why this matters for choosing a part. You must match the interface the machine supports:
If the machine has SATA ports and drive bays → a 2.5-inch SATA SSD is the safe, universal upgrade.
If the motherboard has an M.2 slot that supports NVMe → an NVMe SSD is faster (but check the slot's key type and
supported length).
A 3.5-inch bay takes a 3.5-inch HDD; a 2.5-inch bay takes a 2.5-inch SSD/HDD (a 2.5-inch drive can usually be mounted in
a 3.5-inch bay with an adapter bracket).
Why an SSD upgrade speeds up an old PC. An HDD must physically spin up and move a head to find data, which is slow.
Windows, on start-up and when opening files, reads thousands of small pieces of data. On an HDD each read waits for the
mechanical parts; on an SSD the data is found instantly. That is why replacing an HDD with an SSD makes an old computer feel
new — the mechanical delay disappears. The CPU and RAM are unchanged; the speed came from removing the
mechanical bottleneck.
Analogy. An HDD is like fetching a book from a huge library by walking down long shelves. An SSD is like having the book
already on your desk. The book is the same; the time to get it is completely different.
Important misconception. "SSD means I can never lose data." SSDs fail too, often suddenly and with less warning than HDDs.
Backups remain essential for both.
Limitations of SSD choice.
An old motherboard with only SATA ports cannot use NVMe — you must use a SATA SSD.
Some very old machines do not support a drive large enough to hold the customer's data comfortably; check capacity
needs.
Cloning requires the new drive to have enough capacity to hold everything on the old drive.
F.4 Choosing a PSU — wattage and connectors
Plain-English definition. The PSU (Power Supply Unit) converts wall electricity into the low voltages the components need.
Wattage (W) is its power capacity — how much total power it can supply.
The two things to match:
1. Enough wattage: the PSU must supply at least as much as the machine needs. A rough guide: a basic office desktop with
no dedicated GPU needs far less than a gaming machine with a big graphics card. If the PSU is too weak, the machine
crashes or shuts down when working hard. If it is far stronger than needed, it is not dangerous, but it is a waste of money
(and efficiency is best around a moderate load).
2. The correct connectors: the PSU must have the connectors the machine needs — the big 24-pin motherboard connector,
the CPU power connector (often 4-pin or 8-pin), SATA power connectors for drives, and PCIe power connectors for a
graphics card if fitted.
Safety — the golden rule. Never open a PSU. Even unplugged, its capacitors hold a lethal charge. A PSU is a replace-only
item.
Realistic Port Harcourt scenario. A customer's desktop shuts down during printing (when the printer and CPU work together).
Diagnosis by swap test shows the PSU is failing. The technician checks the new PSU has enough wattage and the right
connectors, fits it, and the shutdowns stop.
Analogy. The PSU is like the generating set for a building. If the generator is too small for all the appliances, everything dims or
the generator cuts out when many appliances run at once. It must be big enough and have the right sockets to plug everything
in.
Important misconception. "A bigger-wattage PSU always makes the machine more reliable." Wattage is capacity, not health. A
failing 700 W PSU is still failing. Match the wattage sensibly and choose a quality unit.
Limitation. Cheap "no-name" PSUs often overstate their wattage and can damage components. Buy from a reputable brand. If
unsure about wattage for a big build, a technician uses a wattage calculator or the GPU maker's recommendation — not
guesswork.
F.5 Upgrading RAM — the procedure
Safety first: power off, unplug, press the power button for a second to discharge leftover power, wear your anti-static wrist
strap, and work on a hard flat surface.
Steps:
1. Confirm compatibility using the Compatibility Check Sheet: correct generation (DDR3/4/5), desktop or laptop type, size
and speed.
2. Open the case and locate the RAM slots on the motherboard.
3. Remove the old stick (if replacing): push the retaining clips at both ends outward until the stick pops up, then lift it out by
its edges. Do not touch the gold contacts.
4. Align the new stick: note the notch (the off-centre gap in the row of contacts). It only fits one way. Line the notch up with
the slot's key.
5. Seat the stick: press down firmly and evenly at both ends until the clips snap up into place. You should hear/feel a click. If
it resists, stop — the notch is probably the wrong way round; do not force it.
6. Run dual-channel (optional): if fitting two sticks, use the paired slots indicated in the motherboard manual (often the same
colour).
7. Close the case, reconnect power, power on.
8. Verify: the machine should POST and start. Check the new total in Windows: Task Manager → Performance → Memory,
or the About page, or the BIOS. Confirm the full amount is recognised (some is reserved by the system/hardware, so a small
shortfall is normal).
Analogy. Seating RAM is like fitting a key into a lock — the key only goes in one way (the notch), and it needs a firm, straight
push, not a twist or a shove.
Common mistake. Forcing the stick. If it will not seat easily, it is almost always the wrong orientation or wrong generation. Back
off and re-check.
F.6 Replacing storage — monitoring, cloning vs fresh install
Safety first: power off, unplug, discharge, wrist strap. If the old drive still works, back up the customer's data before doing
anything — cloning can fail, and a fresh install erases the old system.
Definition: cloning. Cloning means copying the entire contents of the old drive (Windows, programs, settings and files) to the
new drive, so the machine starts up exactly as before but on the new hardware. It saves the customer from reinstalling
everything.
Definition: fresh install. A fresh install means putting a brand-new copy of Windows on the new drive (Session 9), then
reinstalling the customer's programs and restoring their files from backup. It is cleaner and often faster, but it is more work and
the customer must have their data backed up.
When to clone vs fresh install:
Situation Better choice
Old drive still readable, customer wants everything unchanged Clone
Old drive is failing and barely reads (may fail mid-clone) Back up files; fresh install is safer
Customer's Windows is full of junk/malware Fresh install (cleaner)
Customer has no backup and the drive is healthy Clone (but still back up first if possible)
Steps to replace the drive:
1. Back up the data (clone tool copies everything, but backup protects against clone failure).
2. Power off, unplug, discharge, strap on, open the case.
3. Locate the drive bay and the current drive.
4. Disconnect the SATA data cable and the SATA power cable from the drive.
5. Remove the screws holding the drive (note where they go; use a tray).
6. Slide the old drive out and set it aside carefully (handle it flat; do not drop an HDD).
7. Fit the new drive into the bay (use an adapter bracket if a 2.5-inch drive goes into a 3.5-inch bay). Secure the screws.
8. Reconnect the SATA data cable (to a motherboard SATA port) and the SATA power cable.
9. Close the case, reconnect power, power on.
10. Now either clone or install Windows:
Clone path: boot from the cloning tool (or run it from Windows, with the old drive attached via a USB adapter) and
copy old → new. Then set the new drive as the boot drive and remove the old one. Verify it boots and everything is
present.
Fresh-install path: boot from the Windows USB and install Windows on the new drive (Session 9), then reinstall
programs and restore files.
11. Verify: the machine boots from the new drive, Windows runs, and the drive shows up as healthy (CrystalDiskInfo) and with
the expected capacity.
Analogy. Cloning is like moving your whole house to a new building — every piece of furniture already in place. A fresh install
is like moving into an empty new building and furnishing it yourself. Cloning is quicker to settle in; a fresh install gives you a
clean start.
Important misconception. "Cloning always works." It can fail — especially with a dying source drive, a drive that is larger than
the destination, or some Windows/partition setups. That is why backing up first is not optional.
Limitation. Cloning needs the new drive to be at least as large as the used space on the old drive. If the old drive is full, a same-
size new drive may not fit the clone.
F.7 Replacing a PSU safely
Safety first — read twice. Power off, unplug the power cable from the wall and from the PSU, press the power button to
discharge, wear your anti-static wrist strap. Never open the PSU case. Never work with it plugged in.
Steps:
1. Confirm compatibility: the new PSU has enough wattage and the correct connectors (24-pin, CPU power, SATA power,
PCIe power if needed).
2. Open the case. Photograph or note every cable before removing it — a phone photo is a technician's best friend.
3. Disconnect ALL PSU cables from the motherboard and every component: the 24-pin, the CPU power, SATA power to each
drive, and PCIe power to the GPU. Also disconnect any cables to extra fans or lights.
4. Remove the four screws at the back of the case holding the PSU (usually at the top-back or bottom-back).
5. Slide the old PSU out and set it aside. Remember: it is not repairable — it is going to be replaced.
6. Slide the new PSU into place and fit the four screws.
7. Reconnect every cable, following your photo/notes: 24-pin first, then CPU power, then SATA power to drives, then PCIe to
the GPU. Make sure each connector is fully seated and not forced into the wrong socket.
8. Double-check every connection. Count them against the photo. A missed cable (especially the CPU power) is the
number-one cause of "it will not start".
9. Keep cables tidy so they do not touch fan blades.
10. Close the case, reconnect the power cable, and power on.
11. Verify: the machine POSTs, fans spin, and it reaches Windows/shows a picture. Check that all drives are detected.
Analogy. Replacing a PSU is like replacing the main breaker of a house: you shut off the supply, label every wire, fit the new box,
and reconnect each wire to exactly the right place. One wrong wire and things do not work (or worse).
Important misconception. "The PSU plugs in one way, so I cannot make a mistake." You can plug the wrong connector into
the wrong socket with force, especially PCIe power into CPU power or vice versa. Check shapes — do not force.
Limitation. Modular PSUs (where cables detach from the PSU) mean you must use only the cables supplied with that PSU
model. Mixing cables from different brands/models can damage components because the pin wiring differs. Never reuse one
PSU's cables with a different PSU.
F.8 Re-applying thermal paste correctly
Plain-English definition. Thermal paste (also called thermal compound) is a grey paste applied between the CPU and its
heatsink/fan (the cooler). It fills the tiny invisible gaps between the two metal surfaces so heat can pass from the CPU into the
cooler more efficiently.
Why it exists. Metal surfaces look flat but are microscopically rough. Air is a poor heat conductor. The paste fills those gaps,
giving a much better heat path. It dries out over years, so replacing it every so often restores cooling.
When to re-paste: when the CPU and cooler are separated (e.g., during a cleaning or a cooler replacement), or when
temperatures have crept up over the years despite clean fans.
Steps:
1. Power off, unplug, discharge, wrist strap on.
2. Remove the CPU cooler (unscrew or unclip it — types differ; note the order).
3. Clean the old paste off both the CPU (top surface) and the cooler base using isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth or
cotton bud, until both are clean and dry. Do not bend the CPU pins (on older socket types the pins are on the CPU; on
newer ones they are in the socket — be careful either way).
4. Do not remove the CPU unless you must; cleaning the top surface in place is safest for beginners.
5. Apply a small amount of new paste — a pea-sized dot in the centre of the CPU is the common, safe method. Some prefer
a small line; the point is a small amount that will spread under pressure.
6. Refit the cooler, pressing evenly so the paste spreads. Tighten screws gently and evenly (a little on each screw in turn, not
one fully tight).
7. Reconnect the cooler's fan cable to the CPU fan header on the motherboard.
8. Power on and verify with HWMonitor: temperatures should be normal or lower than before.
Analogy. Thermal paste is like adding a thin layer of cooking oil between a frying pan and the burner — it helps heat travel
evenly where the surfaces do not perfectly touch. Too little and heat does not transfer; too much and it makes a mess and can
squeeze out.
Important misconception. "More paste is better." No — too much can squeeze out onto the motherboard and can actually
reduce contact. A small pea-sized amount is correct.
Safety limitation. Removing a cooler on some machines also removes the CPU (on a few older designs). If the CPU comes out
stuck to the cooler, do not pull it apart — ask the instructor. Twisting or pulling can bend pins and destroy the CPU.
F.9 Verifying after replacement — the professional finish
The idea. A replacement is not finished when the part is fitted; it is finished when the machine is verified working. Never hand
back a machine you have not tested.
A verification checklist for any replacement:
1. POST and boot: power on; the machine completes POST and reaches Windows (or the installer). No beeps, no errors.
2. Detected correctly: the OS sees the new part.
RAM: Task Manager → Performance → Memory shows the new total.
Storage: This PC / Disk Management shows the new drive with the right capacity; CrystalDiskInfo shows it as Good.
PSU: all drives and the GPU power up; no random shutdowns during a quick load test.
Thermal paste: HWMonitor shows normal (or improved) CPU temperature.
3. Run for a while: use the machine for a few minutes; open programs; watch for crashes, restarts or overheating.
4. Document: record the old part, the new part, the reason, and the verification results on the Replacement Service Log.
5. Return the machine to the customer with a plain-language explanation and any advice (e.g., back up regularly).
Important misconception. "If it powers on, it is fixed." Powering on is step one. A drive might not be detected, a RAM stick
might be faulty, or the machine might overheat ten minutes later. Verify and test under use.
F.10 Summary of the session
Compatible means the part matches the machine (RAM generation/size/speed; storage interface; PSU wattage/connectors;
laptop = model-specific).
RAM: match DDR generation, DIMM vs SO-DIMM, size and speed; seat by the notch, click the clips, verify the total.
Storage: HDD is mechanical and slow; SSD is fast; SATA uses cables; NVMe uses an M.2 slot. An SSD upgrade speeds up an
old PC by removing the mechanical bottleneck.
Cloning copies everything to the new drive; a fresh install is cleaner but more work. Back up first either way.
PSU: match wattage and connectors; never open it; reconnect every cable and double-check.
Thermal paste: clean off the old, apply a pea-sized amount, refit the cooler, check temperatures.
Verify every replacement before returning the machine.

Say: "Watch the notch — it is the safety feature."

Forgetting the wrist strap.

G.4 Demonstration 3 — Drive replacement and clone/install (5 minutes)
Say: "This is the upgrade customers love."

2. Power off, unplug, discharge, strap on.
3. Disconnect the SATA data and SATA power cables from the old drive. Point them out clearly.
4. Remove the screws, slide out the old drive, fit the new SSD, secure it, reconnect the SATA data and power cables.

Common errors to show and correct:
Not backing up before starting. Say: "If the clone fails and we wiped nothing yet, the old drive still has the data. But if we

Connecting the data cable to the wrong port or leaving the power cable off.
Handling the HDD roughly.
Questions: "When would a fresh install be better than cloning?" (When the old Windows is full of junk/malware, or the source

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mr. Okafor runs a small cyber café and business centre near a school in Port Harcourt. He has five desktop PCs
used for typing, printing and browsing. They are about six years old: each has 4 GB RAM and a 500 GB mechanical hard drive.
Customers complain that the machines take "forever" to start and freeze when two people use them. Mr. Okafor is considering
buying five new computers, which he cannot really afford.
Relevant facts.
The hard drives are healthy (CrystalDiskInfo: Good) — they are not faulty, just slow (old mechanical technology).
The machines are short of RAM for modern browsing and office work.
The CPUs and other parts are still serviceable.
A few machines were recently diagnosed with malware, now cleaned.
The decision point. Buy five new computers, or upgrade the five old ones?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy five brand-new desktop computers (expensive).
2. Upgrade each machine: add matching RAM and replace the HDD with a SATA SSD (clone the existing system so nothing is
relearned).
3. Do nothing and tell customers to be patient (bad for business).
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. For each machine: check compatibility (DDR generation, SATA ports),
add a matching RAM upgrade, and clone each HDD onto a SATA SSD. The machines will start in seconds instead of minutes,
and the RAM upgrade stops the freezing. The cost is a small fraction of five new computers, and each upgrade is a job Mr.
Okafor can even pay a CEA graduate to do.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 solves everything but costs many times more; the old CPUs would still be fine for café work.
Approach 2 is affordable, dramatic in results, and preserves the existing setups; it requires care (compatibility, backup,
cloning) and takes time per machine.
Approach 3 loses customers.
Professional connection. This is exactly how a real repair business grows: offering a cost-effective upgrade that gives the
customer most of the benefit of a new machine at a fraction of the price. The certificate holder who can correctly choose
compatible parts and clone a drive is the person a cyber café owner trusts with five machines.
Transfer exercise (ask the class): "Mr. Okafor asks, 'Can I just put a bigger HDD instead of an SSD?' What do you tell him, and
why?"
Suggested answer: "Yes, a bigger HDD will give you more space, but it will not fix the slowness — an HDD is still mechanical.
For speed, the drive type matters more than the size. An SSD of the same or smaller capacity will make the machines far faster. If
you need both speed and space, use an SSD for Windows and programs and keep a large HDD for storing files."

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the session's live troubleshooting guide.
K.1 Machine will not power on after a replacement
Symptoms: no lights, no fans after closing up and pressing power.
Likely causes: the 24-pin or CPU power cable is loose or not reconnected; the PSU switch is off; the wall socket/cable is
faulty; a cable was left disconnected during the swap.
How to investigate: re-open and check the 24-pin and CPU power connectors are fully seated; check the PSU switch and
power cable; compare against your photo/notes.
Safe corrective steps: reseat connectors; use a working socket/cable. Never open the PSU.
How to confirm resolved: the machine POSTs and boots.
Prevention: photograph every cable before removal; double-check all connections before power-on.
K.2 RAM not detected / wrong amount shown
Symptoms: the boot fails, beeps, or Windows shows less memory than fitted.
Likely causes: the stick is not fully seated; wrong orientation (notch); wrong generation; a faulty stick; or the OS is 32-bit
and cannot use the full amount.
How to investigate: reseat and press until both clips click; confirm generation; check the OS type (32/64-bit) in Windows.
Safe corrective steps: reseat correctly; replace a faulty stick; explain 32-bit limits to the customer.
How to confirm resolved: Task Manager shows the expected total (minus a small normally-reserved amount).
Prevention: match generation and type on the Compatibility Check Sheet first.
K.3 New drive not detected
Symptoms: BIOS/Windows does not see the new drive.
Likely causes: SATA data or power cable not connected; loose cable; a bad SATA port; a dead drive (rare, if known-good).
How to investigate: check both cables; try a different SATA port; try a known-good cable.
Safe corrective steps: reconnect both cables fully; change port/cable.
How to confirm resolved: the drive appears in BIOS and in Windows Disk Management.
Prevention: label the data vs power cable; push each connector fully home.
K.4 Machine boots to "no operating system" after fitting a blank drive
Symptoms: POST passes, but there is no Windows.
Likely cause: the new drive is blank (expected if not cloned/no OS installed).
How to investigate: confirm the drive is the new one and is empty of an OS.
Safe corrective steps: clone the old drive to the new one, or install Windows (Session 9) and restore data.
How to confirm resolved: Windows boots from the new drive.
Prevention: plan clone vs install before fitting; back up first.
K.5 Random shutdowns return after fitting a new PSU
Symptoms: machine still shuts down under load after a PSU swap.
Likely causes: the new PSU is underpowered for the load; a connector is loose; the real cause was overheating or RAM, not
the PSU.
How to investigate: check the PSU wattage vs the machine's needs; reseat all connectors; re-check temperatures
(HWMonitor) and RAM (memory test).
Safe corrective steps: fit a correctly-rated PSU; fix the actual cause.
How to confirm resolved: the machine runs a heavy load without shutting down.
Prevention: size the PSU correctly; diagnose thoroughly before replacing (Session 5).
K.6 Temperatures worse after re-pasting
Symptoms: CPU temperature higher than before re-pasting.
Likely causes: too little paste; the cooler is not seated evenly; the fan cable is not reconnected; the cooler is loose.
How to investigate: check the cooler is flat and tight; check the fan cable is on the CPU fan header; check the fan spins.
Safe corrective steps: reseat the cooler evenly; add a small correct amount of paste; reconnect the fan cable.
How to confirm resolved: HWMonitor shows normal/lower temperatures.
Prevention: even tightening; pea-sized amount; always reconnect the fan cable.
K.7 Distinguishing likely from confirmed
As always: a replacement is only confirmed good after verification (POST, OS detection, temperature/health reading, and a
short period of real use). Never hand a machine back based on "it powered on once".

Machine: CEA practice desktop, DDR4, SATA ports, no M.2 slot. Currently 4 GB RAM, 500 GB HDD (healthy), 64-bit
Windows. Compatibility sheet: RAM = DDR4, DIMM; storage = SATA; PSU needed ≈ (per practice-machine note)
modest, no dedicated GPU. RAM choice: add one 8 GB DDR4 DIMM of matching speed (total 12 GB). Compatible: correct
generation and desktop form factor; Windows is 64-bit so it will use the memory. Storage choice: 2.5-inch SATA SSD, 500
GB–1 TB. Compatible: the board has SATA ports; the drive fits a 2.5-inch bay (adapter if needed). Reason for speed: the
SSD removes the mechanical HDD bottleneck. Clone vs install: Clone — the customer wants everything unchanged; SSD
capacity chosen ≥ used space on the HDD. Backup to external drive first. Steps: 1) Back up data. 2) Power off, unplug,
discharge, wear wrist strap. 3) Open case; remove RAM, fit matching stick (notch, clips click). 4) Disconnect SATA data +

shows 12 GB (minus reserved); CrystalDiskInfo shows Good; boot and file-opening clearly faster; run design program +
browser together without freezing; open sample files to confirm the clone. Plain-language summary: "We will add more
memory so the computer can run several programs at once, and replace the old spinning hard drive with a modern solid-

begin, so nothing is lost."

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. What does it mean for a replacement part to be compatible? Q2. Multiple choice. A motherboard supports
DDR4 memory. Which RAM can you fit? a) DDR3 b) DDR4 c) DDR5 d) Any of them, RAM always fits Q3. Explain in your own
words. Why does replacing an old mechanical hard drive with a solid-state drive make a computer faster, even though the CPU
and RAM are unchanged? Q4. Practical instruction. What are the two safety must-dos before you open a computer to replace a
part? And what is the one thing you must never open? Q5. Short answer. Give one reason to clone a drive and one reason to
do a fresh install instead. Q6. Scenario. After replacing RAM, the machine beeps and shows nothing on screen. What is the
most likely cause, and what do you do? (Remember the golden rule about forcing.)
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. It means the part is of the correct type and size to work with that specific machine — matching, for example, RAM
generation/form factor, storage interface, or PSU wattage and connectors — so it will fit, connect and work (and not damage
anything).
A2. (b) DDR4. DDR3 and DDR5 are different shapes and cannot be fitted.
A3. An HDD must physically spin and move a reading head to find data, so every read — and Windows makes thousands of
small reads when starting up and opening files — waits for mechanical parts. An SSD has no moving parts and finds data
almost instantly. Removing that mechanical wait is what makes the machine feel new; the CPU and RAM did not change.
A4. The two must-dos: power off and unplug (and press power to discharge), and wear an anti-static wrist strap (ESD
protection). The one thing you must never open is the PSU (power supply) — its capacitors hold a lethal charge even when
unplugged.
A5. Clone: when the old drive is healthy and the customer wants Windows, programs and files kept exactly as they are (saves
reinstallation). Fresh install: when the old Windows is full of junk/malware, or the old drive is failing and barely readable, so a
clean start is safer.
A6. Most likely the RAM stick is not fully seated, or it is the wrong type/orientation. Do not force it. Power off and unplug,
reseat the stick correctly (check the notch and press until both clips click), confirm it is the correct generation, then power on
and re-check.
Marking: award for correct concept; deduct heavily for unsafe answers (opening the PSU, forcing parts).

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned to replace components safely — the skill that turns a diagnosis into a repair. You
learned that every part must be compatible: RAM must match the motherboard's generation (DDR3/4/5), type (DIMM for
desktop, SO-DIMM for laptop), size and speed; storage must match the interface (SATA uses cables, NVMe uses an M.2 slot);
and a PSU must have enough wattage and the right connectors. You learned to upgrade RAM by seating it on the notch until
the clips click; to replace a drive and choose between cloning (copy everything) and a fresh install (clean start) — always
backing up first; to replace a PSU by reconnecting every cable and double-checking, while never opening it; and to re-apply
thermal paste with a pea-sized amount. Finally, you learned that a replacement is only done when the machine is verified
working.
Five important takeaways:
1. Compatibility first — matching saves money and prevents damage.
2. An SSD removes the mechanical bottleneck and makes old PCs feel new.
3. Back up before replacing storage; choose clone vs fresh install on purpose.
4. Never open a PSU; replace it and double-check every cable.
5. Verify every replacement before returning the machine.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I check whether a RAM stick is compatible before buying it?
Can I fit RAM and confirm the new amount in Windows?
Can I choose between clone and fresh install and explain why?
Can I replace a PSU safely and reconnect every cable?
Can I re-paste a CPU and confirm the temperature improved?

## O. INSTRUCTOR EVALUATION NOTES (instructor-only)
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner checks compatibility (generation, interface, wattage) before fitting anything.
The learner seats RAM by the notch and confirms both clips are up, without forcing.
The learner correctly identifies SATA data vs power cables and reconnects both.
The learner chooses clone or fresh install with a clear reason and backs up first.
The learner reconnects every PSU cable and double-checks before power-on.
The learner verifies (POST, OS detection, CrystalDiskInfo, temperature) before declaring success.
The learner automatically follows ESD and power-off safety and refuses to open a PSU.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Any RAM fits any PC." → DDR3/4/5 are different shapes; DIMM ≠ SO-DIMM.
"More RAM always makes it faster." → Only if the machine is short of memory.
"An SSD cannot fail." → It can; back up regardless.
"Cloning always works." → It can fail; back up first.
"A bigger PSU is always more reliable." → Wattage is capacity, not health.
"More thermal paste is better." → A pea-sized amount is correct.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Compatibility sorting: give ten part cards and ten machine cards; have the learner match them and explain each match.
2. Cable tracing: on a real machine, have the learner point to and name SATA data, SATA power, 24-pin, CPU power and PCIe
power connectors.
3. Order-the-steps drill: shuffle the RAM and PSU replacement steps and have the learner reorder them, including the safety
steps.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed Compatibility Check Sheet and step card; the learner completes the
RAM upgrade with hands-on help.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written (RAM + drive).
Level 3 (challenge): perform the full clone to an SSD and the RAM upgrade, then verify both; or replace a PSU and GPU as
well, documenting every step on the service log.
Timing guidance: This is a hands-on, safety-critical session. Protect the guided practical above all. If time is short, do the RAM
upgrade as the core practical and demonstrate the drive replacement while learners watch; move the PSU swap to an instructor-
supervised demonstration only. Never rush a safety step. Cloning can be slow — start it early or demonstrate the process and
let learners complete it in Session 9 if needed.
