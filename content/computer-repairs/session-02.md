# Safety and Tools

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module: Components and Safety

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes (1 hour 45 minutes)

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 — Computer Components. The learner must already be able to name the major components. Connection to previous session Session 1 named the parts. This session teaches how to be safe around those parts: electrical safety, ESD (electrostatic discharge) protection, the correct tools, and a safe workspace. Connection to the course Every later session — disassembly (3), diagnostics (4–6), repair (7–8), the capstone (12) — requires these safety habits and tool skills. This is the session that makes the rest of the course possible without injury or damage.

**Preparation before class:** Tool kits per learner/group (Phillips PH1 and PH2, flathead, tweezers, torch, needle-nose pliers); anti-static wrist straps (one per learner); anti-static mat; safety glasses; a workspace table; a powered-off desktop to demonstrate on; printed Safety Procedures Checklist and Tool Identification Chart; a balloon for the opening ESD demonstration; optional multimeter (demonstration only). Safety note for this session: This session is about safety, so model it perfectly. Nothing is opened on mains power. The demonstration machine must be unplugged and discharged before any handling. The PSU is never opened. Learners must not test anything on a live socket. The multimeter is used only by the instructor on a low-voltage example (for example a battery) and never on mains wiring. If a learner has any medical condition that makes electrical work risky, speak to them privately and provide alternative observation roles.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain ESD (electrostatic discharge) in plain words, describe how it damages components, and explain why damage can
be invisible.
2. Demonstrate the correct ESD-safe routine — wearing and connecting an anti-static wrist strap, working on an anti-
static mat, and touching bare metal before handling parts.
3. Apply electrical safety rules — unplugging from the wall, discharging residual electricity, never working inside a powered
machine, and never opening the PSU.
4. Identify and correctly name the common computer repair tools, and state what each is used for.
5. Use a screwdriver of the correct type and size and explain why the wrong size damages screws.
6. Set up a safe, organised workspace with good lighting, screw organisation and safe handling.
7. Practise safe lifting and handling of computers and components.
8. State clearly when a repair is beyond their skill level and must be referred to a qualified technician or supervisor.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"The Invisible Danger"
Instructor setup: Stand near a light switch or the front of the room. Hold a balloon. Rub it briskly on your hair or on a woollen
cloth for several seconds. In a dark corner, you may even see tiny sparks. Then hold the balloon near a small, light piece of paper
or your hair to show attraction.
What the instructor says:
"Watch this. [Rub the balloon.] I have just made electricity — but not from a socket. I made it by rubbing. You cannot see
it on the balloon, but it is there. Now watch what it does to paper. [Bring the balloon near the paper; it jumps.] That
invisible thing is called static electricity.
Now here is the serious part. Today, when we rub things together — our clothes, a plastic bag, our shoes on a carpet —
we build up this same invisible charge on our bodies. It can be thousands of volts. When you touch something metal, that
charge jumps across in a spark. Usually you feel a tiny shock, or nothing at all.
But inside a computer, that same invisible spark — one you cannot even feel — can kill a chip. Dead. And you will not
find out until weeks later, when the part fails for 'no reason.'
We call this danger ESD — electrostatic discharge. It is the number-one silent killer of computer parts in Nigeria and
everywhere. And there is more than one danger today: plain electricity from the wall, tools used wrongly, and lifting
machines carelessly.
Today you learn how to touch a computer without harming it — or yourself. This is the most important session in the whole
course, because if you are not safe, your skills do not matter."
Then show the invisible damage: Hold up the dead board labelled "FAULT — DEMO ONLY." Say:
"This board looks perfectly fine, does it not? Shiny. Clean. No burn marks. And yet it is dead. Static electricity leaves no
mark. That is why we do not rely on our eyes — we rely on our habits."
Expected learner responses: Learners are often surprised that ESD damage is invisible and that they may have killed parts
before without knowing. Some may say "but my phone works when I touch it" — accept this and explain that phones and
finished products are more protected than exposed circuit boards during repair. Some may mention carpets at home. Praise
that, because carpet is a real risk.
Why this opener works: It makes an invisible, abstract danger physical and memorable using a simple balloon, and it
immediately connects to the expensive consequences (dead parts, lost money). It also previews the whole session's theme:
safety first.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a quick oral review. Ask different learners each time.
Question 1: "Name at least six components inside a desktop computer and say what one of them does."
Expected answer: Motherboard, CPU, RAM, HDD/SSD, PSU, GPU (and fans, connectors). Any correct function.
Common gap: learners forget the cooling fan or connectors. Remind them that the fan is a component too.
Bridge: "Today you will not just name these parts — you will learn to handle them without killing them."
Question 2: "Which component must we never open, and why?"
Expected answer: The PSU (power supply). It can hold a dangerous charge even when unplugged.
Common gap: learners think "unplugged means safe" for everything. Clarify: unplugging makes the machine safe to work
in except inside the PSU, where a stored charge can remain; that is why we never open it.
Bridge: "That rule starts today and stays with you for life."
Question 3: "How should you hold a RAM stick, and why?"
Expected answer: By the edges; do not touch the gold contacts, because finger oils damage the connection.
Common gap: learners may not know why. Explain the oil problem.
Bridge: "Correct handling is part of being safe — safe for the component. Today we also learn safe for you."
Question 4: "What is the difference between RAM and storage?"
Expected answer: RAM is temporary, empties on power-off; storage is permanent.
Common gap: still mixing the two. Correct briefly; it comes up again in the practical.
Bridge: "Keep this clear, because today's case study involves a mistake with physical parts, not memory."
Question 5: "Have you ever received a small electric shock touching a car door or a metal tap? What caused it?"
Expected answer: Static electricity discharging.
Common gap: some say "faulty wiring." Clarify the difference between harmless static and dangerous mains electricity —
both are real, but they behave differently, and today we handle both.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Think about a hospital operating theatre. The surgeon has the best skills, but none of that matters if the equipment is
not sterile and the theatre is not safe. A computer repair bench is our operating theatre. Today we set the rules and the
tools so that every repair we do later is safe — for us and for the machine. Safety is not a lecture before the real work;
safety is the real work."
If more than a third cannot answer Question 2 or 3, spend an extra three minutes re-teaching the PSU rule and the handling rule
before continuing, since both are safety-critical.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 ESD — the invisible killer
Plain-English definition. ESD (electrostatic discharge) is the sudden jump of a small electric charge from one object to
another when they touch. It is the same little "snap" you feel when you touch a car door or a metal gate after walking on a rug.
Why it happens. When two different materials rub together (your shoes and carpet, your shirt and a chair, a plastic bag and
your hand), tiny charges build up on your body. Your body becomes like a tiny battery. When you then touch something that
conducts electricity — a metal object, or a circuit board — the charge jumps across in an instant.
How it damages components. Computer chips and boards are designed for very small voltages. A static discharge that you
cannot even feel can carry a voltage far higher than a chip can survive. Two things can happen:
1. Immediate damage: the chip dies instantly. The machine fails right away.
2. Latent (delayed) damage: the chip is weakened but still works. Weeks or months later, it fails "for no reason." This is the
most dangerous kind, because you may blame the wrong part or the wrong technician.
Why you cannot rely on your eyes or your senses. A static shock that you can feel is only a small portion of the danger. You
can damage a component with a discharge you never feel and never see. As the demonstration showed, a dead board can
look perfect.
Important limitation — do not invent numbers. It is tempting to say "10 volts kills a chip" or "your body holds 3,000 volts."
Different sources quote different figures, and the exact numbers vary with the situation. The safe, honest teaching is this: static
electricity from ordinary daily activity can cause voltages far beyond what a chip can survive, even when you feel nothing.
Teach the principle and the habit, not a made-up exact figure.
Real-life comparison. ESD is like sand in an engine. One grain is nothing; you cannot always see it going in; but over time, or at
the wrong moment, it destroys the engine. You never allow sand near an engine, even though it looks harmless. In the same
way, you never allow static near a board, even though you cannot see it.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A technician in a Port Harcourt shop reseats a customer's RAM on a hot, dry afternoon while
standing on a tiled floor and wearing a synthetic shirt. Three weeks later the customer returns: the same machine now crashes
randomly. The RAM had a latent ESD failure from that day. The technician blamed the customer. The truth is that a 30-second
habit — wearing the strap — would have prevented it. This is why ESD is taught before any tool is picked up.
Common misconceptions.
"ESD is not real; I have never destroyed anything." You may have — you just never connected the failure to your handling.
Invisible damage is the whole problem.
"Living in a humid climate, I am safe." Humidity reduces static buildup but does not remove the risk. Port Harcourt's
humidity helps, but strap up anyway.
"Only cheap parts are at risk." All exposed boards are at risk; expensive ones hurt the most when they die.
Safety implications. ESD protection is a rule, not an option. No learner handles a board without the routine in F.2. A component
that a learner is unsure about should be handled only under supervision.
F.2 How to prevent ESD — the routine
Principles. The aim is to keep your body at the same electrical level as the machine, so that no charge jumps when you touch it.
This is called grounding or bonding yourself to the equipment.
The ESD-safe routine (teach learners as a fixed sequence):
1. Wear an anti-static wrist strap. It is a band that goes on your wrist with a coiled wire that clips to bare metal of the
computer case (or the anti-static mat). It slowly and safely drains any static from your body.
2. Work on an anti-static mat where possible. The mat is designed to carry charge away safely rather than let it build up.
3. Touch bare metal of the case before handling any component — every time, and again each time you come back to the
machine after moving around. Unpainted metal, not the plastic or painted panels.
4. Handle all boards by their edges. Never touch gold contacts, chips or pins.
5. Avoid building up static: do not shuffle on carpets, do not rub balloons or plastic, do not work in socks on a synthetic rug,
and avoid wearing loose woollen or synthetic clothing if possible.
6. Keep components in anti-static bags when they are not installed. The shiny grey or pink bags you receive parts in are
antistatic; do not throw them away.
7. Never place a board on a carpet, a foam mattress, a plastic tablecloth or a metal surface. Use the mat, or the antistatic
bag, or the original box.
Why the wrist strap must be clipped to the case, not to a wall socket. The strap grounds you to the equipment you are
working on, so that you and the equipment are at the same level. Never, ever clip an anti-static strap or any wire to a mains
socket or a live conductor — that is extremely dangerous. The strap is a safety tool, not a power wire.
Real-life comparison. Wearing the strap is like sharing a plate of food with a friend — you are both at the same table, so
nothing "jumps" between you. Without it, you are like a person holding a live electric fence and then touching your friend; the
spark finds the path.
Common misconceptions.
"I only need the strap when I am touching RAM." No — any board, and every time, including when you first sit down.
"A cloth or aluminium foil works as well." No. Use a proper anti-static strap and mat.
"If I keep the machine plugged in, it is grounded." Partly true for some systems, but the safe rule for beginners is: unplug
the machine first, then use the strap. Never assume that being plugged in makes you safe — it can make you less safe.
Limitations and safety. The strap protects equipment, not from mains electricity. It must be worn in addition to unplugging,
never as a substitute. A damaged strap (torn wire, broken clip) must be replaced.
F.3 Electrical safety — the rules that keep you alive
Plain-English idea. Inside the computer, the PSU and some parts use mains-derived electricity (from the wall, around 220–240
V in Nigeria). Touching certain parts while the machine is live can give a serious or fatal shock. The rules below are absolute.
The rules:
1. Always unplug the power cable from the wall before opening the case. Pull the plug out, do not just switch off at the
socket — switch off first to be safer, then pull the plug.
2. Press the power button once after unplugging. This is called discharging residual electricity. Some parts (especially
capacitors) can hold a small stored charge for a short time. Pressing the power button with the plug removed helps drain it
so there is no surprise.
3. Never work inside a powered-on computer. Not even to "look." Not even to "just plug the fan." If the machine is on, stop
and switch off.
4. Never open the PSU. It holds a dangerous charge even when unplugged, and mending it is beyond this course. If it is
faulty, replace it — with supervision as you learn.
5. Never use metal tools near live circuits. No jewellery, no loose metal, no tools resting inside a live machine. Remove
rings, watches and metal bangles before working.
6. Keep liquids away. Water and electricity do not mix. No drinks on the bench; if there is a spill, switch off at the wall before
doing anything.
7. If you smell burning, see smoke, or hear a loud pop — stop. Switch off / unplug from a safe distance if possible, do not
touch the machine, and call the instructor. Never move or open a burning or smoking machine that is still plugged in.
8. Know the emergency switch. Point out the room's main switch or fuse board to every learner. In an emergency, cut the
power there — do not grab the machine.
9. Watch the cables. A trailing power cable or a laptop charger is a trip hazard. Keep the floor clear.
10. Know your limits. If a job involves mains wiring, the inside of the PSU, wall sockets or anything you are not confident about
— stop and refer it to a qualified electrician or technician. No repair is worth your life.
Real-life comparison. Electricity in a computer is like a caged lion. While the cage is closed (unplugged), the lion is still real and
still dangerous if you poke into the wrong cage (the PSU), but the area around it is safe. Once the cage opens (live power),
everything changes. Our rule is simple: we work only when the lion is asleep and the area is checked.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A technician in a hurry opens a case to swap a hard drive while the machine is still plugged in,
intending to "just be quick." His metal wristwatch touches the exposed PSU connector. The result is a burn and a ruined
machine. Ten seconds of unplugging would have prevented it. This is a true-to-life pattern — hurry causes more injuries than
difficulty.
Common misconceptions.
"The power switch on the PSU or the case makes it safe." No — always pull the plug from the wall and discharge.
"Low voltage = no danger." The motherboard and internal parts run on low voltage, but the PSU input side and mains
cable are high voltage, and stored charges can still bite. Respect the whole machine.
"I will just hold the tool carefully." Caution does not replace unplugging. The plug always comes out first.
Limitations and safety. This course never asks you to work on mains wiring or inside a PSU. Those are outside scope and
dangerous. When in doubt, stop and ask the instructor.
F.4 The correct tools — and what each one is for
Why tools matter. The right tool makes a job safe and clean; the wrong tool strips screws, damages boards and causes injury. A
professional technician's skills begin with good habits with basic tools.
1. Screwdrivers — the most important tool.
Phillips screwdriver (cross-shaped tip). Used for most computer screws. Sizes PH1 and PH2 cover most jobs; PH2 is the
everyday size for case screws. Using the correct size matters: too small, and you slip and chew up the screw head; too large,
and it will not seat. A magnetised tip helps hold screws in tight corners.
Flathead screwdriver (flat blade). Used for prying open plastic clips and gentle levering, and for some slotted screws. Use
with great care near boards — the flat blade can slip and scratch or cut circuit tracks. Never use a flathead as a chisel or to
force a screw.
Precision (small) screwdrivers. Needed for some laptops and small screws. If you do not have the correct size, do not force
it — get the right driver.
Why screw size and type matter (teach this clearly). Computer screws come in different lengths and thread types. A long
screw forced into a short hole can damage the board. Always keep screws organised (see workspace) and always match screw to
hole.
2. Tweezers. Fine-point tweezers are used to retrieve dropped screws, hold small jumpers and connectors, and place tiny screws
in awkward spots. Always use them with the machine unplugged, and never force a screw out with tweezers — you may bend
the board.
3. Torch / flashlight. A small torch is essential for seeing inside a dark case, checking for dust, and reading labels. It also helps
spot a bent pin or a loose cable.
4. Needle-nose pliers. Used for gripping, straightening and bending in tight spaces, e.g., carefully retrieving a part, or holding a
nut while you turn a screw. Never use pliers to force a screw head — that is how screws get destroyed. Insulated handles are
safer.
5. Anti-static wrist strap and mat. Already covered — the ESD tools. Treat them as tools you use every single time.
6. Compressed air (canned air). Used to blow dust out of heatsinks, fans and vents. Important safety: hold the can upright,
never tilt it (liquid propellant can spray out and chill or damage parts), keep a safe distance, do not spin a fan with the air jet
(which can over-speed and damage the bearing), and wear safety glasses and use it in a well-ventilated area (ideally
outdoors) because dust can be harmful to breathe. Do not use a vacuum cleaner on electronics — it can create static.
7. Isopropyl alcohol (90% or higher). Used to clean gold contacts, remove old thermal paste, and clean stubborn grime. It
evaporates quickly and leaves no residue. Do not use water, and do not use petrol or kerosene. Use on a lint-free cloth or a
cotton bud.
8. Microfibre / lint-free cloth. Used for cleaning screens, contacts and surfaces without leaving fibres.
9. Thermal paste. A grey compound applied between the CPU and its heatsink. Only used when a cooler is removed and
refitted (Session 8). Not a beginner's first tool, but you should recognise it and know its purpose.
10. Cable ties / velcro straps. Used to tidy cables so air can flow and the machine looks professional. Good cable management
is part of good work.
11. Screw tray / container (or an egg carton). Used to keep screws organised and separated by location, so you can put each
one back in the right place.
12. Multimeter (instructor demonstration only). A multimeter measures voltage, current and resistance. Professionals use it to
test a PSU's output and check continuity. In this course, the multimeter is demonstrated by the instructor only, on a safe
low-voltage example (such as a battery), never on mains wiring. Beginners do not test live circuits. Recognising the tool and
knowing its purpose is the goal for now.
Real-life comparison. Tools are like a carpenter's plane and chisels. The right tool for the right job produces clean work; using
a chisel as a hammer ruins the wood and the tool. In computer repair, a wrong-size screwdriver is the most common self-
inflicted damage.
Common misconceptions.
"Any screwdriver will do." No — the wrong size strips screws and damages boards.
"A bigger screwdriver gives more power." No — it gives more damage. Match the size.
"A vacuum cleaner is fine for dust." No — it can generate static and zap parts. Use compressed air, carefully.
"Water cleans contacts." No — isopropyl alcohol only.
Limitations and safety. Do not use damaged tools (rounded tips, cracked handles, loose heads). Do not use a tool you do not
understand. Ask before using anything unfamiliar. Keep tools dry and away from liquids.
F.5 Workspace setup and organisation
Why it matters. A safe workspace prevents accidents and mistakes. On a cluttered, badly lit bench, screws vanish, cables get
mixed, and slips happen.
A good computer-repair workspace has:
1. A clean, flat, sturdy table at a comfortable height (so you are not stooping).
2. An anti-static mat under the work.
3. Good lighting — a desk lamp plus overhead light, so you can see into the case.
4. A screw tray or egg carton, ideally with the compartments labelled by location (case, motherboard, drive, bracket).
5. Tools laid out within reach, not scattered where you might knock them into the case.
6. Enough free space to lay out removed components safely, ideally on the antistatic mat or on antistatic bags.
7. A hard floor or a non-static mat under your feet; avoid working directly on carpet if possible.
8. A container for loose screws and small parts, so nothing rolls onto the floor and into a fan.
9. Good ventilation if you will be using compressed air or cleaning fluids.
10. No food or drink on the bench.
Real-life comparison. Setting up the bench is like a surgeon preparing the operating theatre before the patient arrives.
Everything must be in its place, clean and ready, before the delicate work begins.
Common misconceptions.
"Organisation slows me down." The opposite — it speeds you up, because you are not hunting for screws or re-opening a
case.
"Screws are all the same, one pile is fine." Different screws go in different places. One mixed pile means wrong screws in
wrong holes and possible damage.
F.6 Safe lifting and handling
Why it matters. Computers and monitors can be heavy and awkward, and many benches are at the wrong height. Back injuries
are a real workplace hazard.
Rules for safe lifting and handling:
1. Assess the weight before lifting. A desktop tower or a CRT monitor can be heavier than expected.
2. Bend your knees, keep your back straight, and lift with your legs, not your back.
3. Hold the load close to your body — do not stretch out your arms.
4. Get help for heavy or awkward items — two people for big monitors or full towers.
5. Plan your path — make sure the way is clear of cables and obstacles before you lift.
6. Place the machine down gently — never drop it on the bench.
7. For components: handle by the edges, support large cards and boards with two hands, and never carry a hard drive loosely
where it can be knocked.
8. Keep the bench tidy as you go, so you do not trip over tools or cables.
Real-life comparison. Lifting a computer is like lifting a bag of cement. If you bend at the waist and jerk it up, your back pays.
If you bend your knees and lift smoothly, you stay safe.
Common misconceptions.
"It is small, so it is light." Some small items (a heavy GPU, a full tower PSU) are deceptively heavy. Assess first.
"I can carry three things at once to save a trip." That is how things get dropped. Take one at a time on the bench.
F.7 Working only on machines you are authorised to repair
Plain-English rule. A technician must only work on equipment they have been given permission to open and repair. This
protects the customer's property, the machine's warranty, and you.
Why it matters:
A machine may still be under a warranty; opening it yourself can void the warranty and cause the customer to lose money.
A machine may belong to a school, church, company or government office, where only approved staff may open it.
Opening it without permission can cost you and the academy serious trouble.
A customer's data is private. You must not browse their files, copy them, or share them. You only work on what is needed to
fix the fault.
If you damage an unauthorised machine, you may be personally liable.
The rule in practice:
1. Get the owner's permission before opening any machine.
2. Record what you received and its condition (with the intake process taught in Session 10).
3. Never open a machine labelled as belonging to someone else without the instructor's instruction.
4. Never plug a customer's drive into your own computer to "look" at their files without a good, authorised reason.
5. If you are unsure whether you are allowed, ask before you touch.
Real-life comparison. It is like a church keyboard or the school's projector. You may use it, but you do not open it and take it
apart unless the person in charge says you may. Respect for others' property is both professional and legal.
Common misconceptions.
"It is a broken machine, nobody will mind." Someone owns it. Always get permission.
"The customer said 'fix it,' so I can do anything." Fixing it does not mean rifling through private files or changing things they
did not ask for.

G.1 What the instructor prepares
A clean bench with the anti-static mat, the powered-off desktop, the full tool kit laid out, the screw tray, and safety glasses.

The balloon (from the opener) and the dead board.

G.2 Demonstration 1 — the ESD-safe routine (6 minutes)
Say: "I am going to handle a component the safe way. Copy my habits exactly, because these are the habits that will protect
every computer you ever touch."
Do this slowly:
1. "First, I unplug the computer from the wall. I do not just switch it off — I pull the plug out."
2. "Then I press the power button once. This discharges residual electricity, like letting the last water drain from a pipe."
3. "Now I put on my anti-static wrist strap and clip it to bare metal on the case."
4. "I touch the metal of the case once more before I pick up any part."
5. "I pick up the RAM by its edges. My fingers never touch the gold contacts."
6. "If I walk away and come back, I touch the metal again before resuming."
7. "When a part is not installed, it lives in an anti-static bag, never on the carpet or a plastic sheet."
Show the wrong way, then correct it (hands-on contrast):
Ruffle your hair, then pretend to grab a board by its contacts. Freeze. "Wrong." Now do it the right way. "Right."
Ask: "Why do we clip the strap to the case and not to anything else?" Expected: to keep us and the machine at the same
electrical level — and never to a socket, which would be deadly.
Expected result: Learners can recite and copy the seven-step routine.
Common errors to watch: clipping the strap to a wall socket (stop at once), touching the case's painted surface instead of bare
metal, forgetting to re-touch metal after walking away.
G.3 Demonstration 2 — tools, one by one (6 minutes)
Say: "Now, the tools of the trade. For each one, I will hold it up, name it, say what it is for, and say the main danger."
Hold up and explain, in this order:
1. Phillips PH2 — "Most computer screws. The danger is using the wrong size and stripping the screw."
2. Phillips PH1 — "Smaller screws. Match the size to the head."

7. Wrist strap — "ESD protection — every single time."
8. Compressed air — "For dust. Upright, never tilt; wear glasses; ventilate; do not spin the fan with the jet."
9. Isopropyl alcohol — "For contacts and thermal paste. Never water."
10. Multimeter — "For measuring voltage. I demonstrate it only on a low-voltage battery. We never test mains wiring."

Task: "The Safety Audit"
Scenario: You are now a junior technician at Elias Computer Clinic, Diobu, Port Harcourt. Your supervisor asks you to inspect

Mark all ten SAFE/UNSAFE.
Give the correction for every unsafe item.

Write one sentence explaining why your top rule matters most.
Constraints:
Use correct safety language: ESD, residual electricity, unplug, ground, anti-static.
Keep each correction to one or two sentences.

Success criteria:
All ten situations correctly judged.
Corrections are accurate and specific.
The top rule captures the spirit of the session.
Hints if stuck:
Hint 1: Anything done while the machine is still plugged in is automatically unsafe.
Hint 2: Static comes from carpets, woollen clothes and rubbing — anything that builds charge.
Hint 3: The wrist strap grounds you to the machine, never to a socket.

1. UNSAFE. After unplugging, press the power button once to discharge residual electricity.
2. UNSAFE. Do not work on carpets or barefoot on wool; work on a hard floor or anti-static mat and wear the strap.
3. UNSAFE. The machine should be unplugged while working. The strap is clipped to the case for grounding — but the

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Tunde is a talented young technician in a busy Port Harcourt shop. He has good hands and learns fast, but he has
developed two dangerous shortcuts: he opens machines while they are still plugged in "to save time," and he rarely wears his
anti-static strap because "Port Harcourt is humid and it never causes problems." One Tuesday, a customer brings in a laptop to
have its RAM upgraded and its dust cleaned. Tunde opens the case with the charger still connected, drops a metal screwdriver
near the PSU area, and then, without a strap, grabs the RAM by its contacts. The machine boots after the upgrade. The customer
pays and leaves. Three weeks later, the customer returns furious: the laptop now shuts down randomly. Tunde insists the RAM
"must be faulty from the shop."
Relevant facts.
The charger was connected while Tunde worked (mains power present).
No anti-static strap was worn; the RAM was touched by its gold contacts.
A metal tool was used near a live PSU area.
The failure appeared weeks later — the classic timing of a latent fault (ESD or a poor contact), not a part that was "faulty
from the shop."
Decision points.
1. What are the probable causes of the weeks-later shutdown — and how would you confirm rather than guess?
2. Was the machine safe to open the way Tunde did it?
3. How should Tunde have handled the RAM?
4. Who is responsible for the failure, and what should the shop do for the customer?
Possible approaches.
1. Blame the RAM supplier and demand a replacement — but the evidence points elsewhere.
2. Re-open the machine safely, check the RAM seating and contacts, test the RAM properly, and inspect for damage. Honest
and methodical.
3. Continue as before, since "it has always worked" — this is how a reputation dies.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. A good technician would admit the machine was handled unsafely, re-open it
unplugged and with the strap, reseat the RAM cleanly (checking for oily contacts), test the memory properly (a skill from
Session 5), and confirm the fault. A latent ESD failure of the RAM stick is likely; the shop should replace the damaged part at the
shop's cost and apologise honestly. Crucially, the shop should then change its habits: always unplug, always strap up, never
bring metal tools near a live board.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is fast and shifts blame, but it is dishonest and will not fix the machine if the cause is a poor contact or latent
damage from handling.
Approach 2 is slower but honest, and it protects the customer and the shop's reputation. It also teaches the lesson.
Approach 3 is what actually causes shops to lose customers — word travels fast in Port Harcourt.
Professional connection. In the repair trade, your reputation is your business. A technician who is careful earns trust and
repeat customers; a technician who cuts corners saves seconds and loses customers. The two rules in this case — unplug, and
strap up — cost nothing and prevent nearly all of the serious damage a beginner can cause. This is why CEA teaches safety
before tools and tools before repair.
Transfer exercise. "Name two habits from this case that, if Tunde had followed them, would have prevented the fault, and
explain how each one helps." (Unplug — prevents shock and accidental short damage; strap and edge-handling — prevents
ESD damage and contact contamination.)
Safety reminder. This case is only to be investigated with the machine unplugged and discharged, with a strap, and under
supervision. Never open a machine that is on, never open the PSU, and never test live mains.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this as the live troubleshooting guide. Ask learners to find the relevant row rather than telling them the answer.
K.1 Working on a plugged-in machine
Symptoms: Possible electric shock, a spark, a sudden fan spin, or a part that dies. In the worst case, serious injury. Likely cause:
Rushing, or assuming "switched off" is the same as "unplugged." Caused by habit, not difficulty. How to investigate: There is
no safe investigation — the rule is prevention. Confirm the plug is out before work begins. Safe corrective steps: Stop
immediately. Unplug from the wall. Press the power button once to discharge. Only then continue. How to confirm resolved:
The machine is verified unplugged and discharged. How to prevent recurrence: Make "unplug and discharge" the first item
on every checklist.
K.2 Skipping the anti-static strap
Symptoms: No immediate sign. Weeks later, random failures, a part that "died for no reason." Likely cause: ESD damage, often
latent. Confirmed only indirectly — there is usually no visible mark. How to investigate: Look for the pattern (recent handling,
dry conditions) and test the suspect part properly (later sessions). You cannot see ESD damage. Safe corrective steps: Adopt
the routine: strap, mat, touch metal, hold by edges, antistatic bags. How to confirm resolved: Prevention, not repair — you
cannot undo ESD damage. How to prevent recurrence: Treat the strap as non-negotiable, every time.
K.3 Using the wrong screwdriver size
Symptoms: A screw that will not turn, a chewed-up screw head, or a screw stuck forever. Likely cause: Wrong driver size or type
(PH1 on a PH2 screw, flathead on a Phillips). How to investigate: Try the correct size; if it does not seat snugly, the head may
already be damaged. Safe corrective steps: Use the correct driver; if the head is rounded, use a proper screw extractor
(instructor-level) — do not keep forcing. How to confirm resolved: The screw turns cleanly with the correct driver. How to
prevent recurrence: Identify the screw type and match the driver before turning.
K.4 Touching gold contacts / mishandling boards
Symptoms: A part not detected, an upgrade that "does not work," intermittent faults. Likely cause: Finger oils on gold contacts,
or physical damage from bending. How to investigate: Inspect contacts; reseat holding only the edges. Safe corrective steps:
Clean contacts gently with isopropyl alcohol (unplugged, supervised) and reseat. How to confirm resolved: The part is
detected and works. How to prevent recurrence: Always hold by edges; never touch contacts.
K.5 Compressed air used wrongly
Symptoms: Liquid spray on the board, a part that suddenly chills, a fan bearing that fails later, or dust in the eyes/lungs. Likely
cause: Tilted can, too close, spinning the fan with the jet, or no eye protection/ventilation. How to investigate: Look for
residue; check the fan spins freely by hand (with the machine off). Safe corrective steps: Hold the can upright; keep distance;
hold the fan still; wear glasses; ventilate (ideally outdoors). How to confirm resolved: No residue; fan spins freely. How to
prevent recurrence: Follow the compressed-air safety rules every time.
K.6 Opening a machine you are not authorised to repair
Symptoms: A warranty voided, an angry owner, a data-privacy complaint, or damage you must pay for. Likely cause: No
permission obtained before opening. How to investigate: Check with the owner or instructor before touching. Safe corrective
steps: Stop, apologise, and get permission. Record the machine's condition. How to confirm resolved: You have documented
permission and the machine is handled properly. How to prevent recurrence: Always ask first — owner, warranty,
permission.
Distinguishing likely vs confirmed: Most safety problems are prevented, not diagnosed. Where a fault has already happened (a
dead board, a stripped screw), the "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only when you check it and the evidence matches. Teach
learners that in safety, the goal is to never need the diagnosis.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version. Answers are in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. What does ESD stand for, and why is it dangerous to computer parts?
Q2. Multiple choice. What is the correct first thing to do before opening a computer case? a) Put on safety glasses b) Unplug

Q3. Explain in your own words. Why can ESD damage be worse than damage you can see?
Q4. Practical identification. Name the tool and its purpose: (a) a cross-shaped screwdriver used for most computer screws; (b)
a fine pair of pincers for picking up dropped screws; (c) a small lamp used to see inside a dark case.
Q5. Error detection. A learner says: "After I unplug the computer, I do not need to press the power button, because no
electricity is flowing." Find the mistake and correct it.

INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. ESD = electrostatic discharge — a sudden jump of static electricity from your body (or another object) to a component. It
is dangerous because it can carry a voltage far higher than a chip can survive, and the damage can be invisible and delayed, so
the part may fail weeks later.
A2. (b) Unplug the power cable from the wall. (Then press the power button to discharge.)
A3. Visible damage (a burn, a crack) can be seen and dealt with immediately. ESD damage often leaves no visible mark and
may be latent — the part works at first and fails later, so the technician blames the wrong cause and the customer loses trust.
Invisible, delayed damage is the hard part.
A4. (a) Phillips screwdriver (PH1/PH2) — used for most computer screws; (b) tweezers — for retrieving small screws and
holding small parts; (c) torch/flashlight — for seeing inside the case.
A5. The mistake is believing that unplugging removes all stored charge instantly. Some parts (capacitors) can hold a residual
charge for a short time. The correction: after unplugging, press the power button once to help discharge residual electricity
before handling anything.
A6. Refuse to open it yourself and explain that opening a machine still under warranty may void the warranty, costing the
owner money. Advise them to take it to the warranty provider (or get the owner's documented permission and check the
terms). Getting permission and protecting the owner's property are the professional rules.
A7. Any two of: hold the can upright (never tilt); use in a well-ventilated area (ideally outdoors); wear safety glasses; keep a
safe distance; do not spin the fan with the jet; never use a vacuum cleaner on electronics.
Marking: up to 2 marks each (Q3, Q6 up to 3). Total ≈ 16; scale proportionally. Full credit requires correct names and
reasoning.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned how to work on computers without harming yourself or the machine. You learned
that ESD (electrostatic discharge) is an invisible danger that can damage parts without any sign, and how to prevent it with a
wrist strap, an anti-static mat, touching bare metal, handling boards by the edges, and using antistatic bags. You learned
the electrical safety rules: always unplug and discharge, never work on a live machine, and never open the PSU. You learned
the tools of the trade and what each is for, why the correct screwdriver size matters, and the safe use of compressed air and
isopropyl alcohol. You set up a safe, organised workspace, learned safe lifting and handling, and learned to work only on
machines you are authorised to repair.
Five important takeaways:
1. ESD is invisible and can kill parts silently — strap up every time.
2. Unplug from the wall and press the power button before opening any case.
3. Never open the PSU — replace it, never repair it.
4. Use the correct tool and the correct screwdriver size — the wrong one causes damage.
5. Know your limits and get permission — refer tricky or unauthorised jobs.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I demonstrate the ESD-safe routine from memory?
Can I explain in one sentence why ESD is dangerous?
Can I name at least eight tools and one danger per tool?
Can I state the rule about the PSU and the wall plug without hesitation?
Connection to the next session: You now know the parts and how to be safe around them. Next session, you put both skills
together: you will disassemble and reassemble a real desktop computer correctly — removing the panel, disconnecting
cables safely, extracting and refitting components, recording screws and cables, and verifying the machine powers on.
Everything you learned about safety today is used in every step of Session 3.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner unplugs and discharges without being reminded.
The learner puts on the wrist strap and touches bare metal as a habit before each session of handling.
The learner selects the correct screwdriver size by inspecting the screw first.
The learner handles boards by the edges and never touches contacts.
The learner can name all common tools and their purposes, with at least one danger each.
The learner states the PSU rule and the authorisation rule confidently.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Static electricity is not dangerous / Port Harcourt is too humid." → Humidity reduces but does not remove risk; strap up
anyway.
"Switched off means safe." → Only unplugged and discharged is safe.
"Any screwdriver will do." → The wrong size strips screws and damages boards.
"I can repair the PSU." → Never open it; replace it.
"If it is broken and off, I can open anyone's machine." → Get permission; respect warranty and data privacy.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Routine drill: have the learner perform the unplug-discharge-strap-touch routine five times until it is automatic.
2. Tool matching cards: match tool names to pictures and to uses, then to dangers.
3. Scenario sorting: give ten cards (safe/unsafe) and have the learner sort them into two piles, explaining each.
4. Screw-size drill: present several screws and drivers and have the learner match them correctly without forcing.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide the worksheets partially filled and walk the learner through the bench setup step by step; pair
with a steady-handed partner.
Level 2 (standard): complete the exercise and the Safety Audit independently.
Level 3 (challenge): research additional ESD/safety equipment (ESD gloves, ionisers, heel straps) and explain when each is
used; also explain the difference between ESD safety and mains safety in detail.
Timing guidance: Safety cannot be rushed. If the room is slow, compress the lecture sections F.5–F.7 into quick summaries,
but never shorten the ESD and electrical rules (F.1–F.3) or the practical routine (H). The practical demonstration of the correct
routine is the assessed priority.
