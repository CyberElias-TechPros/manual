# Service Reports and Documentation

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 4 — Module 4: Professional Practice

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (writing and reasoning skills)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–10 — the diagnostic workflow, hardware and software repairs, OS installation, and customer service (intake, plain language, estimates, approval).

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 10 you captured the fault and agreed the work with the customer. Today you close the loop: you document exactly what was found, what was done, what it cost and what you recommend. Writing the report is the last professional step of every repair — and it is the deliverable that proves you completed the capstone repair in Session 12.

**Preparation before class:** Prepare a printed "Service Report Template" (one per learner); prepare one strong sample report and one weak sample report for analysis; have the Section G scenario written up so learners can see a report being built; ensure Microsoft Word (or a plain text editor) is available. Professional note A service report is a permanent record that protects the customer, the technician and the shop. It must be accurate and honest — never exaggerate work, never inflate parts, never hide a mistake.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why service reports matter — proof of work, warranty, records and professionalism.
2. List and describe every required section of a professional service report.
3. Record the reported fault accurately in the customer's own words alongside the observed facts.
4. Document the diagnosis — tests performed, results, and the confirmed root cause.
5. Describe the action taken — parts used (with details), software installed, and work performed.
6. Record the tests performed after repair and the verified result.
7. Write clear, specific and honest language, avoiding vague phrases and unnecessary jargon.
8. Recommend preventive maintenance and next steps for the customer.
9. State costs, warranty terms and get the customer's signature on the finished report.
10. Handle customer data responsibly while keeping and storing records.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->

Software
Microsoft Word (or a simple text editor / Google Docs if available). If no computer is available for typing, a printed template

Internet
Not required. If available, the instructor may optionally show one real-world company's published service-report format as
inspiration near the end.
Alternative for learners who lack a facility
Learners without a laptop or Word can complete the entire session on paper. The instructor types a shared example on the
projector. Encourage neat, clear handwriting — legibility is part of professionalism.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: Display (or hold up) two service reports side by side — one strong, one weak. The weak one is messy: it says
"Fixed computer, customer happy", with no dates, no parts, no signature and handwriting all over. The strong one is clean and
complete.
What the instructor says:
"Look at these two papers. Both claim to be service reports for the same job. The one on the left says, 'Fixed computer,
customer happy. ₦25,000.' The one on the right lists the customer, the device, exactly what was wrong, the tests done, the
parts used, the total, the warranty and two signatures.
Now, I want you to answer three questions with me: One — if the customer comes back in one month and says the fault
returned, which report lets the shop prove what it did? … The right one. Two — if the technician is accused of damaging
the machine, which report tells the truth of what happened? … The right one. Three — if the shop's owner wants to know
how much money that week's parts cost, which report gives the answer? … The right one.
That piece of paper is not just paperwork. It is your protection, your memory and your professional name. Today you will
learn to write it properly, because this report is exactly what you will produce for your Final Project next session."
Expected learner response: Learners will quickly see the messy report is untrustworthy. Some may say "the good one is more
work". Acknowledge that it takes a few minutes more — and saves hours of argument later.
Why this opener works: It frames documentation as self-interest, not bureaucracy: the report protects the technician. That
motivation makes learners take the writing seriously.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a quick oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four steps of the customer intake process?"
Expected answer: greet, gather information, set expectations, document.
Common gap: learners forget the final "document" step. Point out that today is that step turned into a full document.
Question 2: "Why do we capture the fault in the customer's own words?"
Expected answer: their words contain clues and prove we listened; translation too early can mislead.
Common gap: learners think the technical finding is enough. Remind them the report must show both the reported fault
and the observed finding.
Question 3: "What did you learn to give the customer before starting work in Session 10?"
Expected answer: a clear written/WhatsApp estimate and approval.
Common gap: learners forget the approval line. The estimate and the service report are two sides of the same professional
habit.
Question 4: "What are the five steps of the diagnostic workflow?"
Expected answer: observe, ask, hypothesise, test, resolve/verify.
Common gap: learners skip "verify". The report must record the verification — the proof that the fix worked.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Look at everything today rides on. You already capture the fault, get approval, diagnose and verify. The service report
simply writes all of that down — in a clear, honest, standard format. Nothing new to invent: just record what you
already do, properly."
Gap handling: If more than a third of the class cannot name the verification step (Question 4), spend five extra minutes on why
we test after repair (open the machine, confirm the fault is gone, check no new faults). The report's "tests performed" and
"result" sections depend on this. Do not proceed until it is solid.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why service reports matter
The idea. A service report is a professional document that records what was reported, what was found, what was done, what
was tested and what the customer now owes — for a specific repair.
Plain-English definition. It is the written story of one repair, kept on record for the shop and the customer.
Why it exists. Repairs are done with hands and memory, and both are unreliable later. A report captures the facts while they are
fresh, in a form that anyone can read and trust.
What it does — five reasons it matters.
1. Proof of work. If a customer disputes the repair ("you never changed that part"), the report shows exactly what was done.
2. Warranty support. A clear record of the part, the date and the work is what makes a warranty claim possible.
3. A record for the future. A later technician can see what was already done, avoiding repeated or conflicting work.
4. Professionalism and trust. A clean, complete report tells the customer the shop is organised and honest.
5. Business information. Reports track parts used, costs and recurring problems — useful for pricing and for spotting a
customer whose machine keeps failing.
Everyday analogy. A service report is like a hospital patient's file. Each visit records symptoms, tests, diagnosis, treatment and
result. Without that file, every doctor starts from zero — and mistakes multiply. Your report is the patient's file for the machine.
Realistic scenario (Port Harcourt). A school brings the same lab computer back three months after a repair, claiming the earlier
fix failed. The shop checks its report: the part was replaced on a specific date, with a 90-day warranty that has expired, and the
customer signed for it. The conversation is calm and factual — because the paper exists.
Common misconception. "Reports are just paperwork; the repair is what matters." The repair earns today's money; the report
earns tomorrow's trust, resolves disputes, and supports warranty and audit.
Limitation. A report is only as good as its honesty and accuracy. A report full of vague or invented claims is worse than none —
it destroys credibility.
F.2 What a service report must contain
The idea. Every professional report has the same standard sections, in a logical order, so nothing important is ever forgotten.
The required sections.
1. Header / shop details. Business name, logo (if any), address, phone, and a unique report number (for example SRV-2026-
0042 ), the date, and the technician's name.
2. Customer and device details. Customer name, phone, and the device: make/model, and serial number or service tag
where available. (Serial numbers matter for warranty and for identifying the exact machine.)
3. Reported fault (the customer's words). What the customer said, in their own words, quoted where helpful.
4. Diagnosis / findings. What you observed; the tests performed and their results; and the confirmed root cause — the real
reason for the fault.
5. Action taken (repairs performed). Exactly what you did: parts replaced (with enough detail to identify them), software
installed or updated, cleaning, reseating, etc.
6. Parts used. An itemised list of parts, with a description (and part number where relevant) and the quantity.
7. Tests performed after repair / result. What you tested to confirm the fix, and the outcome: "Fault resolved; machine
boots, all devices working."
8. Costs (parts and labour). An itemised breakdown: parts cost, labour cost, diagnosis fee if any, and the total.
9. Recommendations. Preventive advice and next steps for the customer (for example, "use a surge protector", "back up
regularly", "consider an SSD upgrade").
10. Warranty terms. What is covered, for how long, and any conditions.
11. Sign-off. Technician signature and date, and customer signature and date.
Note: different shops combine some sections (for example, "parts used" inside "action taken"), and add a diagnosis fee line.
The content above is what matters; the exact layout can vary. Our CEA template presents them as separate, clearly
labelled sections so nothing is missed.
Analogy. The sections are like a story with a beginning, middle and end: the customer's complaint (beginning), the diagnosis
and repair (middle), and the result, cost and recommendation (end). Skip a part and the story is incomplete.
Common misconception. "If the repair was simple, I can write a short note." Even a simple repair (for example, reseating a RAM
stick) deserves the full record: reported fault, finding, action, test, cost, signature. The only thing that changes is how brief each
section is — not whether it exists.
Limitation. The report must be readable by the customer. A report stuffed with raw technical output without explanation fails
the customer, even if it is technically complete.
F.3 Recording the fault and the diagnosis
The idea. Keep the customer's reported fault and your observed findings separate but connected.
Why separate them.
The reported fault is what the customer experienced ("it restarts by itself").
The observed finding is what you actually measured (for example, "memory test failed with errors in slot 1").
Sometimes they match; sometimes the customer's symptom has a different cause than expected. Recording both shows
your professional reasoning.
Good practice.
Quote the customer's words: Customer reported: "It makes a rattling noise and then shuts down."
Record each test and result: Test: reseated RAM; ran Windows Memory Diagnostic — no errors. Test: checked fan — fan
bearing worn; CPU temperature rose to 92°C under load. Result: overheating caused automatic shutdown.
State the root cause plainly: Root cause: worn CPU fan causing overheating and thermal shutdown.
Analogy. It is like a detective's case file: the witness statement (reported fault) and the forensic evidence (observed findings)
are both recorded, and the conclusion (root cause) follows from the evidence.
Common misconception. "The customer's description is unimportant once I find the fault." Wrong — the description explains
why they came, and demonstrates you listened and understood.
Limitation. Customers can be inaccurate. Record their words faithfully, but always base the root cause on evidence, not on
their guess.
F.4 Documenting the action, tests and result
The idea. State exactly what you did, then prove it worked.
Action taken — be specific.
Instead of: "Fixed the problem." → Write: "Replaced faulty CPU cooling fan (original part: 92 mm, 4-pin). Applied new
thermal paste and reconnected the fan cable to the CPU_FAN header."
Include software actions: "Installed network, chipset, graphics and audio drivers. Ran Windows Update until no updates
remained. Activated Windows with the customer's licensed digital licence."
Parts used — be identifiable.
Description, quantity and (where relevant) part number or specification.
Example: "1 × 500 GB SATA SSD", "1 × 8 GB DDR4 2666 MHz RAM module", "1 × 500 W PSU".
Tests performed after repair / result — prove the fix.
Example: "Booted to Windows; ran CPU temperature test under load — stable at 55°C. Ran memory diagnostic — no
errors. Checked Device Manager — no warnings. Tested audio and network — working."
State the outcome plainly: "Fault resolved. Machine returned to full working condition; customer's data restored and
verified."
Why this matters. The "action + tests + result" trio is what turns your claim of a successful repair into evidence of a successful
repair.
Common misconception. "If it switches on, it is fixed." A machine can switch on and still have a hidden fault (for example, one
of two RAM sticks failing). The report must show the tests that confirm the whole system is healthy.
Limitation. Some faults cannot be reproduced immediately (intermittent faults). Be honest: record what you tested, and state
clearly if the fault was intermittent and could not be fully reproduced.
F.5 Writing clearly and honestly
The idea. The report must be understood by the customer and trusted by any reader — while remaining completely truthful.
Principles of clear writing.
1. Plain language. Write for the customer, not for other technicians. Explain any technical term you must use.
2. Be specific. "Replaced faulty RAM module in slot 2" beats "fixed memory".
3. Be complete. Include every test, part and step. No gaps.
4. Be accurate. Double-check serial numbers, part numbers, dates and costs. A single wrong figure undermines the whole
document.
5. Be professional. Correct grammar and spelling; no personal opinions, no complaints about the customer, no sarcasm.
6. Be honest. Never claim work you did not do or parts you did not use. If you made a mistake, record the facts and the
resolution.
The honesty rule. The report must describe what actually happened. Inflating labour hours, adding parts not fitted, or hiding
damage is fraud and destroys trust. A short honest report is worth more than a long false one.
Analogy. Writing a service report is like giving evidence in court: you state facts you can stand behind, clearly and without
exaggeration. If you are caught exaggerating once, everything you say afterwards is doubted.
Common misconception. "More technical language sounds more professional." The opposite: clear, honest language reads as
professional; dense jargon reads as evasive.
Limitation. You cannot write a perfect report if the work was rushed. A good report is the natural result of careful work —
another reason to do the repair properly.
F.6 Costs, warranty and sign-off
The idea. The report ends with the money, the promise and the signatures.
Costs. Itemise parts, labour and any diagnosis fee, then state the total. This must match the estimate the customer approved. If
the cost changed mid-repair, note that the customer approved the revised estimate.
Warranty terms. State plainly:
What is covered (for example, "the replacement PSU part only", or "parts and labour").
How long (for example, "90 days from the repair date").
Conditions (for example, "void if the case is opened by another person or if liquid damage is found").
Be careful not to promise a warranty you cannot honour. A warranty is a commitment.
Sign-off. The report is not finished until:
The technician signs and dates it.
The customer signs and dates it, confirming the work was done, the cost agreed and the device returned in the stated
condition.
Why the customer's signature matters. It is the moment the customer accepts the repair and the terms. It protects the shop
from later false claims and gives the customer a record of their consent.
Common misconception. "The customer's signature is a formality." It is a genuine agreement. Explain the report to the
customer before they sign — do not just push paper at them.
Limitation. A warranty cannot cover everything (for example, a new fault unrelated to the repair, or damage caused later by
liquid or drops). Say so honestly in the terms.
F.7 Keeping records and handling data
The idea. Once the report is signed, it must be kept safely and the customer's data protected.
Record keeping.
Store reports in a safe place — a folder, a filing cabinet, or a secure digital file with a backup.
Keep them for a sensible period (many shops keep records for at least a year, often longer for warranty).
Give the customer a copy (paper or a photo/PDF via WhatsApp) and keep one.
Data handling notes.
The report itself should not list private contents of the customer's files ("customer's bank statements found"). Record only
what is relevant to the repair.
When you back up a customer's data for a repair, delete your working copy once the job is safely returned — do not keep
customer data longer than necessary.
Never copy, share or gossip about a customer's data (Session 10 confidentiality).
Analogy. A service report is like a receipt and a medical note combined: you keep a copy for your records, you give the
customer one, and you do not write their private business all over it.
Common misconception. "Once the customer takes the machine, the record is no longer needed." The record is exactly what
you need if a problem returns. Keep it.
F.8 Summary of the session
A service report is the written story of one repair; it protects the customer, the technician and the shop.
Required sections include: header/report number, customer and device details, reported fault, diagnosis/findings,
action taken, parts used, tests/result, costs, recommendations, warranty, sign-off.
Keep the customer's words and your observed findings separate but connected.
Action + tests + result turn a claim into evidence.
Write clearly, specifically and honestly — no jargon, no vague phrases, no exaggeration.
Record costs, warranty and signatures, and store records safely while protecting customer data.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
What the instructor prepares:
A blank Service Report Template on the projector.
The worked scenario below, so the class can see a report built live, section by section.
G.1 The scenario
Customer: Mr. Okafor. Device: HP laptop, serial number ABC123 . Reported fault: "My computer is running very slowly
and sometimes freezes." Diagnosis: The hard drive was nearly full (95% used) and running slowly; the machine also had
only 4 GB of RAM, which is too little for the customer's workload. The old spinning hard drive was the main cause of
slowness. Repair: Replaced the HDD with a 500 GB SSD; upgraded RAM from 4 GB to 8 GB; reinstalled Windows (licensed)
and installed all drivers; ran Windows Update; restored the customer's data. Parts: 500 GB SSD — ₦25,000; 8 GB DDR4
RAM — ₦15,000. Labour: ₦10,000. Total: ₦50,000. Tests after repair: booted in under 20 seconds; memory diagnostic
clean; Device Manager clean; audio and Wi-Fi working; data restored and opened. Warranty: 90 days on parts; labour
covered for 30 days.
G.2 Building the report live (narrate each section)
Say: "Watch how every fact from the job goes into a section. Nothing is invented; nothing is left out."
1. Header: instructor writes the shop name, a report number SRV-2026-0042 , the date, and "Technician: [name]".
2. Customer and device: Mr. Okafor; phone; HP laptop; serial ABC123 .
3. Reported fault: "Customer reported: 'My computer is running very slowly and sometimes freezes.'" Explain why it is quoted.
4. Diagnosis/findings: write the tests and the root cause.
5. Action taken: write the replacement, RAM upgrade, Windows reinstall and driver work.
6. Parts used: the itemised table.
7. Tests/result: the verification list. Say: "This is where I prove the repair worked."
8. Costs: the breakdown summing to ₦50,000.
9. Recommendations: "Back up regularly; avoid filling the drive past about 80%; consider a surge protector."
10. Warranty: the terms.
11. Sign-off: technician line; explain that the customer signs here.
Ask the class after each section: "Could a stranger read this and understand exactly what happened? Could this report defend
the shop in a dispute?"
G.3 Deliberately show a vague phrase and improve it
Write: "Fixed the slow computer." Then rewrite it as the specific version above. Say: "One of these protects you; the other is a
blank cheque for an argument."
G.4 Show the weak sample and diagnose it
Display the weak sample report from the opening. Ask the class to find:
Missing sections (for example, no serial number, no tests, no warranty).
Vague language ("fixed the problem").
Any dishonesty or exaggeration.
Expected result: Learners spot that a report missing key sections cannot protect anyone.
Error to watch: Learners may want to write only the parts they remember. Insist on using the template so no section is
forgotten.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why do we record the customer's exact words?" → Clues and proof of listening.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. A customer, Mr. Nwosu, brings a desktop back to a Port Harcourt repair shop eight weeks after a repair. He claims the
shop "never actually replaced the power supply" and demands a refund, threatening to post about it online. The shop owner
opens the filing cabinet and finds the signed service report.
Relevant facts. The report shows:
A unique report number and date (eight weeks earlier).
The customer's reported fault in his own words.
The diagnosis and the test performed (known-good PSU test).
The action taken (500 W PSU replaced).
The part used, with description.
The tests performed after repair and the result.
The cost breakdown, signed by the customer.
A 90-day warranty on the part.
Decision point. How does the report resolve the dispute?
Possible approaches.
1. Argue from memory — "I remember we changed it."
2. Produce the signed report and let it speak.
3. Give the refund to avoid trouble.
Recommended solution. Approach 2.
The technician calmly shows Mr. Nwosu the report he signed, with the date, the part and the test results.
The shop offers to open the case and show him the new PSU, with its fitting date sticker, to confirm.
Because the repair was within the 90-day warranty, the shop agrees to re-check the PSU at no charge if there is a genuine
new fault — good service on top of good records.
Mr. Nwosu withdraws the claim.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 leaves the shop at the mercy of a customer's memory versus the technician's; unwinnable in a public argument.
Approach 2 is factual, calm and quick — and shows professionalism.
Approach 3 rewards a false claim and may encourage others. It also admits fault the shop did not commit.
Professional connection. The report is not just for the customer — it is the shop's memory and its defence. This is why
Session 11 exists, and why the Final Project requires a complete report. The documented, signed record is what separates a
professional shop from a roadside guess.
Transfer question for the class: "Which single section of the report do you think was most important in defending the shop —
and why?" (Strong answer: the customer's signature, because it proves agreement to the work and the cost; the test results are a
close second because they prove the fix.)

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table live. When a learner's report has a problem, point them to the row.
K.1 Vague descriptions
Symptoms: The report says "fixed the problem" or "did maintenance". Likely cause: Rushing, or not knowing what detail
matters. Safe corrective steps: Rewrite with the exact part, action and result. Ask: "Could a stranger repeat this from my
words?" Prevention: Use the template prompts for each section.
K.2 Missing sections
Symptoms: No serial number, no tests, no warranty, no signature. Likely cause: Skipping parts of the template. Safe corrective
steps: Compare against the full section list and fill every gap. Prevention: Always write from the template; never from memory.
K.3 Inaccurate numbers or serial numbers
Symptoms: Total does not add up; wrong model/serial; date errors. Likely cause: Careless recording. Safe corrective steps: Re-
add the figures; re-check the serial from the device; correct the report before signing. Prevention: Double-check all numbers
before the customer signs.
K.4 Technical jargon in the report
Symptoms: Customer cannot understand their own report. Likely cause: Writing for technicians, not customers. Safe
corrective steps: Translate terms; use plain language; keep technical detail brief and explained. Prevention: Write as if the
customer will read it aloud to a friend.
K.5 No customer signature
Symptoms: Report is unsigned; dispute later has no written agreement. Likely cause: Not asking the customer to sign. Safe
corrective steps: Explain the report to the customer at collection and ask them to sign; add the date. Prevention: Make sign-
off a required last step of every job.
K.6 Exaggeration or dishonesty
Symptoms: Labour hours or parts overstated; work claimed that was not done. Likely cause: Temptation to inflate a small job.
Safe corrective steps: Correct the report to the truth immediately; refund any overcharge; apologise. Prevention: Remember:
honesty protects your name and, in serious cases, keeps you out of legal trouble.
K.7 Customer data written into the report or carelessly handled
Symptoms: Private contents listed; backup copies kept too long. Likely cause: Not thinking about confidentiality. Safe
corrective steps: Remove private details; delete working copies of customer data once the job is returned. Prevention: Record
only repair-relevant facts; treat data as a sealed envelope.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Fix what you can confirm (missing section, wrong total). Do not guess at facts — if unsure of
a part number or test result, check the device or your notes before writing.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner uses the template and completes every section without prompting.
The learner quotes the customer's reported fault and separates it from the observed findings.
The learner writes specific action lines and records actual tests and results.
The learner's totals are accurate and the warranty is stated honestly.
The learner writes in plain language a customer could understand.
The learner includes both technician and customer sign-off and can explain why.
The learner treats customer data confidentially in the report.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Reports are just paperwork." → They protect the customer, the technician and the shop, and support warranty and
disputes.
"A short note is enough for a simple job." → Even a simple repair gets the full record; only the length of each section
changes.
"The customer won't read it." → Even if they do not, the report is your protection and your record.
"I'll write it later from memory." → Write it while the details are fresh; memory fades and errors creep in.
"My signature is enough." → The customer must sign too; it proves agreement.
"Big technical words look professional." → Plain, honest language is what reads as professional.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Section-matching drill: give a list of facts and a list of sections; ask the learner to match each fact to its section.
2. Vague-to-specific drill: hand out ten vague sentences ("fixed it", "cleaned it") and ask for specific rewrites.
3. Fill-the-template drill: provide a partially completed report with gaps; ask the learner to finish it.
4. Honesty drill: give a scenario with a tempting exaggeration; ask the learner to write the honest version and explain why.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed report with prompts in each section; allow the learner to write on paper;
check section by section.
Level 2 (standard): complete the guided exercise and assignment as written, including the reusable template.
Level 3 (challenge): write reports for two different repairs (one hardware, one software/OS), and add a short customer-
facing summary paragraph at the top of the report that a non-technical customer can read in 30 seconds.
