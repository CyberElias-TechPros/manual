# Disassembly and Reassembly

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module: Components and Safety

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes (1 hour 45 minutes)

**Difficulty:** Beginner, but hands-on and delicate; needs close supervision

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–2: component names and functions; ESD, electrical safety, tools. Connection to previous session The learner can name the parts and work safely. This session applies both skills by opening a real desktop, removing components in the correct order, and rebuilding it so it works. Connection to the course Disassembly and reassembly are the physical foundation of all repair. Session 7 (component replacement) and the Session 12 capstone both require exactly these skills. This session also completes the Week 1 mini-project, "My First Teardown."

**Preparation before class:** At least two practice desktop computers (ideally non-working or expendable units) openable by simple screws; tool kits; anti-static wrist straps per learner; anti-static mats; screw trays; a smartphone/camera for photographing connections; printed Disassembly and Reassembly checklists; tape and a marker for labelling cables; a working reference machine to test against. Safety note for this session: This is the first session where learners physically open a computer. Every rule from Session 2 applies and is enforced. The machine must be unplugged from the wall and discharged (power button pressed) before the case is opened. No mains-powered work. Never open the PSU. Never force a component. All work stops immediately if a learner is unsure. Learners work in pairs or small groups with an instructor circulating. Laptops are not dismantled in this session; desktop practice only. If a practice machine is a working one, test power-on only briefly and with the instructor present.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Prepare a machine safely — unplug, discharge, set up the anti-static mat, wear the strap, and photograph the internals
before starting.
2. Safely disconnect all external cables from the back of a desktop computer.
3. Remove the side panel without damaging clips or threads.
4. Disconnect internal cables — SATA data and power, front-panel, and power connectors — safely and without straining
them.
5. Remove components in the correct order (expansion card, RAM, storage drives, then cooler) and set them aside safely.
6. Reinstall components in the correct order, applying the correct technique (for example, RAM clips snapping into place).
7. Reconnect all cables correctly, using photographs and labels to guide them.
8. Verify the machine powers on after reassembly, and know what to do if it does not.
9. Record screw locations and cable positions so reassembly is accurate and repeatable.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware (provided by CEA):

None required. (Optional: a system-info tool is not needed for this session.)

Anti-static wrist straps and mats.
Safety glasses if compressed air is used (optional this session).
A first-aid kit in the room.
Sample / fault components:
A spare dead hard drive and a spare RAM stick for practice handling, in case a group finishes early.
A machine deliberately set up with a "loose RAM" fault for the troubleshooting discussion (optional, instructor-prepared).

Worksheet 3.1 — "Disassembly Checklist" (one per learner).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"The Teardown Challenge"
Instructor setup: Place a fully assembled desktop computer on the front bench, connected to a monitor. Power it on and let
the class see it working. Then power it off properly and unplug it. Keep the tool kit nearby.
What the instructor says:
"Look at this computer. It is working. It has taken engineers years to design and factories hours to build. Now watch this."
Unplug the machine and hold up a single screwdriver.
"With one small screwdriver and the right knowledge, I am going to take this whole thing apart — and then put it back
together so that it still works. Anybody can break a computer by opening it. It takes a technician to open it and give it back
working.
Here is your challenge for today: by the end of this session, you will do exactly what I am about to do. You will open a real
desktop, remove its parts, put them back, and press the power button — and it must come on. This is the skill that
separates a technician from somebody who just 'carries' computers to the repair shop.
But before we touch anything, I want you to remember Session 2. What is the first thing we do before opening any case?"
Pause for answers.
"Correct — we unplug it. Then we press the power button to discharge. Then we strap up. Safety first, always. Today,
safety and skill walk together."
Then create the question: Point to the inside (once the panel is off) and say:
"Tell me — if you had to remove all these parts, which would you take out first? The RAM? The big card? The hard drive?
Why does the order even matter? By the end of today, you will know the correct order and the reason for it."
Expected learner responses: Learners may guess "biggest first" or "whatever is easiest." Accept the answers. Some will
remember the safety steps from Session 2 (unplug, discharge, strap) and should be praised. Note their guesses — you will
correct the reasoning in the lecture.
Why this opener works: It frames a mechanical task as a challenge with a clear, observable win (the machine works at the end),
connects to the previous session's safety rules, and creates a genuine question (what order, and why) that the lecture answers.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a quick oral review; ask different learners.
Question 1: "What are the first two things you must do before opening a computer case?"
Expected answer: Unplug the power cable from the wall; press the power button to discharge residual electricity.
Common gap: learners say "switch it off." Correct: unplug from the wall, then discharge. Off is not enough.
Bridge: "Today, that is step one of everything."
Question 2: "What is ESD, and name two ways you prevent it."
Expected answer: Electrostatic discharge; prevent with an anti-static wrist strap clipped to bare metal, an anti-static mat,
touching bare metal, holding by edges, antistatic bags.
Common gap: learners remember the strap but forget to touch bare metal before handling. Emphasise.
Bridge: "You will handle real boards today, so this matters every minute."
Question 3: "Name three essential tools for opening a desktop."
Expected answer: Phillips screwdriver (correct size), tweezers, torch (and anti-static wrist strap, pliers, screw tray).
Common gap: not knowing the screwdriver size matters. Remind them: wrong size strips screws.
Bridge: "Choosing the correct screwdriver is the first skill of disassembly."
Question 4: "Where is the power supply, and what is the golden rule about it?"
Expected answer: The metal box that supplies electricity to all parts; never open it — replace it if faulty.
Common gap: thinking the PSU can be repaired. Correct firmly.
Bridge: "You will meet the PSU today but you will never open it."
Question 5: "Why do we keep screws organised and take photos?"
Expected answer: So we know which screw goes where and how the cables were connected, making reassembly correct.
Common gap: learners think it wastes time. Explain it saves time and prevents damage.
Bridge: "Today, taking photos and logging screws is not optional — it is how professionals work."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Think of disassembling a computer like dismantling a rented room you must hand back exactly as you found it. Every
item has a place; every wire has a route. If you throw everything in a pile and forget where the wires went, you will
struggle to rebuild it. So the professional's secret is not strength or speed — it is recording and order. Today you learn the
order, and the discipline of recording."
If more than a third cannot answer Question 1, spend an extra three minutes re-teaching the unplug-and-discharge routine,
because it is safety-critical.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why order matters — the idea of reversibility
The idea. Disassembly is the process of taking a computer apart; reassembly is putting it back together. The single most
important principle is this: reassembly is the reverse of disassembly. If you removed A, then B, then C, you reinstall C, then B,
then A.
Why it exists. Components are often stacked or overlapping. A long graphics card may sit over the RAM clips; a cooler may sit
over the CPU; a drive cage may block a cable. If you try to remove things in the wrong order, you will find you cannot — or you
will force something and break it. The order is not arbitrary; it follows the physical layout.
Real-life comparison. Think of dressing a child for school. You put on the vest first, then the shirt, then the jumper. To undress,
you reverse it: jumper, shirt, vest. If you tried to remove the vest first, you would fail. A computer is no different.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A new technician at a Port Harcourt shop removes the CPU cooler first, before the RAM. He
knocks the cooler into a RAM stick and bends a pin in a slot. Now, instead of a simple cleaning job, the board is damaged. Order
is not a formality; it prevents damage.
Common misconceptions.
"Order does not matter; I will just take out whatever I can reach." It matters — the layout dictates the order.
"Faster is better." In repair, correct is faster, because mistakes cost far more time than care.
Limitations and safety. Different machines have different layouts. The order below is the standard, but always look first, take
photos, and adapt to your specific machine. If you are unsure, stop and ask the instructor.
F.2 Safety preparation before any disassembly
The non-negotiable pre-flight checklist (do this every time):
1. Get permission. Only open machines you are authorised to repair (Session 2, F.7).
2. Record the machine. Note the model, the condition, and any existing damage. Take a photo of the outside.
3. Switch off the operating system properly (do not just yank the power while Windows is running, unless it is already dead).
4. Unplug the power cable from the wall.
5. Press the power button once to discharge residual electricity.
6. Set up the bench: anti-static mat, good light, screw tray, tools within reach, no drinks.
7. Wear the anti-static wrist strap and clip it to bare metal of the case.
8. Touch bare metal before handling any internal part.
9. Photograph the inside before disconnecting anything. Take several photos from different angles, especially of the cable
connections.
10. Label cables with tape and a marker if the machine is unfamiliar or complex.
Why each step matters:
Permission protects the owner, the warranty and you.
Recording protects you if something is already damaged.
Proper shutdown protects the data and the operating system.
Unplugging and discharging protect you from shock and the parts from surges.
The mat, strap and bare-metal touch protect the parts from ESD.
Photos and labels protect you from forgetting where things go.
Real-life comparison. This is like the pre-flight checklist a pilot runs before take-off, no matter how many times they have
flown. The checklist is not a sign of inexperience; it is what professionals rely on so that nothing is forgotten.
Common misconceptions.
"I have done this before; I do not need the checklist." Experience is exactly why professionals use checklists — to stay
consistent when they are confident and could become careless.
"I can skip photos for a simple job." Even simple jobs go wrong; photos cost nothing and save everything.
Safety implications. Never proceed without steps 4, 5, 7 and 8. These are the life-and-machine rules. If you are interrupted, re-
touch bare metal before resuming, and re-check the plug is out.
F.3 Disassembly — the correct order, step by step
Below is the standard desktop disassembly order. Each step lists what to do and why.
Step 1 — External cables. Disconnect everything from the back: monitor cable, keyboard, mouse, USB devices, network
(Ethernet) cable, speakers, and the power cable (already unplugged). Label any that are unclear. Why: You must free the
machine so you can move it to the bench safely. Cable labels help you reconnect exactly.
Step 2 — Move to the bench and open the panel. Place the machine on its side on the anti-static mat (or stood up, per its
design). Remove the screws holding the side panel (usually two at the back). Some cases use thumb screws (turn by hand) or
slide-and-lift latches. Why: The side panel gives access. Keep the panel screws in the screw tray — do not put them on the
floor.
Step 3 — Photograph and note the layout. Before touching cables, photograph the whole inside, then close-ups of the
connections: the 24-pin power connector, the CPU power connector, the SATA data cables, the SATA power cables, and the
front-panel wiring. Why: This is your map for reassembly. The front-panel wires (power switch, reset, front USB/audio) are the
most easily forgotten.
Step 4 — Disconnect internal cables. Work carefully and grip the connectors, not the wires (pulling wires can break them):
Unplug the SATA data cables from the drives (press the small latch if present).
Unplug the SATA power connectors from the drives.
Unplug the power connectors from the motherboard only if you will remove the board — otherwise leave them.
If removing the board, note the front-panel connectors carefully. Why: Cables must be out of the way before components
can be removed. Grip the connector to avoid damaging the wire and the socket.
Step 5 — Remove expansion cards (the graphics card first). If a graphics card is fitted, it is usually the first component to
remove because it often lies over other parts. Remove the screw holding its bracket to the case, release the clip at the back of
the PCIe slot, and pull the card straight up by its edges, supporting its weight. Why: The card overlaps and blocks other parts;
removing it early frees access. Supporting it prevents bending it or cracking the slot.
Step 6 — Remove RAM. Push down the retaining clips at both ends of each RAM slot; the stick will lift up. Pull the stick
straight out by its edges and place it in an anti-static bag. Why: RAM is easy to remove and blocks nothing else, so it comes
after the card and before the drives. Holding by the edges protects the contacts.
Step 7 — Remove storage drives. Remove the screws holding each drive (or the drive cage screws), disconnect any
remaining cables, and slide the drive out. Handle HDDs gently. Why: Drives sit in bays that may need the cables cleared first.
Gentle handling protects the fragile HDD.
Step 8 — Remove the CPU cooler (only if the task requires). Unplug the fan's power connector from the board. Then
unscrew or release the cooler's clips. Gently twist the cooler slightly to break the seal of old thermal paste, then lift it off. Place it
aside, fan-side up. Why: The cooler sits on top of the CPU; it comes off late because it is the most delicate and requires handling
paste. Do this only when needed (for example cleaning or CPU work). Warning: With the cooler off, never power on the
machine — the CPU can overheat in seconds.
Step 9 — Remove the motherboard (only if required). Remove all screws holding the board to the case (note their
positions), disconnect any remaining cables, and lift the board out carefully, holding by its edges. Why: This is the last step
because the board is the foundation to which everything else attaches. Beginners usually leave the board in place unless the task
demands otherwise.
A note on laptops. Laptops follow the same principle (reverse order) but the parts are far smaller and more delicate, and many
are held by hidden clips and tiny screws. Do not dismantle laptops in this session. Only attempt laptops under supervision and
after you are confident on desktops.
Real-life comparison. Disassembly is like unpacking a well-packed suitcase: you take out the top layers first, and you keep
note of how things were folded. Reassembly is packing it the same way so it closes properly.
Common misconceptions.
"The CPU cooler is always the first thing to remove." No — it is near the last, because it sits on top and handling is delicate.
"I can pull cables by the wire." No — always grip the connector.
"If it does not move, pull harder." Never. Check for a screw, clip or cable.
Limitations and safety. Some machines use proprietary screws and clips (common in branded desktops). If you meet resistance
you cannot explain, stop and ask. Never force.
F.4 Reassembly — the reverse order, with extra care
Reassembly is the reverse of disassembly, but with two extra emphases: care with paste and verification before closing.
Step 1 — Motherboard (if removed) first. Place the board back, align it with the case's standoffs, and fit the I/O shield
correctly at the back. Replace all screws evenly, without over-tightening.
Step 2 — CPU cooler. If the cooler was removed, the old thermal paste must be cleaned off both the CPU and the cooler with
isopropyl alcohol, and a small pea-sized amount of fresh paste applied to the centre of the CPU. Refit the cooler, tighten or clip
it evenly (tighten screws in a cross pattern, a little at a time), and reconnect the fan cable. Why: Old, dried paste does not
transfer heat; too much paste is also bad; even pressure seats the cooler properly.
Step 3 — Storage drives. Slide each drive into its bay and secure the screws. Reconnect SATA data and SATA power cables,
checking your photos.
Step 4 — RAM. Open the clips, align the notch on the stick with the key in the slot (they only fit one way), and press down
firmly and evenly at both ends until the clips snap into place. Listen and feel for the click. Why: A RAM stick not fully seated is
one of the most common causes of a computer that powers on but shows nothing. The snap tells you it is seated.
Step 5 — Expansion card (graphics card). Remove the case's expansion slot cover if needed, align the card with the PCIe slot,
press down firmly until the slot clip engages, and replace the bracket screw. Reconnect any power cable the card needs.
Step 6 — Internal cables. Reconnect the SATA data and power cables, the motherboard power connectors if removed, and
— most carefully — the front-panel cables (power switch, reset, USB, audio), using your photos and labels. The power switch
wire is the one that lets the power button work; if it is misconnected, the machine will not start from the button.
Step 7 — Check before you close. Before replacing the panel, do a careful visual check: Are all cables firmly seated? Is the
RAM clipped? Are there any loose screws inside? Is the CPU cooler connected? Are the drive cables correct? A screw left loose
inside can short-circuit the board. Why: This silent check catches most reassembly errors before power-on.
Step 8 — Close and reconnect. Replace the side panel and its screws. Reconnect the external cables (monitor, keyboard,
mouse, power). Plug in the power cable.
Step 9 — Power-on verification. Press the power button. Check: Do the fans spin? Does a light show? Does the monitor
display? Does it reach the operating system (or the BIOS/POST screen)? Why: This is the moment of truth. If it does not start,
you diagnose (Section K) — you do not panic.
Real-life comparison. Reassembly is like reassembling a bicycle after fixing a puncture. You fit the inner tube, the tyre, the
wheel, then check the brakes before you ride. You always check before you trust it — you do not just close the case and hope.
Common misconceptions.
"Any amount of thermal paste is fine." No — a pea-sized amount, not a thick layer.
"The RAM will seat with a light push." Not always — it needs a firm, even push until it clicks. But never force it in the wrong
orientation.
"I can close the case and test later." Test as soon as possible, before fully working the machine, so faults are easier to trace.
Limitations and safety. Do not over-tighten screws (they strip and can crack the board). Do not power on without the CPU
cooler. If the machine does not start, unplug again before re-opening.
F.5 Recording screws, cables and the mirror-image rule
Recording screws. Different screws have different lengths and threads. A long screw forced into a short hole can damage the
board. Use a screw tray with compartments labelled by location (case, motherboard, drive, bracket) and place each screw where
it belongs as you remove it. Keep a written Screw and Cable Log (Worksheet 3.3).
Recording cables. Photograph before disconnecting, label awkward cables, and note the front-panel layout. The front-panel
connectors are the most common source of "the power button does nothing" after reassembly.
The mirror-image rule. Disassembly order and reassembly order are exact mirrors. Write them down as numbered lists so you
can simply read one backwards to do the other.
Real-life comparison. Recording is like a tailor taking measurements before cutting cloth. The few minutes spent measuring
save the whole garment. In repair, a few photos and a screw log save the whole repair.
Common misconceptions.
"I will remember where everything goes." You will not — especially on an unfamiliar machine, and especially after a break.
"Photos are for beginners." Professionals photograph and label constantly, especially on complex jobs.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
One practice desktop on the bench with the anti-static mat, wrist strap, tool kit and screw tray.
A phone/camera to demonstrate photographing.
Tape and marker to demonstrate labelling.
The projector showing the machine (or a second camera) so all learners can see.
The Disassembly and Reassembly checklists on the wall or projected.
G.2 Demonstration — full teardown and rebuild, with narration (15 minutes)
Say: "Watch every step, and listen to the reason behind it. I will disassemble, then reassemble, and the machine will work at the
end."
Do and say (slowly, narrating reasons):
1. "I confirm this machine is mine to work on (permission). I note its condition."
2. "I shut it down properly, unplug it from the wall, and press the power button to discharge."
3. "I set up the mat, put on my strap and clip it to bare metal. I touch bare metal again."
4. "I remove the side panel screws and place them in the tray. Compare — the correct driver seats perfectly."
5. "I photograph the inside and the connections. This is my map."
6. "I disconnect the SATA data and power cables, gripping the connectors, not the wires."
7. "I remove the graphics card first — screw, slot clip, straight up by the edges, supporting it."
8. "I remove the RAM — clips down, straight out by the edges, into an anti-static bag."

"I remove the graphics card first because it lies over everything else."

Instructor answer key (do not show learners):
Disassembly procedure (model answer):
1. Confirm permission; record the machine's model and condition; shut it down properly.
2. Unplug from the wall; press the power button to discharge; wear the wrist strap and touch bare metal.
3. Set up the anti-static mat, tools, and a labelled screw tray.
4. Photograph the inside and each cable connection; label awkward cables.
5. Remove the side panel screws (into the tray) and remove the panel.

9. Remove the storage drive (screws, slide out gently).
10. (If required) remove the CPU cooler — unplug the fan, release the clips, twist gently, lift off.
11. (If required) remove the motherboard — all screws noted, cables disconnected, lift by the edges.
12. Place all components safely on the mat/antistatic bags.

5. Refit the graphics card into the PCIe slot until the clip engages; replace the bracket screw.
6. Reconnect all internal cables, especially the front-panel wires, following your photos.

8. Replace the side panel and screws; reconnect external cables and the power cable.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze is a careful learner who opens her home desktop to clean out the thick dust of Port Harcourt's dry season.
She unplugs the machine, discharges it, straps up, and takes photos — good habits. She removes the side panel, the graphics
card, the RAM and the drive, and cleans everything thoroughly with compressed air. Happy with her work, she rebuilds the
machine: drive, RAM, graphics card, cables, panel. She plugs in and presses the power button. The fans spin and the lights come
on, but the screen stays black. Nothing on the monitor. Her heart sinks — did she break it?
Relevant facts.
The machine powers on (fans and lights) but there is no display.
Adaeze removed and refitted the RAM and the graphics card.
She used compressed air and cleaned thoroughly.
The monitor cable was connected to the graphics card's port when she started.
Decision points.
1. Is the machine "dead," or is this a common, fixable reassembly issue?
2. What is the most likely cause given she removed and refitted RAM and the card?
3. What should she check, and in what order?
4. What could have damaged the machine — if anything?
Possible approaches.
1. Assume the worst and rush it to a shop for an expensive repair.
2. Panic and start removing everything again randomly.
3. Stay calm, unplug, and check the most likely, simplest causes first: RAM seating, card seating, display cable and port.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. "Powers on but no display" after a teardown is very often a RAM stick not fully seated
or the graphics card not fully in its slot, or the display cable in the wrong port or loose. The correct steps: unplug and
discharge, re-open the case, press the RAM sticks down until the clips snap, press the graphics card firmly into its slot until the
clip engages, check the bracket screw, and confirm the monitor cable is firmly in the graphics card's port (not the
motherboard's). Close, reconnect, and test. In Adaeze's case, one RAM stick had not clicked home. With an even push, it
snapped in, and the picture returned.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 wastes money and time on a fault she could fix in two minutes.
Approach 2 risks causing real damage by random handling.
Approach 3 is systematic, free, and teaches the exact lesson the course is built on: check the simple, likely physical causes
first.
Professional connection. "Powers on but no display" is one of the most common situations in any real repair shop. The
technician who methodically reseats RAM, checks the card, and checks the cable solves it quickly and cheaply; the one who
guesses replaces parts the customer did not need. This case is a direct bridge to Session 4 (Diagnostic Workflow) — the same
logic, made formal.
Transfer exercise. "List, in order, the first three things a technician should check when a freshly reassembled desktop powers
on but shows no display." (RAM fully seated; graphics card fully seated; display cable connected to the correct port and monitor
powered on.)
Safety reminder. All of this checking is done with the machine unplugged and discharged, on the anti-static mat, with the
strap worn. Never re-open or poke inside a machine that is still plugged in.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use as the live guide. Ask learners to find the relevant row.
K.1 Component will not come out
Symptoms: A part feels stuck; pushing harder seems to work but you hesitate. Likely cause: A screw, clip, latch or cable you
have not released. Cause is confirmed once you find and release it. How to investigate: Stop. Look all around the part (front,
back, under) with a torch. Check for hidden screws and slot clips. Safe corrective steps: Release the missed fastener, then lift
the part straight out. Never use force. How to confirm resolved: The part lifts out smoothly with no resistance. How to
prevent recurrence: Study the machine's layout and take photos before removing; work slowly.
K.2 Powers on but no display after reassembly
Symptoms: Fans spin, lights on, but the monitor shows nothing. Likely cause: RAM not fully seated, graphics card not fully
seated, display cable loose or in the wrong port, or monitor off. How to investigate: Unplug and discharge; re-open; check
RAM clips, the card's seating and clip, and the display cable/port. Safe corrective steps: Reseat RAM until it clicks; reseat the
card; move the display cable to the graphics card's port; confirm the monitor is powered. How to confirm resolved: The
display appears. How to prevent recurrence: Always check seating and cables before closing (Step 7 in reassembly).
K.3 Power button does nothing after reassembly
Symptoms: Machine completely silent; no fans, no lights. Likely cause: The front-panel power switch connector is loose or
misconnected, or the main power connectors are not seated, or the wall plug/socket is off. How to investigate: Check the wall
socket and plug; check the 24-pin and CPU power connectors; check the front-panel power wire against your photo. Safe
corrective steps: Reseat the front-panel power connector and the power connectors (machine unplugged). How to confirm
resolved: The power button starts the machine. How to prevent recurrence: Photograph front-panel wiring before
disconnecting; check it in the pre-close step.
K.4 Loose screw left inside the case
Symptoms: A rattle when moving the machine; or a sudden short/failure on power-on. Likely cause: A screw fell inside during
work. Confirmed by finding it. How to investigate: With the machine unplugged and discharged, tip and inspect carefully; use
a torch. Safe corrective steps: Retrieve it with tweezers or by gently tilting; never shake a machine containing drives violently.
How to confirm resolved: No rattle; the screw is found and put in its place. How to prevent recurrence: Always count screws;
keep them in the tray; do the pre-close check.
K.5 Stripped or cross-threaded screws
Symptoms: The screw turns but does not tighten, or bites the wrong way and jams. Likely cause: Wrong driver size, or forcing a
screw into the wrong hole/angle. How to investigate: Try the correct size; inspect the head and the hole. Safe corrective
steps: Turn the screw slowly backwards first to find the thread, then tighten gently. If it is badly stripped, get instructor help (a
screw extractor may be needed). How to confirm resolved: The screw tightens cleanly without force. How to prevent
recurrence: Match driver to screw; never over-tighten; start screws by hand.
K.6 Forgetting to reconnect a cable
Symptoms: A specific function fails (e.g., no power from the button, no front USB, a drive not detected), while the rest works.
Likely cause: A cable left disconnected or mis-plugged. Confirmed by comparing with your photo. How to investigate:
Compare the current connections with your pre-disassembly photos. Safe corrective steps: Unplug and discharge, re-open,
reconnect the correct cable. How to confirm resolved: The function works again. How to prevent recurrence: Use photos
and labels; do the pre-close check.
Distinguishing likely vs confirmed: In every case, the "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only when you actually check it (find
the missed screw, see the RAM clip snap, trace the cable against the photo). Teach learners to check before "fixing."

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version. Answers are in the Instructor Key below.)
Q1. Short answer. What is the correct order for removing the main components during disassembly, and why?
Q2. Multiple choice. Why do we take photographs before disconnecting internal cables? a) To show the customer b) To know
where to reconnect them during reassembly c) To make the job look professional d) It is not necessary
Q3. Explain in your own words. Why is reassembly described as "the reverse of disassembly"?
Q4. Practical identification. How do you know a RAM stick is correctly installed?
Q5. Error detection. A learner says: "If a component will not come out, I should pull harder." Find the mistake and correct it.
Q6. Scenario. After reassembling a desktop, the fans spin and the lights come on, but the monitor shows nothing. List, in order,
the first three things you would check.
Q7. Short answer. Why should you never power on a computer with the CPU cooler removed?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. Remove the graphics card first, then the RAM, then the storage drives, and only later (if required) the CPU cooler and
motherboard. The card often lies over other parts, so it must go first to free access; the RAM and drives come next; the cooler
and board are last because they are the foundation and the most delicate.
A2. (b) To know where to reconnect them during reassembly. (Photos are your map, especially for the front-panel wires.)
A3. Because components are stacked/overlapping: whatever was removed last is the foundation and must be placed first, and
whatever was removed first (the top layer) is placed last. Reversing the order guarantees nothing is trapped or blocked, and that
everything fits as before.
A4. The retaining clips at both ends snap into place, and the stick sits level and firm in the slot. (You hear/feel the click.)
A5. The mistake is using force. The correction: stop and check for a screw, clip, latch or cable you have missed; a part that will
not move easily is being held by something. Never force it — forcing causes damage.
A6. (1) Confirm the machine is unplugged/discharged, then check the RAM is fully seated (clips snapped); (2) check the
graphics card is fully seated and its clip engaged; (3) check the display cable is firmly connected to the correct port (the
graphics card, not the motherboard) and the monitor is powered on.
A7. Because the CPU produces heat very quickly and relies on the cooler (heatsink/fan + thermal paste) to remove it. With the
cooler off, the CPU can overheat within seconds and shut down or be permanently damaged.
Marking: up to 2 marks each (Q1, Q6 up to 3). Total ≈ 16; scale proportionally. Full credit requires correct order, correct
component names, and correct reasoning.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you opened a real computer and put it back together. You learned the safety preparation
(permission, recording, unplugging, discharging, strap, bare-metal touch), the correct disassembly order (external cables →

1. Safety first, every time: unplug, discharge, strap, bare metal.
2. Disassembly order matters, and reassembly is its mirror image.

4. Never force a component; check for the missed screw, clip or cable.
