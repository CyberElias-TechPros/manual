# The Diagnostic Workflow

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module: Diagnostics

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes (1 hour 45 minutes)

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with formal fault-finding)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3: component names and functions; ESD and electrical safety; tools; disassembly and reassembly. Connection to previous session In Session 3 the learner met the classic "powers on but no display" problem after reassembly. This session turns that informal troubleshooting into a structured, repeatable method. Connection to the course This workflow is the backbone of the whole course. Sessions 5 (hardware diagnostics), 6 (software diagnostics), 7 (replacement), and the Session 12 capstone all run through this method. The capstone is assessed partly on whether the learner follows it.

**Preparation before class:** Scenario cards (at least six faults); at least one practice computer with a simulated fault (for example RAM not fully seated) that can power on but show no display; a working reference machine; printed Symptom-to-Cause Reference and Diagnostic Questions Checklist; whiteboard for drawing the workflow; optional: a machine that beeps on power-on (to demonstrate POST beeps) or a diagram of BIOS beep codes. Safety note for this session: All physical diagnosis is done on unplugged and discharged machines, with the anti-static strap worn and boards handled by the edges. Never reseat or swap a component while the machine is powered. When testing power on, the instructor supervises and only brief tests are made. Never open the PSU. Because this session involves deliberate faults, students must be told clearly that these are practice machines and that the same actions must never be attempted on a customer's machine without permission.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. State and follow the five-step diagnostic workflow — gather symptoms, confirm the obvious, form a hypothesis, test
one thing at a time, and verify and document.
2. Gather symptoms systematically by asking effective diagnostic questions and observing look/listen/smell/feel.
3. Confirm the obvious first — power, cables and switches — before assuming a deep fault.
4. Explain POST (power-on self-test) and what it means when a machine fails it.
5. Interpret basic BIOS beep codes and no-display symptoms as diagnostic clues (recognising that codes vary by maker and
must not be invented).
6. Use swap-testing to isolate a faulty component safely.
7. Apply a safe boot troubleshooting order for common "no display" and "no power" situations.
8. Document each step of a diagnosis clearly, including what was tested, the result, and the conclusion.
9. Explain why a structured method beats guessing, using the two ideas of "likely cause" and "confirmed cause."

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware (provided by CEA):
At least one practice computer with a simulated fault that powers on but shows no display (for example, RAM deliberately
left unseated, or the display cable in the wrong port).
A working reference machine to compare against (very useful for swap-testing).
One or two spare known-good components for swap-testing, e.g., a tested RAM stick, a tested display cable.
A desktop for the instructor demonstration.
Anti-static wrist straps and mats; tool kit; screw tray; torch.
Software:
None essential. Optional: a free system-information viewer (Windows Task Manager → Performance) to show live activity,
but the session can run entirely without software. Note: no statistical or hardware-specific claims should be made.
Safety gear:
Anti-static wrist straps and mats.
First-aid kit available.
Sample / fault components:
Known-good RAM stick for swap-testing.
A known-good display cable and/or a second monitor if available.
Clearly-labelled "FAULT — DEMO" parts where relevant.
Worksheets (printed):
Worksheet 4.1 — "Symptom-to-Cause Reference" (one per learner): a table of symptoms and likely causes.
Worksheet 4.2 — "Diagnostic Questions Checklist" (one per learner): the seven questions.
Worksheet 4.3 — "Diagnostic Worksheet / Service Report Draft" (one per learner): a structured page with fields for
symptoms, observations, questions, hypothesis, tests and results, resolution, and verification.
Scenario cards (at least six) for the guided and independent exercises.
Alternatives for a learner or academy with limited equipment:
The workflow can be taught almost entirely through paper scenarios if no faulty machine is available. Use the scenario

If only one faulty machine exists, run it as a whole-class demonstration and let learners fill in the worksheet together, then
give each learner different scenario cards to work through independently.
If a learner is uncomfortable with hands-on fault work, let them be the "diagnostic recorder" and the "customer", which still
practises the workflow and the questions.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"The Detective Analogy"
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a simple picture or drawing of a detective with a magnifying glass and a notebook,
beside a computer with a question mark over it. Keep the faulty practice machine hidden or off to the side.
What the instructor says:
"Imagine a detective arrives at a crime scene. Does the detective walk in and immediately say, 'It was the gardener'? No.
That is a guess. What does a good detective do? They look at the scene. They ask witnesses questions. They form a theory.
They test the theory — fingerprints, alibi, evidence. And only when the evidence is solid do they say who did it.
A computer technician is a detective. When a customer brings a faulty computer, the customer has given you a clue — a
symptom — like 'it will not come on' or 'the screen is black.' Your job is to find the cause. And here is the trap: the worst
technicians are the ones who guess. They open the case, see a dirty fan, and say, 'Ah, it is the fan!' Maybe. Or maybe the
real problem is somewhere else entirely.
Today you learn the detective's method. You will learn a fixed set of steps — the same steps every single time — that
stops you from guessing and leads you to the true cause.
Let me ask you: which is better, a technician who guesses fast, or a technician who is right?"
Pause for answers.
"Correct. Right beats fast, every time. A wrong guess costs the customer money and costs you your reputation. A method
makes you reliable."
Then create the hook: Reveal the faulty practice machine. Say:
"This computer is the crime scene. It powers on — you can see the fans spin — but there is no picture. Somewhere in here
is the cause. By the end of today, using the method, you will find it — without guessing, and without replacing a single
part."
Expected learner responses: Learners often suggest "the screen is broken" or "we need to check the graphics card." Accept all
answers but point out that these are hypotheses, not yet confirmed. Some learners may guess correctly — praise them but
insist on the method: "You may be right, but how will you prove it? That is the skill."
Why this opener works: It uses a familiar role (detective) to make an abstract method concrete, draws a sharp contrast between
guessing and method, and gives the class a real, solvable problem to look forward to.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run as a quick oral review; ask different learners.
Question 1: "Name the major components and one thing each does."
Expected answer: Motherboard (connects everything), CPU (does the work), RAM (temporary memory), storage
(permanent), PSU (power), GPU (images), fans (cooling).
Common gap: learners forget the PSU in the list, yet it is central to "no power" diagnosis. Remind them.
Bridge: "All of these are suspects for today's detective."
Question 2: "What is ESD, and why does it matter when we swap parts?"
Expected answer: Electrostatic discharge; invisible damage; prevented by strap, mat, bare-metal touch, edge handling.
Common gap: forgetting to re-touch bare metal between parts. Emphasise when swapping.
Bridge: "When we swap components to test, we handle many parts — so the ESD habit matters even more."
Question 3: "What are the first two things to check before opening a case?"
Expected answer: Unplug from the wall; press the power button to discharge.
Common gap: confusion between the power button and the wall plug. Clarify order.
Bridge: "And note — the very first diagnostic clue is often outside the case entirely."
Question 4: "From Session 3: if a freshly reassembled computer powers on but shows no display, what were the first things to
check?"
Expected answer: RAM seated (clips snapped); graphics card seated; display cable in the correct port; monitor powered.
Common gap: jumping to "the GPU is broken."
Bridge: "Today we turn that instinct into a proper order of operations."
Question 5: "What is the difference between a 'likely' cause and a 'confirmed' cause?"
Expected answer: A likely cause is a guess or probability; a confirmed cause is proven by a test or observation.
Common gap: learners treat their first idea as confirmed. Clarify the difference — it is the heart of today.
Bridge: "This distinction is the whole point of the method: confirm before you fix."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Think of diagnosis like tracing a fault in the NEPA/PHED line after an outage. Would you climb the pole and start
replacing cables immediately? No — you would first check whether the whole area is out, then whether your neighbour
has light, then whether it is only your compound, then your meter, then your wiring. You follow an order from the biggest,
simplest possibility to the smallest. Today's method is exactly that discipline, applied to a computer."
If more than a third cannot answer Question 5, spend an extra three minutes on likely-vs-confirmed, as it underpins the whole
session.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why a method beats guessing
The idea. Diagnosis is the process of finding the cause of a fault. A workflow is a fixed sequence of steps you follow every time,
so that you never skip something important and never jump to a conclusion.
Plain-English definition. The diagnostic workflow is a step-by-step method: gather the symptoms, confirm the obvious, form a
hypothesis, test one thing at a time, then fix and verify. It replaces "I think" with "I have proven."
Why it exists. Computers have many parts that can cause similar symptoms. "No display," for example, can come from RAM,
the graphics card, the cable, the monitor, the motherboard, or even the power supply. Guessing wastes time and money and
often replaces healthy parts. A method narrows the possibilities in an efficient, safe order.
What it does. It makes your work:
Repeatable — the same fault is diagnosed the same way every time, by anyone.
Reliable — you confirm the cause instead of assuming it.
Safe — you test simple, safe things before complex or risky ones.
Accountable — because you document each step, you can explain your reasoning to a customer or a colleague.
Real-life comparison. The workflow is like a doctor's consultation. The doctor asks about symptoms, checks the basics
(temperature, blood pressure), forms a suspicion, orders a test, confirms the diagnosis, treats it, and then checks you again. A
doctor who prescribed medicine without checking would be dangerous. A technician who replaces parts without checking is
the same.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. Two technicians receive the same "no display" computer. The first guesses "graphics card" and
fits a new one — the fault remains, and the customer is charged for nothing. The second follows the method: checks the cable
and port, reseats the RAM (which was loose), and the display returns in two minutes for free. Same fault, opposite results — the
difference is the method.
Common misconceptions.
"Experienced technicians do not need a method; they just know." Experienced technicians have internalised the method;
they still follow it. When a fault is unusual, even they return to the steps.
"The most obvious cause is always the true one." Sometimes the obvious cause is a red herring. You only know by testing.
Limitations and safety. The workflow guides you, but some faults (intermittent, or requiring special tools) need more time or
specialist help. Recognising limits is part of the method — Step 5 explicitly includes referring onward when needed.
F.2 The five-step diagnostic workflow
Step 1 — Gather the symptoms (observe and ask). Collect every clue before you touch anything.
Observe (your own senses):
Look: Is there visible damage? Swelling (swollen capacitors or batteries)? Burn marks? Dust? A loose cable? A fan not
spinning? Any light at all?
Listen: Do you hear beeps (POST codes)? Clicking or grinding (often a failing HDD)? Rattling (a loose fan or screw)? Silence
(no power)?
Smell: Is there a burning or "hot" smell (often an overheated part or a failing PSU)? If you smell burning, stop and unplug
safely — do not continue.
Feel (carefully, only after unplugging): Is a part unusually hot? Is a connector loose?
Ask the customer (the seven questions are detailed in F.4): What happened, when, what were they doing, what changed, does it
happen always or sometimes, have they tried anything, is there any unusual sound/smell/damage.
Step 2 — Confirm the obvious (the simple things first). Before assuming a deep fault, rule out the simple, common causes:
Is the machine actually plugged in and is the wall socket working (test with a known-good device, or a lamp)?
Is the power switch (on the PSU, on a laptop, on a power strip) in the correct position?
Is the monitor switched on and set to the correct input (HDMI/VGA/DisplayPort)?
Are the external cables connected (display, keyboard, power)?
Is there power in the building (a blackout affects everything)? Many "faults" are none of these being right. Always check
them first.
Step 3 — Form a hypothesis (an educated guess). Based on your observations and the customer's answers, write down the
two or three most likely causes, in order of probability and ease of testing. A hypothesis is not a conclusion; it is a smart
guess to be tested. Example: "Given the machine powers on but shows no display, the most likely causes are (1) RAM not seated,
(2) display cable/port, (3) graphics card not seated."
Step 4 — Test one thing at a time (and change one thing at a time). Test your hypotheses, starting with the simplest and
most likely. Crucially, change only one thing per test, then observe the result. If you change three things at once, you will not
know which one fixed (or caused) the problem. Common safe tests:
Reseat the suspected component (RAM, card, cable).
Swap-test: replace a suspected component with a known-good one (for example, a tested RAM stick). See F.6.
Compare with a working machine. After each test, note the result. If the fault is gone, you have likely found the cause;
confirm it (Step 5).
Step 5 — Resolve, verify and document. Fix the confirmed cause (or, at this stage, identify and record it). Verify the fix: does
the machine now work fully? Test more than just the original symptom — for example, restart it, and check it behaves normally
under use. Then document everything: the symptoms, the questions, your hypothesis, the tests and results, the cause, and the
fix. Documentation protects the customer and you, and it is the basis of the professional service report (Sessions 11 and 12).
The golden rule of testing: One change at a time. This single rule transforms confusing diagnosis into clear, logical progress.
Real-life comparison. The five steps are like finding which bulb has blown in a house. You notice the lights are off (symptom).
You check whether the whole street is off and whether the switch is on (confirm the obvious). You suspect a fuse or a bulb
(hypothesis). You try one bulb at a time and one fuse at a time (test one thing). You confirm the light comes on (verify) and note
what you changed (document). You would never replace every bulb in the house at once.
Common misconceptions.
"Test many things at once to save time." This does the opposite — it creates confusion. One change at a time.
"Fix it and move on." Always verify and document; a fix that is not verified may not be a fix at all.
"Hypothesis means I already know." No — a hypothesis is only a guess until a test confirms it.
Limitations and safety. Never test by working on a powered machine. All component reseating and swapping happens with the
machine unplugged and discharged, wearing the strap. If a test requires mains-level work (for example, testing a PSU's output),
that is a supervised, later-session activity and is not attempted here on live circuits.
F.3 POST — the computer's start-up self-check
Plain-English definition. POST (Power-On Self-Test) is a basic self-check the computer runs automatically the moment it is
switched on, before it tries to load the operating system. It is built into the motherboard's firmware (called the BIOS or, on
newer machines, the UEFI). Firmware is small built-in software that runs before Windows does.
Why it exists. Before handing control to the operating system, the computer checks that its essential parts are present and
working: it detects the CPU, the RAM, the graphics, and the storage, and confirms basic settings. If something essential is
missing or faulty, the POST fails, and the machine signals the problem instead of pretending everything is fine.
What the POST does.
It checks that basic components are detected and respond.
If the check passes, the machine usually continues to the BIOS/UEFI screen briefly and then boots the operating system (or
shows a "no bootable device" message if there is no bootable system, which is a different issue from a dead machine).
If the check fails, the machine can signal with a beep code (a pattern of beeps) if a small internal speaker is fitted, and/or a
display message, and/or codes on the motherboard. Many modern machines have no internal speaker, so they cannot beep
— instead they may show LED indicators or simply show no display.
How POST helps diagnosis. When a computer powers on but does not reach its operating system, you can often tell where it
stopped:
No display, no beep, fans spinning: often a failure very early — RAM, graphics, or the POST itself.
Beep pattern: a code that (on machines that do beep) points to a particular subsystem.
Reaches the BIOS screen but cannot find the OS: the hardware is basically fine, but the boot drive or the operating
system has a problem (a software or storage issue, not a dead board).
A crucial honesty note — beep codes vary. BIOS beep codes are not universal. Different makers (AMI, Award, Phoenix, and
different manufacturers) use different beep patterns, and modern machines often have no speaker at all. Therefore, do not
memorise invented "universal" beep codes. The professional approach is:
1. Note the make and model of the machine or motherboard.
2. Look up that maker's documented beep codes (from the official manual or the manufacturer's website).
3. Interpret the beep against that specific documentation. Teach learners this discipline: the code is only meaningful with the
maker's own chart. Do not invent or quote specific patterns as if they were universal.
Real-life comparison. POST is like the security check at the airport gate. Before anyone boards, staff verify the plane's
essential systems and passengers. If a check fails, the flight does not proceed until it is fixed. The beep code is like the specific
alarm message: "gate 3 sensor fault" — but you must read that airport's manual to know what gate 3 means, because every
airport numbers things differently.
Common misconceptions.
"Beeps mean the same thing on every computer." No — they differ by maker. Always consult the maker's chart.
"No beep means it is definitely broken." Not always — many machines have no speaker, so silence is normal on POST
failure.
"If it beeps, the POST passed." Not necessarily — some beeps indicate a fault.
Limitations and safety. POST only checks the basics, not everything; a machine can pass POST and still have a failing drive or
software fault. And you must never rely on invented codes. If unsure, use the method: confirm simple things, then test
components one at a time.
F.4 The seven diagnostic questions
The idea. What the customer tells you is often the single biggest clue. Asking the right questions saves hours.
The seven questions:
1. "When did the problem start?" — Ties the fault to a time or event (a power cut, a move, a new update).
2. "What were you doing when it happened?" — Context reveals the trigger (gaming → overheating or GPU; a certain
program → software or driver).
3. "Has anything changed recently?" — New hardware, a new program, an update, a new cable, a different room.
4. "Does it happen all the time or only sometimes?" — Intermittent faults are harder; consistent faults are usually easier to
reproduce and test.
5. "Have you tried anything to fix it?" — You need to know what was already done or changed, so you do not undo it or
chase your tail.
6. "Is there any unusual sound, smell, or visible damage?" — Physical clues (burning, clicking, swelling, a spill) are often
decisive.
7. "Does it happen with specific programs, or all the time?" — Helps separate a software problem from a hardware one.
How to ask well. Ask in plain, respectful language a customer will understand. Do not make the customer feel stupid. Repeat
back what they say to confirm. Write down the answers — memory is unreliable, and notes become your report.
Real-life comparison. These questions are like a doctor asking about your symptoms and history. The more accurate the
information, the faster and more accurate the diagnosis.
Common misconceptions.
"I will just open it and see." You will miss vital clues. Ask first.
"The customer does not know anything." The customer may not know the cause, but they always know what changed and
when — that is gold.
F.5 Safe boot troubleshooting order for "no display" and "no power"
When a machine shows no picture (or no power at all), follow a fixed, safe order from the outside inward and from simplest to
most complex. This is an application of the workflow.
A. No display, but the machine powers on (fans spin, lights on). Work in this order:
1. Monitor and cable first: Is the monitor powered on? Is the cable firmly connected at both ends? Is the monitor set to the
correct input? (These are free and easy.)
2. Cable port: If a dedicated graphics card is fitted, is the display cable in the graphics card's port, not the motherboard's? (A
very common mistake.)
3. Signal check: Does the monitor say "no signal" or is it completely dark? "No signal" points to a source/cable issue;
complete darkness may point to the monitor itself.
4. RAM reseating: Unplug and discharge; reseat the RAM until the clips snap. (One of the most common no-display causes.)
5. Graphics card reseating: Reseat the card firmly into its slot until it clicks; check the bracket screw.
6. Swap-test the cable and monitor with known-good ones if available.
7. Only then consider deeper causes (motherboard, RAM fault, etc.), ideally with supervision.
B. No power at all (completely dead: no fans, no lights). Work in this order:
1. Wall power: Is the socket working? Test with a known-good lamp or device. Is there power in the building?
2. Power cable: Is it firmly connected at both ends? Try a known-good cable.
3. Power switch: Is the PSU switch (if present) on? Is a power strip switched on?
4. Front-panel power button: Is the front-panel power connector seated on the board? (A common result of reassembly.)
5. PSU test: With supervision, and using a safe method, check whether the PSU is delivering power. Never open the PSU. At
this stage, swap-testing with a known-good PSU is done only under supervision.
6. Motherboard/board-level: If a known-good PSU still gives nothing, suspect the board — but confirm carefully, do not
guess.
The principle behind the order. Always start with the cheapest, simplest, safest, most likely checks and move toward the
expensive, complex, riskier ones. This saves money, avoids damage, and usually finds the fault sooner.
Real-life comparison. This order is like checking your phone will not charge: first you check the socket (is there power?), then
the cable (is it damaged?), then the charger, and only last the phone's charging port. You would not open the phone first.
Common misconceptions.
"No display means the GPU is dead." The cable, port or RAM is more often the cause. Check those first.
"No power means the PSU is dead." It may be the socket, the cable, the switch, or the front-panel button. Confirm first.
Limitations and safety. Never test power-related faults while working inside a live machine. PSU testing is a supervised activity
and the PSU is never opened. If you smell burning, stop and unplug safely.
F.6 Swap-testing — isolating the faulty component
Plain-English definition. Swap-testing (also called substitution testing) means replacing a suspected component with a
known-good one to see whether the fault moves or disappears. If the fault disappears after the swap, the suspected part was
faulty. If the fault remains, the problem is elsewhere.
Why it works. It turns a doubt into a fact. Instead of guessing whether the RAM is faulty, you prove it by fitting a stick you know
works.
How to swap-test safely.
1. Identify the component you suspect, based on the workflow.
2. Power off, unplug and discharge the machine.
3. Wear the strap and touch bare metal. Handle parts by the edges.
4. Remove the suspected part and fit a known-good part (from a working machine or a tested spare).
5. Power on and observe. One component per test.
6. If the fault is gone, you have strong evidence the original part was faulty. If it remains, put the original part back and move
to the next hypothesis.
Critical cautions.
Only swap-test components you are authorised to handle, and only on practice machines in this course.
Never swap a part from a customer's working machine into another machine without permission — you could introduce a
fault or lose data.
When swapping storage drives, be extremely careful: a drive may contain important data. Do not swap drives around
casually.
After swap-testing, restore the machine to its correct configuration — do not leave a "spare" part installed and forgotten.
Real-life comparison. Swap-testing is like trying a different phone charger to see if your phone charges. If the new charger
works, your old charger was faulty. If it still does not charge, the problem is the phone, not the charger.
Common misconceptions.
"If the new part fixes it, the old part is definitely dead." Usually true, but confirm — sometimes the act of reseating (not the
part itself) fixes a poor connection.
"Swap many parts at once to be quick." Never — you will not know which change did what. One at a time.
Limitations and safety. Swap-testing needs known-good spares. Without them, rely on the other steps. Never swap a PSU or
open it without supervision. Keep track of which part went where.
F.7 Documenting the diagnosis (the beginning of the service report)
Why document. A diagnosis you cannot explain is of little professional value. Documentation:
Records what you did, so you or a colleague can pick up the job later.
Protects you if the customer claims you did something you did not.
Becomes the service report the customer receives (Sessions 11–12).
Helps you learn — patterns you record today become experience tomorrow.
What to record (a simple structure):
Customer and machine details: name, contact, model, any visible condition.
Reported symptoms: in the customer's words.
Your observations: look/listen/smell/feel.
Questions and answers: the seven questions.
Hypotheses: the likely causes you listed.
Tests and results: what you tested, one at a time, and what happened.
Confirmed cause: the root cause once proven.
Fix/plan: what was done or recommended.
Verification: how you confirmed the machine is now working.
Real-life comparison. Documentation is like a doctor's case notes. If you see a different doctor next time, the notes tell them
your whole history. Without notes, every visit starts from zero.
Common misconceptions.
"Documentation is paperwork that wastes time." It saves time, prevents repeated mistakes, and is what separates a
professional from a tinkerer.
"I will remember." You will not — especially after several jobs in a day.

G.2 Demonstration — diagnosing the simulated "no display" fault (15 minutes)
Say: "Watch me follow the five steps exactly. Notice that I do not guess, and I test one thing at a time."
Do and say, step by step:
Step 1 — Gather symptoms.
"I power on the machine. The fans spin; a light comes on; the monitor says 'no signal.'"
"I listen — no beeps. I look — no burn marks, no loose drive. I smell — nothing burning."
"I ask the 'customer' (I will play both roles): 'When did it start? What were you doing? Has anything changed?' The customer
says: 'It worked yesterday; today I opened it to clean dust and now there is no picture.'"
Step 2 — Confirm the obvious.
"Is the monitor on? Yes. Is the cable connected? I check both ends. Is it on the correct input? Yes. Is the cable in the right
port? I check — the machine has a graphics card, and I see the cable is in the graphics card port, which is correct."
"So the simple things are fine. Now I move inward."
Step 3 — Form a hypothesis.
"Given it worked until the case was opened for cleaning, and now there is no display, my likely causes are: (1) RAM not fully
seated, (2) graphics card disturbed, (3) a cable knocked loose. I write these down in order."
Step 4 — Test one thing at a time.
"I power off, unplug and discharge. I wear the strap and touch bare metal."
"Test 1: I reseat the graphics card first? No — I start with the most likely and simplest: the RAM. I press each stick until the
clips snap." (If the simulated fault is loose RAM, one stick was not clipped.)
"I check the graphics card is seated — it is fine."
"I make sure I changed only the RAM seating. Nothing else."
Step 5 — Resolve, verify, document.
"I close, reconnect, and power on. The display appears. The fault is gone."
"I verify beyond the symptom: I restart it; the display returns and the machine behaves normally."
"I document: symptoms, the customer's answer, my hypothesis, the test (reseat RAM), the result (display returned), the
confirmed cause (RAM not fully seated), and the verification."
Expected result: The machine shows a display; the class sees the method find the fault without any part being replaced.
Reasoning to state aloud at key points:
"I start with the cable and monitor because they are outside the machine, free, and easy."
"I reseat RAM before touching the graphics card because in a case that was just cleaned, RAM is very commonly nudged
loose, and reseating it is simple and safe."
"I change one thing at a time so I know exactly what fixed it."
"I verify by restarting, not just by seeing one picture."
Errors to watch and mention:
Do not test by opening the machine while it is plugged in — narrate the unplug/discharge clearly.
Do not change two things at once — demonstrate the discipline.
Do not declare success after one picture — verify.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why did I check the monitor and cable first?" (Simplest, outside the machine, free.)
"After reseating the RAM, how do I know the fix is real?" (The display returns and it restarts normally — verified.)
"What would I do if reseating the RAM had not worked?" (Put it back, move to the next hypothesis — reseat/test the card,
then swap-test.)

5. Test plan: Describe, one test at a time, how you would confirm or rule out your top hypothesis. Include one swap-test.

7. Documentation: Complete a one-page diagnostic note (you may use Worksheet 4.3).

Never open a live machine or the PSU.
Use the workflow steps as headings in your answer.

All five workflow steps are present and correctly applied.
Symptoms are distinguished from confirmed observations.

Hint 3: A safe, quick test for overheating is to observe whether it fails sooner with the case side off in a cool spot with a fan

1. Symptoms: Freezes and restarts by itself; worse in the hot afternoon and under heavy use; fine in the morning and light use.

2. Obvious checks: Confirm the power supply from the wall is steady (a cyber café may have many machines and a weak

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. A customer brings a desktop to the shop with a complaint that sounds familiar: "It works fine for a few minutes, then
the picture freezes or the machine restarts." A young technician at the shop tests the RAM and finds nothing wrong. He tests the
hard drive and finds nothing wrong. He tests the power supply and finds nothing wrong. Frustrated, he tells the customer: "Your
machine is fine — I cannot find any problem." The customer leaves annoyed, and the fault continues. A day later, the same
customer returns, even more upset.
Relevant facts.
The fault is intermittent — it does not appear during casual testing.
The machine works for a while, then fails.
Standard component tests (RAM, drive, PSU) show "nothing wrong."
The young technician tested for the fault without understanding when and why it happens.
Decision points.
1. Why did "testing everything" fail to find the fault?
2. What did the technician fail to do in Step 1 (gather symptoms)?
3. What question should he ask that would change everything?
4. How should he confirm the cause once he has a hypothesis?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep testing components randomly, replacing parts until something works.
2. Give up and tell the customer "it is fine."
3. Return to Step 1: gather better symptoms, especially what task triggers the fault, then form a targeted hypothesis.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Intermittent faults are diagnosed by reproducing them and by asking sharper questions
— not by testing parts at random. The technician should ask: "Does it happen when you are doing a particular thing, like
watching videos or playing games, or when the room is hot?" Suppose the customer says: "Yes — it happens when I watch
videos for a while, especially in the afternoon." That points strongly to overheating under load. The technician then monitors
the machine's behaviour under load in a controlled way and finds the CPU temperature climbing and the fan
struggling/blocked. Confirmed cause: overheating from a dust-clogged fan and dried thermal paste. Fix: clean the cooling
system and replace the thermal paste (Session 8 skill), then verify by running the machine under load for a longer period and
confirming the fault no longer occurs.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (random replacement) wastes the customer's money and may introduce new faults. It is guesswork disguised as
testing.
Approach 2 (giving up) destroys trust; the fault is real, even if it is intermittent.
Approach 3 (better symptoms → targeted hypothesis → reproduce → confirm) is slower at the start but finds the true
cause and builds a lasting reputation.
Professional connection. Intermittent faults are common and are where a real technician earns respect. The skill is patience and
structure: ask when and under what conditions, reproduce the fault, test one variable at a time, and confirm before charging
the customer for anything. This is also where the "likely vs confirmed" distinction matters most — you must reproduce the
fault and see it disappear after the fix.
Transfer exercise. "Why are intermittent problems harder to diagnose than problems that happen every time, and what two
techniques help?" (They do not appear during casual testing; techniques: ask sharper questions to find the trigger and
reproduce the fault deliberately; and test one variable at a time, often over a longer period or under load.)
Safety reminder. Any handling of the machine during this diagnosis is done unplugged and discharged, with the strap worn,
and without opening the PSU. If testing under load, it is supervised, and the machine is never left unattended while running a
stress test on a practice unit.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use as the live guide. Ask learners to find the relevant row.
K.1 Jumping to conclusions (guessing)
Symptoms: Replacing a part that was probably fine; the fault returns; the customer is charged for the wrong work. Likely cause:
Skipping Steps 1–3 and going straight to a favourite guess. Confirmed when the fault persists after the "fix." How to
investigate: Ask: what evidence did I actually have? Did I confirm the simple things first? Safe corrective steps: Return to Step
1. Gather symptoms, confirm the obvious, list hypotheses, and test one at a time. How to confirm resolved: The fault
disappears and you can explain the evidence. How to prevent recurrence: Always write the hypothesis list before touching a
part.
K.2 Not asking enough questions
Symptoms: A diagnosis that fits the symptom but misses the context (like "only when hot" or "only when busy"). Likely cause:
Rushing to open the machine. Confirmed when the customer reveals a key detail you never asked about. How to investigate:
Use the seven questions; write the answers. Safe corrective steps: Re-interview the customer and revise the hypothesis. How
to confirm resolved: The new information explains the pattern. How to prevent recurrence: Make the seven questions a fixed
script.
K.3 Testing the most complex cause first
Symptoms: Wasted time and money on deep checks while a simple cause is ignored. Likely cause: Excitement, or a bias toward
a favourite part. Confirmed when a simple check (like a loose cable) fixes it. How to investigate: Always start from the outside
in, simplest first. Safe corrective steps: Follow the safe boot order (F.5). How to confirm resolved: The simple cause is found
and fixed. How to prevent recurrence: Memorise the "cheapest, simplest, safest, most likely first" rule.
K.4 Changing more than one thing at a time
Symptoms: The fault disappears but you do not know why, or it returns unpredictably. Likely cause: Multiple changes in one
test. Confirmed by the confusion when it fails again. How to investigate: Recall exactly what you changed; re-test one variable
at a time. Safe corrective steps: Redo the tests, one change each time. How to confirm resolved: You can point to the single
change that fixed the fault. How to prevent recurrence: Adopt the golden rule: one change per test.
K.5 Not verifying the fix
Symptoms: The machine seems fine, then the fault returns; the customer loses trust. Likely cause: Declaring success after one
observation or a single picture. Confirmed by the return of the fault. How to investigate: Test the machine properly — restart
it, use it, run it under the conditions that caused the fault. Safe corrective steps: Verify under real conditions before returning
the machine. How to confirm resolved: The fault does not return after proper verification. How to prevent recurrence: Make
verification the final, mandatory step.
K.6 Not documenting
Symptoms: You cannot explain your work later; a colleague must start from scratch; the customer disputes what was done.
Likely cause: Believing documentation is a waste of time. Confirmed when memory fails. How to investigate: N/A — it is a
habit. Safe corrective steps: Fill in the Diagnostic Worksheet during the job, not after. How to confirm resolved: Your notes
completely describe the job. How to prevent recurrence: Make documentation part of the workflow, not an extra.
K.7 Misreading beep codes / inventing universal codes
Symptoms: A wrong diagnosis based on a beep pattern from the wrong maker. Likely cause: Assuming all BIOS beeps are
universal. Confirmed when the maker's actual chart contradicts the assumption. How to investigate: Identify the make/model
and consult that maker's documented beep codes. Safe corrective steps: Interpret the beep using the maker's own chart; if
unavailable, fall back to the workflow. How to confirm resolved: The interpretation matches the maker's documentation and
the tested fault. How to prevent recurrence: Never memorise invented "universal" beep codes.
Distinguishing likely vs confirmed: Every "likely cause" above becomes "confirmed" only when checked and verified. In
diagnosis, an unverified conclusion is just another hypothesis.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version. Answers are in the Instructor Key below.)

Q2. Multiple choice. Why should you confirm the obvious things (power, cables, switches) first? a) Because they are the most

Q3. Explain in your own words. What is POST, and what does it mean when a machine "fails POST"?
Q4. Practical identification. A computer powers on (fans spin, lights on) but shows no display. Give the first three things you

Q5. Error detection. A learner says: "I will replace the RAM, the graphics card and the cable all at once to save time." Find the
mistake and correct it.
Q6. Scenario. A customer says their computer restarts by itself, only when the room is hot. Which part of the diagnostic
workflow did most technicians forget, and what would you do next?
Q7. Short answer. Why must you not treat BIOS beep codes as universal?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. (1) Gather the symptoms (observe and ask); (2) confirm the obvious (power, cables, switches); (3) form a hypothesis; (4) test
one thing at a time; (5) resolve, verify and document.
A2. (b) Because they are the simplest, cheapest, safest and often the actual cause — and checking them first prevents wasting
time and money on deeper faults.
A3. POST (Power-On Self-Test) is the basic self-check the computer runs when switched on, before loading the operating
system, to confirm its essential parts are present and working. If a machine "fails POST," it means this early check did not pass
— often shown by a beep code (on machines with a speaker), LED indicators, or simply no display. It tells you the problem is
very early, before the system loads.
A4. (1) Monitor and cable (is the monitor on; is the cable connected at both ends and in the correct port — graphics card vs
motherboard; is the input correct); (2) reseat the RAM until the clips snap; (3) reseat the graphics card. (Order of the first two
may vary; the point is simplest/outside-first.)
A5. The mistake is changing more than one thing at a time. The correction: change one thing per test, observe the result, then
move on. Otherwise, if the fault changes, you will not know which change caused it. The golden rule is one change at a time.
A6. The technician forgot Step 1 — gather symptoms properly (especially the context: "only when hot"). Next: ask more
targeted questions and reproduce the fault under the trigger conditions, form the hypothesis of overheating, and confirm it by
checking the cooling system and observing whether the fix removes the fault. (This is a context-rich, intermittent fault.)
A7. Because BIOS/UEFI beep codes differ between makers (AMI, Award, Phoenix, and manufacturers), and many modern

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned to diagnose faults like a professional — not by guessing, but by a method. The
five-step workflow is: gather the symptoms (observe and ask), confirm the obvious (power, cables, switches), form a
hypothesis, test one thing at a time, and resolve, verify and document. You learned about POST (Power-On Self-Test) —
the computer's automatic start-up self-check — and how to interpret basic clues (remembering that beep codes differ by
maker and must not be treated as universal). You learned a safe boot order for "no display" and "no power," the technique of
swap-testing to isolate a faulty component, and the discipline of documentation. Above all, you learned the difference
between a likely cause and a confirmed cause.
Five important takeaways:
1. Follow the five-step workflow every time — method beats guessing.
2. Confirm the obvious first — power, cables and switches are often the cause.
3. Test one thing at a time (and swap-test with known-good parts).
4. Beep codes are not universal — always check the maker's own chart.
5. Verify and document — an unverified fix is not a fix, and undocumented work is not professional.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I list and explain the five steps of the workflow?
Can I gather symptoms using the seven questions?
Can I give the safe order of checks for "no display" and "no power"?
Can I explain the difference between a likely and a confirmed cause?
Connection to the next session: You now have the method. Next session you put it to work with hardware diagnostics —
using tools and software to test RAM (for example MemTest86), check a hard drive's health (for example CrystalDiskInfo),
monitor temperatures (for example HWMonitor), and check a power supply. The workflow you learned today decides when and
why each of those tools is used.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner states and follows the five steps without prompting.
The learner gathers symptoms before opening anything, and asks good questions.
The learner confirms the obvious (power/cables/switches) first.
The learner tests one thing at a time and uses swap-testing correctly.
The learner distinguishes "likely" from "confirmed" causes.
The learner documents each step clearly.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"The obvious cause is always right / guessing is fine." → Form a hypothesis and test it; the obvious cause can be a red
herring.
"Experienced technicians skip the method." → They follow it faster; it is what makes them reliable.
"Test many things at once." → One change at a time.
"Once it works, stop." → Always verify; and always document.
"Beep codes are universal." → They are maker-specific; consult the maker's chart.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Step-by-step prompting: give a single scenario and walk the learner through the five steps aloud, one at a time, until the
sequence is automatic.
2. Question drill: role-play the customer while the learner practices the seven questions and takes notes.
3. Likely vs confirmed sorting: give ten statements and have the learner mark each as "likely" or "confirmed" and justify it.
4. Safe-order drill: shuffle the checks for "no display" and "no power" and have the learner re-sequence them.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed Diagnostic Worksheet with the symptoms pre-filled and the steps
labelled, and work through one scenario together before letting the learner try independently.
Level 2 (standard): complete the guided and independent exercises as written.
Level 3 (challenge): diagnose a real intermittent fault on a practice machine (for example a partially blocked fan), timing
how long it takes to reproduce the fault, and produce a full written report; or design a new scenario card and model answer
for the class.

exercise (Section I). If the room is slow, compress the lecture sections on swap-testing (F.6) and documentation (F.7) to
summaries, but do not shorten the five steps (F.2), safe boot order (F.5), or the POST honesty note (F.3). Keep the case study as a
short story if time is tight.
