# Computer Components — What Is Inside the Box

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module: Components and Safety

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes (1 hour 45 minutes)

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with computer hardware)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Basic computer skills: typing, saving files, opening programs. No hardware experience needed. Connection to previous session This is the first session of the Computer Repairs course. It assumes only the Typing & Computer Basics course. Connection to the course This session gives every learner the vocabulary of the trade — the names and jobs of every part inside a computer. Every later session (safety, disassembly, diagnostics, repair, the capstone) depends on knowing these parts by name and function.

**Preparation before class:** Physical samples of motherboard, CPU (+ cooler), RAM, HDD, SSD, PSU, GPU, SATA cable, power cable; at least one powered-off desktop computer; projector or large screen; printed Component Identification Chart (one per learner); anti-static wrist straps; component name cards for a labelling race. Safety note for this session: Learners will handle real components. Even though this is an "identification" session, every learner must wear an anti-static wrist strap or regularly touch the bare metal of the case before touching any board. The machine used for demonstration must be unplugged from the wall and the power button pressed once to discharge residual electricity. No component is opened, and no mains-powered work is done. All handling is done by the edges only. Where the academy has only one working demonstration machine, learners should be grouped and supervised.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Identify and name all major internal components of a desktop computer — motherboard, CPU and its heatsink/fan, RAM,
HDD, SSD, PSU, GPU, expansion cards, cooling fans, and the main ports and connectors.
2. Explain the function of each major component in their own words, using a familiar comparison.
3. Describe how data flows through a computer from a user's input to the screen, naming the components involved at each
step.
4. Distinguish clearly between RAM and storage, and between HDD and SSD, including speed, durability and cost
differences.
5. Explain the relationship between components — how they connect to the motherboard and why the system needs all of
them to work.
6. Compare a desktop and a laptop, stating at least three differences in how components are arranged and serviced.
7. Handle components safely — holding boards by their edges, never touching gold contacts, and using anti-static
protection.
8. Identify components physically on a real open machine or on labelled photographs and diagrams.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware (provided by CEA):
Physical samples of each component: motherboard, CPU with heatsink/fan, RAM stick(s), one HDD, one SSD (SATA type if
available), one M.2/NVMe stick if available, PSU, a dedicated GPU card, a spare expansion card (sound/network card), a case
cooling fan, SATA data cable, SATA power connector, 24-pin ATX power connector, and a 8-pin CPU power connector.
At least one desktop computer, powered off and unplugged, with its side panel removed or removable, for live
identification.
A laptop (any working or non-working unit) for the desktop-vs-laptop comparison.
Projector or large screen connected to the instructor's computer for showing photographs and diagrams.
Software:
A simple slideshow or photo gallery of computer internals (instructor's own photos or freely available images). No special
software required.
Optional: a free system-information viewer already built into Windows (Task Manager → Performance tab) to show live
CPU, RAM and disk activity. This is only a demonstration aid and is not essential.
Safety gear:
Anti-static wrist straps (one per learner, or one per pair).
Anti-static mat for the demonstration table (one is enough).
Clean, dry, well-lit table surface. No food or drink on the table.
Sample / fault components:
A dead/faulty RAM stick and a dead hard drive (clearly labelled "FAULT — DO NOT FIT") to show what failed parts look like.
These are for looking only.
A burnt-looking PSU (clearly labelled) to introduce the idea that components can fail. Do not open it.
Worksheets (printed):
Worksheet 1.1 — "Component Identification Chart" (one per learner): a table with columns for component name, what it
does, one key fact, and one common problem.
Worksheet 1.2 — "Data Flow Diagram" (one per learner): a blank flow diagram the learner fills in during the guided exercise.
Component name cards (small cards with one component name each) for the opening labelling race.
Tools:
A Phillips PH2 screwdriver (only for the instructor to remove a panel; learners do not unscrew anything this session).
A small torch/flashlight to point out parts inside the case.
Alternatives for a learner or academy with limited equipment:
If no real components are available, use printed or projected high-quality labelled photographs and diagrams. The
identification exercise can be done entirely from labelled photos.
If there is only one desktop machine, rotate small groups of three to four learners around it while the others work with
photo sheets and the handling samples.
If a learner has low vision or shaky hands, pair them with a careful partner and let them identify and describe while the
partner holds the part.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
"What Is Inside the Box?"
Instructor setup: Bring a sealed cardboard box to the front. On a side table, hidden under a cloth, place the loose components
(motherboard, RAM, PSU, etc.). Do not reveal them yet. On the projector, keep a blank screen.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning, everyone. Welcome to the Computer Repairs course. Before we open a single computer, I want to play a
game. I have a box here. Inside this box is a mystery item. I want you to guess what it is. I will give you one clue: it is
something you all use, some of you every single day, and most of you have never seen the inside of it."
Take three or four guesses from around the room.
"You are all close. The answer is: it is a computer — or at least, all the parts of one. Now here is the shocking part. Look at
Chinedu's phone, look at the cyber café machines down the road, look at the school computer lab — inside, they are all
made of the same handful of parts, just arranged differently.
Here is my question for you: if you took a computer apart, could you name every single thing inside it? Raise your hand if
you could."
Most hands stay down.
"By the end of today, every hand in this room will be up. You will know the name, the job, and the feel of every major
component. And here is why that matters in Port Harcourt: when a customer walks into your shop and says 'my system is
slow,' the technician who knows the parts can ask the right questions and guess the right cause. The technician who only
knows how to click does not.
Now — let us meet the parts."
Then reveal the hook: Pull the cloth off the side table and hold up the motherboard — the strangest-looking part. Ask: "Is this
a radio? A small road? A city map?" Let them guess. Say:
"This is the motherboard — the main board every other part plugs into. Everything we learn today connects back to this
single board. By the end of the session, you will be able to point to every part on this board and tell me what it does."
Expected learner responses: Learners may guess "brain," "memory," "hard disk," or stay quiet. Accept all answers, because
some will be partly right. Some will confuse the motherboard with a "chip" or the whole case. Note these misconceptions
silently — you will address them in Section F.
Why this opener works: It creates curiosity about a familiar object, connects the lesson to real Port Harcourt life (cyber café,
school lab), and turns a dry list of parts into a mystery to be solved. It also sets the promise of the session clearly and
measurably.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a quick oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. Ask different learners each time; do not let one person
answer everything.
Question 1: "What is the difference between hardware and software?"
Expected answer: Hardware is the physical parts you can touch (the keyboard, the screen, the parts inside). Software is the
programs and instructions (Windows, a game, a browser) — you cannot touch it.
Common gap: Some learners say software is "the internet." Gently correct: the internet is something software uses, not
software itself.
Bridge line: "Today is entirely about hardware — the parts you can touch."
Question 2: "When you type a letter on the keyboard and it appears on the screen, is anything physical happening?"
Expected answer: Yes — electricity moves through the parts inside the computer to turn the keypress into a letter on the
screen.
Common gap: "It is magic" or "the computer just knows." Explain that the computer is following instructions, and the parts
we learn today are what carry those instructions.
Question 3: "What happens to your typed document when the electricity goes off suddenly?"
Expected answer: If you had not saved it, it is lost. Saved work stays. This introduces the idea of temporary vs permanent
storage, which this session clarifies with RAM and HDD/SSD.
Common gap: Some learners believe everything is always "saved." Use this as the hook for RAM vs storage.
Question 4: "Name one part inside a computer. Even if you are not sure, guess."
Expected answer: Any of: memory, hard disk, fan, "the thing that makes it fast." Accept everything.
Common gap: Many beginners name only the case or the screen. Reassure them that by the end of today they will name
eight or more.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Think of a computer like a restaurant kitchen. The kitchen has a head chef who does the cooking — that is the CPU. It
has a prep table where ingredients sit while being used — that is the RAM. It has a store room that keeps ingredients
even overnight — that is the storage. It has a gas cylinder that supplies the fire — that is the PSU. It has a manager
who coordinates everyone — that is the motherboard. Today, we meet the whole kitchen staff."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1 or 2, spend an extra three minutes re-explaining hardware vs software
before continuing; the rest of the session depends on that distinction.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a computer actually is
The idea. A computer is a machine that does three things over and over, extremely fast: it takes in information (input), it works
on that information (processing), and it produces a result (output). It also keeps information for later (storage).
Plain-English definition. A computer is an electronic machine made of separate physical parts that work together. Each part
does one main job, and no single part can do everything alone. When people say "the CPU is the brain," that is a comparison,
not the whole truth — a computer needs its memory, storage, power and board just as much as a person needs lungs, stomach
and heart, not only a brain.
Why the parts are separate. Have you ever noticed that you can buy a bigger hard disk, or add more memory, without buying
a new computer? That is deliberate. By making the parts separate, a computer can be repaired, upgraded and replaced part by
part instead of being thrown away whole. This modular design is the reason the computer repair trade exists — and the reason
you have a job to learn.
Real-life comparison. A computer is like a Nigerian market stall. The stall itself (the case) holds everything. The stall owner
(CPU) makes the decisions. The table in front (RAM) holds only what is being sold right now. The store behind (storage) keeps
the goods overnight. The generator (PSU) supplies power. The signboard (GPU/monitor) shows customers what is on offer. If
the generator dies, the whole stall stops — even if the owner and goods are fine. That is why one failed part can stop a whole
computer.
Important limitation / misconception. Learners often think the "computer" is the screen. It is not. The screen (monitor) is an
output device — a separate thing. The computer itself is the box (the system unit). Confusing the two causes wrong diagnoses
later, for example blaming the monitor when the fault is inside the case.
F.2 The motherboard — the city where everything meets
Plain-English definition. The motherboard is the large flat green (or sometimes black, blue or red) circuit board fixed inside the
case. It is the main board that every other internal component plugs into or connects to. A circuit board is a flat board that has
tiny metal pathways printed on it, along which electricity travels like traffic on roads.
Why it exists. If every component tried to connect to every other component directly, there would be thousands of messy
cables and the machine could never be built or repaired. The motherboard solves this by providing a single meeting point. Every
part plugs into the board, and the board carries signals between them.
What it does. The motherboard:
Holds the CPU socket — the special square socket where the processor sits.
Provides RAM slots — long thin slots where memory sticks click in.
Provides expansion slots (usually PCIe slots) where graphics cards and other add-on cards go.
Provides SATA ports where hard drives and SATA SSDs connect by cable.
Provides an M.2 slot (on modern boards) where NVMe SSDs slot in flat against the board.
Carries the main power connector (the 24-pin ATX connector) where the PSU plugs in.
Holds the back I/O panel — the row of ports at the back of the case for USB, network, audio and sometimes video cables.
Often holds a small coin-shaped CMOS battery that keeps the clock and basic settings alive when the machine is off.
Real-life comparison. The motherboard is like Rumuola junction in Port Harcourt — the central roundabout where many
roads meet. Cars (signals) come from all directions and pass through the junction. If the junction is blocked, traffic stops
everywhere at once, even though every car is fine.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A customer brings a desktop that powers on but never shows a picture. You open the case and
notice the RAM stick is not clicked fully into its slot on the motherboard. You reseat it and the machine works. Nothing was
"broken" — a connection on the main board simply was not complete. This is one of the most common quick fixes, and it is
why knowing the board matters.
Common misconceptions.
"The motherboard is the processor." No — the processor sits on the motherboard, but they are different parts.
"All motherboards are the same." They are not. Different boards fit different CPU types and memory types, and they are not
freely interchangeable. A beginner must never assume a random board fits a random CPU.
Limitations and safety. The motherboard is delicate. Its surface has tiny tracks and small components. Never place a board on a
carpet or a metal surface; never bend it; hold it by the edges; and always discharge static before touching it. Some repairs on the
board itself (soldering, replacing capacitors) are advanced and are not part of this course — a beginner should refer those to a
specialist.
F.3 The CPU — the processor (the brain)
Plain-English definition. The CPU (Central Processing Unit) is the small square chip that carries out the instructions of every
program. It does the arithmetic and the decision-making. It is often called the "brain" of the computer.
Why it exists. Someone has to actually do the work — add numbers, compare values, move data. The CPU is the part that
executes those instructions, billions of them every second.
What it does. It reads instructions from programs, performs calculations, makes simple decisions (is this number bigger than
that one?), and coordinates the rest of the parts by telling them what to do.
Key facts (plainly stated, no invented numbers):
Two main makers of desktop CPUs are Intel and AMD.
Speed is commonly described in gigahertz (GHz) — a measure of how many billions of cycles it can run per second.
Generally a higher GHz means faster work, but it is not the only factor.
A CPU runs hot when working. It therefore has a metal top (the heat spreader) and sits under a heatsink and fan (the
cooler) that carries heat away.
The cooler is critically important: without it, a CPU can overheat within seconds and shut the machine down, or be
permanently damaged.
The heatsink and fan — the CPU's cooling team. A heatsink is a block of metal with many thin fins. It presses against the
CPU to soak up heat. A fan blows air across the fins to carry that heat away. Between the CPU and the heatsink, a thin layer of
thermal paste (a grey, slightly sticky compound) fills the tiny gaps so heat passes through properly. Too little, too old or dried-
out paste is a common cause of overheating.
Real-life comparison. The CPU is like the head chef in a busy kitchen. The chef cooks very fast and gets very hot. The heatsink
and fan are like the kitchen's extractor fan and cooling system — without them, the chef collapses. The thermal paste is like
oil in a frying pan, helping heat pass evenly.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A customer complains their laptop shuts itself off after about twenty minutes of use and the
base gets very hot. You open it, find the fan clogged with dust and the thermal paste dried and crusty. You clean the fan and
replace the paste, and the shutdown stops. The CPU was never faulty — it was simply being cooked. (Full cleaning is Session 8;
today you only need to recognise the parts.)
Common misconceptions.
"A faster CPU always makes the computer faster." Not always. If the computer has too little RAM or a slow hard drive, a
faster CPU will not fix the slowness. The slowest part sets the pace, like a convoy that moves at the speed of its slowest
vehicle.
"The CPU is the whole computer." No — the CPU is one chip on the board.
Limitations and safety. Never touch the pins under a CPU or the contacts in the CPU socket. Even a tiny bend can stop the
machine working. Never power on a computer with the CPU cooler removed. CPU installation (Session 7 covers replacement)
requires great care and should be practised only on a machine you are authorised to repair.
F.4 RAM — the short-term worktable
Plain-English definition. RAM (Random Access Memory) is the computer's short-term, working memory. It holds the
programs and data that the computer is using right now. While the power is on, RAM keeps this information; the moment the
power goes off, everything in RAM is erased.
Why it exists. The CPU works incredibly fast, but permanent storage is much slower to read from and write to. If the CPU had to
fetch every tiny piece of data straight from the hard disk, it would spend most of its time waiting. RAM solves this: it is a fast,
temporary holding area close to the CPU.
What it does. When you open a program, the computer copies it from storage into RAM. As you work, the CPU reads from and
writes to RAM. When you close the program or switch off, RAM is cleared.
Key facts:
RAM comes in sticks (long, thin, flat modules) that click into the RAM slots on the motherboard.
Capacity is measured in gigabytes (GB) — 8 GB, 16 GB and 32 GB are common in modern machines.
More RAM lets more programs stay open at once without slowing down.
RAM comes in generations called DDR (DDR3, DDR4, DDR5). Different generations are not interchangeable — a DDR4
stick will not fit a DDR5 slot, and forcing it will damage the board.
Real-life comparison. RAM is the prep table in a kitchen. Ingredients being used right now sit on the table. When the kitchen
closes (power off), the table is cleared — nothing stays on it overnight. The store room (storage) is where ingredients are kept
permanently.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A cyber café operator says their machines "start hanging when many browser tabs are open."
You check and find only 4 GB of RAM. The fix is not a repair at all — it is an upgrade to more RAM. This is one of the most
common and profitable jobs in a Port Harcourt repair shop, and it exists purely because people confuse "slow" with "broken."
Common misconceptions.
"RAM and hard disk are the same thing." The single most common beginner mistake. RAM is temporary and small (GB) and
empties on power-off; storage is permanent and large (hundreds of GB or TB).
"More RAM makes the computer store more files." No — more RAM lets you run more at once. Storing files is the job of
storage.
"Adding RAM always speeds things up." Only if RAM was the bottleneck. If the slowness is from a failing hard drive or
malware, more RAM will not help.
Limitations and safety. RAM sticks are delicate. Hold them by their edges and never touch the gold contacts — the shiny strip
along the bottom. Oils from your fingers on those contacts cause poor connections. Always handle RAM with anti-static
protection.
(Note: "GB" for RAM and "GB" for storage use the same letters but mean different jobs — one is the size of the worktable, the
other is the size of the store room.)
F.5 Storage — the store room (HDD, SSD, NVMe)
Plain-English definition. Storage is where the computer keeps information permanently — the operating system, your
programs, documents, photos and videos. Unlike RAM, storage keeps its contents when the power is off.
Why it exists. You need your files to survive when the machine is switched off. Storage is the long-term memory of the
computer.
The three main types a beginner must know:
1. HDD — Hard Disk Drive. Inside an HDD are shiny magnetic platters that spin, with a read/write head that moves across
them — like an old record player. Because parts move, an HDD is slower, can be damaged by drops or knocks, and makes soft
whirring or clicking sounds while working. It is usually cheaper per gigabyte and comes in large sizes (for example 1 TB, 2 TB).
2. SSD — Solid State Drive. An SSD has no moving parts — it stores data in electronic memory chips. It is much faster than
an HDD, more durable (nothing to break when dropped), uses less power, and is silent. It is usually more expensive per
gigabyte than an HDD.
3. NVMe — Non-Volatile Memory Express. NVMe is the fastest type of SSD. It plugs flat into a special slot (the M.2 slot)
directly on the motherboard, rather than connecting by cable like a SATA drive. It is commonly used for the operating system
and the programs that must load fast.
SATA — mention this because learners will hear it constantly. SATA is a standard for connecting storage drives and is the name
of both the cable and the port. A SATA SSD uses the SATA cable; an NVMe SSD slots straight into the board.
Real-life comparison. An HDD is like a warehouse with a worker on a bicycle fetching each item — it works, but takes time.
An SSD is like a warehouse where every item sits on a numbered shelf within arm's reach — same goods, far faster to get.
An NVMe is like a shelf right beside your hand.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A customer says "my computer takes forever to start up — I have to wait five minutes." You
find the machine runs on an old HDD. You clone the system onto an SSD, and startup drops to seconds. The customer thinks
you performed magic, but you simply moved the data from a spinning warehouse to an electronic shelf. Explaining this plainly
is part of good customer service (Session 10).
Common misconceptions.
"SSDs are just smaller hard drives." No — they are a completely different technology (electronic vs mechanical). Size is not
the difference; technology is.
"A 256 GB SSD holds less than a 256 GB HDD." Both hold about the same; they differ in speed and durability, not in how
much they can hold for the same number.
"I can drop an HDD because it is metal." Do not. The internal platters and head are extremely delicate; a fall can destroy it
and your data.
Limitations and safety. HDDs must be handled gently and never shaken while running. SSDs are more robust but can still fail,
and when they fail they often give little warning. This is why backups matter. "Storage fails" is one of the most common real
repair cases, so treat data with respect from the very first session.
F.6 The PSU — the power supply unit (the gas cylinder)
Plain-English definition. The PSU (Power Supply Unit) is usually a metal box at the top or bottom-back of the case. It takes
electricity from the wall socket (which in Nigeria is commonly around 220–240 volts alternating current) and converts it into
the lower, steady direct-current voltages that the computer's parts need (commonly +3.3 V, +5 V and +12 V).
Why it exists. The wall socket supplies one kind of electricity; the delicate components need a different, carefully controlled
kind. Without the PSU, the computer cannot run. A computer has no internal battery to fall back on (a desktop, unlike a laptop,
has no built-in battery).
What it does. It converts and delivers power, through cables, to the motherboard (the big 24-pin connector), the CPU (the 4-pin
or 8-pin connector), the drives and the graphics card.
Key facts:
Capacity is measured in watts (W) — for example 400 W, 500 W, 650 W. The PSU must supply enough total power for all
the components together.
It provides several different voltages through different cables.
A failing PSU can cause random crashes, failure to start, sudden shutdowns, or damage to other components.
Real-life comparison. The PSU is like the generator or gas cylinder that supplies the fire for the kitchen. If it is weak, everything
in the kitchen suffers. If it is faulty, nothing can cook at all, no matter how good the chef (CPU) is.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. (This is the Session's case study, expanded here.) A customer's desktop will not power on at all
— no lights, no fan spin. You check the wall socket and the cable first (always the simplest things first). Those are fine. You then
test the PSU and find it has failed. You replace it. This is one of the most common reasons a computer shows "no sign of life."
Common misconceptions.
"Any PSU will do; more watts is always better." A PSU must match the machine's needs and, importantly, must be of good
quality. A cheap, badly built PSU can fail and damage everything else.
"I can repair a PSU myself." No. Inside the PSU are components that can hold a dangerous charge even when unplugged. A
beginner must never open a PSU. If it is faulty, replace it. This is a hard safety rule.
Limitations and safety.
Always unplug the computer from the wall before opening the case.
Never open, drill, or tamper with the PSU itself — the components inside can store a lethal charge.
If you smell burning or see smoke, unplug immediately and do not touch it beyond that. Replace the PSU; never try to "fix"
a burning one.
This session does not test or replace the PSU. Those skills come later (Sessions 5 and 7), and even then, the PSU is replaced,
not opened.
F.7 The GPU — the graphics card (the signboard painter)
Plain-English definition. The GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) handles images, video and everything shown on the screen. It
turns the computer's data into the picture you see.
Two types:
1. Integrated graphics — built into the CPU or motherboard. Good enough for everyday work: browsing, typing, watching
videos, light school work. It shares the main memory.
2. Dedicated graphics card (GPU) — a separate card that plugs into an expansion slot (PCIe) on the motherboard. It is far
more powerful, has its own memory (called VRAM), usually has its own fan, and may draw extra power from the PSU. It is
used for gaming, video editing, design and 3D work.
Why it exists. Modern screens show millions of tiny dots (pixels) that must be calculated and redrawn many times per second.
The CPU can do this, but a dedicated GPU does it far faster and frees the CPU for other work.
Key facts:
The two main makers of dedicated GPUs are NVIDIA and AMD.
A dedicated card has its own memory and its own cooling.
Some powerful cards need a dedicated power cable from the PSU.
Real-life comparison. If the CPU is the head chef, the GPU is the artist who paints the signboard. The chef cooks; the artist
makes it look good to the customer. A busy restaurant needs both, and if the artist is weak, the signboard looks poor even if the
food is perfect.
Realistic repair-shop scenario. A customer says their monitor shows "no signal" even though the computer seems to be
running (fans spin, lights on). You check the display cable — it is plugged into the motherboard's port, not the graphics card's
port. Because a dedicated card is fitted, the motherboard port is often disabled. You move the cable to the card and the picture
appears. The customer was sure the computer was "dead," but it was a one-minute fix — because you understood the GPU.
Common misconceptions.
"A graphics card is only for games." Not true — many design and video jobs need one, and even everyday machines use
graphics (built into the CPU).
"No display always means the GPU is broken." No — it may be a loose cable, an unseated RAM stick, or the cable in the
wrong port. This is why Session 4 teaches a proper workflow.
Limitations and safety. A dedicated GPU can be large and heavy; support it when removing it so it does not bend in its slot.
Some cards keep warm after use; let the machine cool before handling. Never touch the gold contact strip at the bottom of the
card.
F.8 Expansion cards, cooling fans, ports and connectors
Expansion cards. An expansion card is any add-on card that plugs into an expansion slot to add a feature the board lacks —
for example a sound card, a network (Ethernet) card, or a TV tuner card. Many modern motherboards have sound, network
and graphics built in, so dedicated cards are less common than they once were, but you will still meet them, especially in older
or specialist machines.
Cooling fans. A computer has several fans. The CPU fan sits on the CPU heatsink. The case fans move air in and out of the case.
The GPU fan cools the graphics card. The PSU fan cools the power supply. Purpose: heat is the enemy of electronics; fans and
heatsinks keep parts within safe temperatures. Dust is the enemy of fans — a dust-clogged fan cannot cool, and the machine
overheats. (Cleaning is Session 8.)
Ports and connectors — where the outside world plugs in. At the back of the case on the motherboard's I/O panel (and
often the front) you will find:
USB ports — for keyboards, mice, flash drives, printers.
Network (Ethernet/RJ45) port — for a network cable.
Audio jacks — for speakers, headphones, microphone.
Display ports — HDMI, DisplayPort or VGA, for monitors (on the motherboard if integrated graphics are used, or on the
graphics card if one is fitted).
Power socket — where the power cable plugs in (at the PSU).
Internal connectors to recognise:
24-pin ATX power connector — the big one from the PSU to the motherboard.
4-pin/8-pin CPU power connector — a smaller PSU cable near the CPU.
SATA data cable — flat, with a small L-shaped connector, links drives to the board.
SATA power connector — a wider, flat connector from the PSU to drives.
Fan connectors — small 3-pin or 4-pin plugs for fans.
Front-panel connectors — tiny plugs from the case's power button, reset button and front USB/audio to the board. These
are fiddly and easy to misplace; we handle them carefully in Session 3.
Optical drive (legacy). An optical drive (CD/DVD drive) is a bay device that reads and writes discs. It is now largely legacy —
modern computers, especially laptops, often have none. You will still see them in older machines. Recognise it as a storage
device with a disc tray, connected by SATA like a hard drive.
Real-life comparison. Ports and connectors are the doors and gates of a compound. Each door is shaped for one purpose: the
gate for cars (power), the pedestrian gate (USB), the network gate (Ethernet). You cannot drive a car through the pedestrian gate,
and you cannot plug a USB cable into an Ethernet port — the shapes protect you from yourself.
Common misconceptions.
"All USB ports are the same speed." They look alike but can differ in speed and purpose. Do not invent exact numbers;
simply teach that they are not identical.
"The VGA/HDMI port is on the wall." Some monitors and older machines use VGA; modern ones use HDMI/DisplayPort.
Learn to look for the port on the machine and match it to the cable.
Limitations and safety. Never force a connector — every plug has one correct orientation. USB plugs only fit one way (though
USB-C fits either way). Forcing a connector bends pins and damages ports. Power off and unplug before plugging or
unplugging internal cables.
F.9 How it all fits together — the relationship and data flow
The relationship in one paragraph. The motherboard connects everything. The CPU does the thinking and sits under a
heatsink and fan to stay cool. The RAM is the fast workspace the CPU uses right now. The storage keeps everything
permanently. The PSU feeds electricity to all of them. The GPU paints what you see. Fans keep the heat down. Ports and
connectors bring the outside world in and out. Remove any one and the whole system fails or runs badly — they are a team,
not a list.
How data flows — follow one keypress from finger to screen. This is the single most useful mental model a repair
technician can carry.
1. Input: You press the letter A on the keyboard.
2. The signal travels through the USB port and along the motherboard to the CPU.
3. The CPU processes the instruction: "show an A."
4. The CPU uses RAM as its workspace while it decides what to do.
5. The CPU sends drawing instructions to the GPU.
6. The GPU turns that into the picture and sends it out through a display port (HDMI/DisplayPort/VGA).
7. The monitor shows the letter A.
8. Meanwhile, if the program is saving the document, the CPU writes the data to storage so it survives when the power goes
off.
9. Throughout all of this, the PSU supplies the electricity and the fans keep everything cool.
Why this matters in the shop. When a customer says "my typing is delayed," you now have a map: is the keyboard/port at fault
(step 2)? The CPU overloaded (step 3)? Too little RAM (step 4)? The GPU or cable (step 6)? The storage busy (step 8)? A good
technician walks the flow, one step at a time. That is exactly the skill Session 4 builds into a formal workflow.
Real-life comparison. Think of sending a letter through the Nigerian postal system. You write it (input), it goes to the local
post office (motherboard), the sorting clerk reads the address (CPU), uses a desk to sort it (RAM), the delivery rider takes it out
(GPU), and it arrives at the house (monitor). If the rider is missing, the letter never arrives, even though you wrote it perfectly.
Common misconceptions.
"The CPU does everything." No — the CPU directs, but RAM, storage, GPU and PSU each do their own jobs.
"Data flows only one way." It flows constantly in both directions: input comes in, output goes out, and the CPU reads and
writes memory and storage continuously.
Limitations and safety. Do not carry this table too far into advanced territory (buses, chipset, cache) — that is beyond a
beginner course. The goal is a working mental map, not an engineering degree.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
The unplugged desktop with side panel removed, on the anti-static mat, on a clean, well-lit table.
Loose component samples in a row, each with a name card placed face-down or hidden so learners must recall.
The wrist strap, drawn on and clipped to bare metal of the case.
The projector showing a labelled motherboard diagram.
The dead/fault parts (RAM, HDD, PSU) clearly labelled "FAULT — LOOK ONLY."
A torch to point inside the case.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — live identification (8 minutes)
Say: "I am going to open up a real computer and name every part inside it. Follow my finger with your eyes, then write the
name on your chart."
Do this in order, pointing with the torch (never with a screwdriver, to avoid slips):
1. Point to the big board: "This is the motherboard — the main board everything plugs into."
2. Point to the square chip under the fan: "This is the CPU, under its heatsink and fan. The CPU is the brain; the fan keeps it
cool."
3. Point to the long thin sticks: "These are RAM sticks. Short-term memory. Notice the shiny gold contacts along the bottom
— we never touch those."
4. Point to the boxy drive: "This is a hard disk drive — permanent storage, with moving parts inside."
5. Point to an SSD or M.2 stick: "This is an SSD or NVMe — permanent storage with no moving parts, much faster."
6. Point to the metal box with many cables: "This is the power supply — it feeds electricity to everything. We never open
this box."
7. Point to the large card with its own fan: "This is the graphics card — it paints what you see on the screen."
8. Point to the spinning fan: "This is a cooling fan — it moves heat out of the case."
9. Point to the row of coloured plugs on the board: "These are connectors — power, SATA and front-panel cables plug in
here."
Expected result: Learners can repeat each name and a one-line job for each part.
Common errors to watch: The instructor must not use a metal tool to point at a powered board (it is unplugged, but the habit
matters). Do not let learners touch the CPU pins or the RAM gold contacts.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Which part stores your files permanently?" (Storage.)
"Which part forgets everything when the power goes off?" (RAM.)
"Which part would you change to make games run better?" (The GPU — or add RAM; accept both with reasoning.)
"Why does the CPU need a fan?" (It gets hot when working.)
G.3 Demonstration 2 — correct, safe component handling (7 minutes)
Say: "Now I will show you how to hold these parts so you do not damage them."
Do this on the projector, slowly:
1. Put on the anti-static wrist strap and clip it to bare metal. "This keeps static electricity from jumping into the parts."
2. Pick up the RAM stick. "I hold it by its edges. I do not touch the gold contacts at the bottom."
3. Pick up the GPU. "I hold it by the metal bracket and the edges, and I support its weight so it does not bend."
4. Pick up the HDD. "I hold it firmly by the sides and I do not drop it — the inside is fragile."
5. Pick up the CPU (if a spare is available). "I hold it by the edges. I never touch the pins underneath or the contacts in the
socket."
6. Point to the "FAULT" parts. "Remember, static damage can be invisible. A part can be dead and look perfect. That is why we
protect every part, every time."
Expected result: Learners can demonstrate the correct grip for RAM, GPU, HDD and CPU.
Reasoning to state aloud: "I wear the strap because a static spark you cannot even feel can kill a chip. I hold boards by the
edges because oils on my fingers corrode the gold contacts. I never open the PSU because it can hold a deadly charge even
when unplugged."
Common errors to watch: Learners grabbing the RAM contacts, touching the CPU pins, or resting boards on carpet or metal.
Correct immediately and kindly.

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)

Task: "The Repair Shop Intake"
Scenario: You are the newest technician at Elias Computer Clinic, Diobu, Port Harcourt. A young man walks in carrying a
desktop computer and complaining. He does not know the names of any parts. Your job is to translate his plain complaints into
the correct technical component, and explain what each part does so he trusts your diagnosis.
Instructions: For each complaint below, write (a) which component is most likely involved, (b) what that component does,
and (c) one question you would ask to find out more. Work alone.
Complaints given to you:
1. "The machine makes a soft ticking sound and sometimes the files take long to open."
2. "It starts but the screen stays black, and I can hear the fan running."
3. "When I have Facebook, WhatsApp and music open at the same time, everything freezes."
4. "Nothing happens at all when I press the power button. No light, no sound."
5. "It gets very hot and switches itself off after some time."
6. "It works, but it is slow to start up in the morning and programs take long to open — it has never been upgraded."
Requirements:
Answer all six complaints.
For each, give a one-sentence plain-English explanation a non-technical customer would understand.
No copying the instructor's demonstration wording — use your own words.
Constraints:
Assume this is a normal desktop computer from around a cyber café.
Keep each explanation to two or three sentences.
Use correct component names.
Time allowance: 15 minutes.
Success criteria:
All six complaints matched to a component with correct reasoning.
Explanations use correct names and are understandable to a non-technical person.
At least one question written for each complaint.
Hints if stuck:
Hint 1: "No power at all, no light" points to the part that supplies electricity to everything.
Hint 2: "Freezes when many programs are open" points to the fast temporary memory.
Hint 3: "Slow to start up, never upgraded" points to permanent storage with moving parts.
Hint 4: "Gets hot and switches off" points to the cooling parts and the CPU.
Instructor answer key (do not show learners):
1. HDD (hard disk drive). It stores files permanently using spinning platters and a moving head; a soft ticking and slow file
access suggests a failing drive. Question: "When did the ticking start, and have you backed up your important files?"
(Possible backup needed before any work — data safety first.)
2. No display with fans running → likely RAM not seated, GPU/cable issue, or display cable in the wrong port. Component
focus: RAM / GPU / display connection. Question: "Did anything change before it happened — a move, a power cut, new
parts?" (Accept a well-reasoned answer that names RAM, GPU or the cable and explains why.)
3. RAM. It is the fast temporary workspace; when too little, the machine slows badly. Question: "How much memory does it
have, and does it happen only when many programs are open?"
4. PSU (power supply). It converts wall electricity to the voltages the parts need; if it fails, there is no sign of life. Question:
"Have you checked the wall socket and the power cable, and does any light come on at all?" (Rule out the simplest things
first.)
5. Cooling system — heatsink/fan and thermal paste, plus the CPU. Dust or dried paste causes overheating and shutdown.
Question: "When did it last have its dust cleaned, and does the base or back feel very hot?"
6. Storage — an old HDD. Slow startup and program opening on a never-upgraded machine suggests an HDD; an SSD
upgrade would help. Question: "How old is the machine, and how much free space is left on the drive?"
Marking guidance (out of 12 — 2 per complaint):
Correct component named (1)
Correct, plain explanation (1)
Bonus credit within the same mark if a sensible question is also given.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu runs a small business centre near the University of Port Harcourt. One Monday morning his main desktop
will not come on at all. He presses the power button repeatedly — nothing: no fan, no light, not even a flicker. He has a queue
of customers waiting to print, type and browse. He calls a technician friend, who says over the phone, "It is a motherboard
problem, you need a new computer" — without opening the machine. Chinedu is about to spend money he cannot afford.
Before he does, he carries the machine to your shop.
Relevant facts.
The computer worked perfectly on Saturday evening.
There was a heavy thunderstorm and a power "surge" (voltage spike) on Sunday night in the area.
The machine is a normal desktop with an internal PSU; no battery.
The customer has important files on the hard drive that he needs for his business.
Decision points.
1. Should you trust the phone diagnosis of "motherboard" and sell him a new machine, or investigate?
2. Where do you start — the most expensive part or the simplest checks?
3. What component is most consistent with "worked Saturday, power surge Sunday, completely dead Monday"?
4. What must you protect before doing anything else?
Possible approaches.
1. Accept the phone diagnosis and replace the whole computer (or motherboard). Fast, but expensive and possibly wrong.
2. Follow a basic order: check the wall socket and power cable, check the PSU, then look deeper. Cheap and methodical.
3. Try to repair the PSU yourself. Unsafe and forbidden — the PSU is replaced, never opened.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. Check the simplest things first (socket, cable, that the machine is actually
receiving power), then test the PSU. Given the surge, a failed PSU is the most likely cause of a completely dead machine.
Replace the PSU (never open it). Before returning the machine, confirm his hard drive is healthy so his business files are safe.
Only if the machine is still dead after a known-good PSU is fitted would you suspect the motherboard — and even then, you
confirm by testing, not guessing.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is fast but risks replacing a healthy expensive board and charging the customer for the wrong repair — bad for
reputation and for the customer.
Approach 2 takes a little longer but is safe, cheap and usually correct, and it protects the customer's data and your
reputation.
Trying to repair the PSU is dangerous: the charge inside can injure or kill, and it is outside the scope of this course.
Professional connection. This case is exactly why component knowledge matters. The technician who knows that the PSU
converts and supplies all the power immediately suspects it after a surge, and checks it early and safely. The technician who
only knows "it won't on, so buy a new one" costs the customer money and loses trust. In Port Harcourt, where power surges are
common, this single insight saves customers thousands of naira — and wins you loyal customers who tell their friends.
Transfer exercise. "A customer's computer runs but the display is blank and fans are spinning. Before blaming the GPU, what
three simple things would you check?" (Display cable connected; cable in the correct port — motherboard vs graphics card;
RAM seated fully.)
Safety reminder. Everything above is done with the machine unplugged. The PSU is replaced, never opened. Working on
mains-powered equipment or opening a PSU is never permitted in this course.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live troubleshooting guide during the practical. When a learner struggles, ask them to check the relevant
row rather than giving the answer immediately.
K.1 Touching the gold contacts on RAM or expansion cards
Symptoms: A part that was working now fails to detect; intermittent faults; an upgrade that "does not show up." Likely causes:
Finger oils or dirt on the gold contacts create a poor connection. The cause is confirmed only after you inspect the contacts and
reseat the part. How to investigate: Look at the contacts for smudges or dullness; check the part seats fully with clips closed.
Safe corrective steps: (Instructor-supervised) remove the part with the machine unplugged, wipe the contacts gently with
isopropyl alcohol (90%+) on a lint-free cloth if available, let it dry fully, and reseat holding only the edges. How to confirm
resolved: The part is detected again and the machine behaves normally. How to prevent recurrence: Always hold boards by
the edges; never touch the contacts.
K.2 Confusing RAM with storage
Symptoms: The learner says "add a bigger RAM to store more videos" or "the RAM is full of files." Likely cause: The two
concepts were never separated in the learner's mind. How to investigate: Ask the learner what happens to a document on the
"RAM" when the power goes off. If they say "it stays," they are confusing RAM with storage. Corrective steps: Re-teach with
the prep-table and store-room comparison. RAM empties on power-off; storage keeps data. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can state the difference in one sentence using their own example. How to prevent recurrence: Use the comparison
every time the topic returns.
K.3 Handling an HDD roughly or while it is running
Symptoms: Clicking or grinding sounds after a knock; the drive stops being detected; files disappear. Likely cause: The internal
platters or head were damaged by a shock; a mechanical failure. (Confirmed by the noise and by a drive test in a later session.)
How to investigate: Listen for clicking, check whether the drive is detected, and (later, with tools) read its health. Safe
corrective steps: Stop using the drive immediately; if there is important data, do not keep power-cycling it — refer for data
recovery. Replace the drive (Session 7). How to confirm resolved: A replacement drive works normally. How to prevent
recurrence: Never shake, drop or move a running HDD.
K.4 "No display always means the GPU is dead"
Symptoms: Learner immediately blames the graphics card when the screen is blank. Likely cause: Skipping the simple checks
(cable, port, RAM seating). How to investigate: Check the display cable is connected and in the correct port; check whether
RAM is fully seated; test with a different monitor/cable if authorised. Corrective steps: Fix the simple cause first; only then
consider the GPU. How to confirm resolved: The display returns. How to prevent recurrence: Always follow a simplest-first
order (this becomes the Session 4 workflow).
K.5 Forgetting anti-static protection
Symptoms: No immediate symptom — the danger is invisible. Later, a part mysteriously fails. Likely cause: Static electricity
discharged into a component, causing latent (delayed) damage. How to investigate: You usually cannot see it; that is the point.
Prevent it rather than detect it. Corrective steps: Always use the wrist strap or touch bare metal before handling parts. How to
confirm resolved: You cannot always confirm — prevention is the only reliable answer. How to prevent recurrence: Make
the strap part of the routine, like wearing gloves before handling a hot pot.
K.6 Forgetting to unplug before opening the case
Symptoms: Risk of electric shock, short circuit, or damage; a fan may start unexpectedly. Likely cause: Rushing. How to
investigate: N/A — this is a discipline issue. Corrective steps: Always unplug from the wall and press the power button once
to discharge residual electricity before opening the case. How to confirm resolved: The machine is verified unplugged and
discharged. How to prevent recurrence: Build the habit into every session's checklist.
Distinguishing likely vs confirmed: In every row above, the "likely cause" becomes a "confirmed cause" only after you check it
— see the contacts, hear the click, test the drive, reseat the part and see the fault disappear. Teach learners to confirm before
they "fix."

Common mistakes to avoid: confusing RAM with storage; touching/claiming to touch contacts; calling the CPU "the whole

Instructor reference solution (summary, do not show learners beforehand):
Motherboard: main board; connects all parts; common problem = loose cable/expansion card.
CPU (+heatsink/fan): carries out instructions; gets hot; common problem = overheating from dust or dried thermal paste.
RAM: fast temporary memory; empties on power-off; common problem = not fully seated, or too little for heavy use.
HDD: permanent storage with moving parts; common problem = mechanical failure (clicking).
SSD: permanent electronic storage, faster; common problem = can fail with little warning — hence backups.
PSU: converts wall power to needed voltages; common problem = total failure (no sign of life) — and it is never opened.
GPU: renders images; common problem = no display (often a cable/port issue, not the card).
Cooling fan: moves heat out; common problem = dust clogging.
Ports/connectors: connect outside devices; common problem = bent pins from forcing plugs.
Data-flow paragraph: keyboard → USB port → motherboard → CPU → RAM works with CPU → GPU → monitor; storage
and PSU noted.

## M. Knowledge Check

(Learner version. Answers are in the Instructor Key below — do not read the key until you have attempted all questions.)
Q1. Short answer. In one sentence each, say what the motherboard, the CPU and RAM do.
Q2. Multiple choice. Which component stores your files permanently, even when the power is off? a) RAM b) CPU c) Storage
(HDD/SSD) d) PSU
Q3. Explain in your own words. Why is RAM described as "short-term" and storage as "permanent"? Give your own everyday
example.
Q4. Practical identification. A customer's computer makes a soft ticking sound from a spinning part inside and files open
slowly. Which component is most likely involved, and why?
Q5. Error detection / truth-check. A learner says: "I will add more RAM so I can store more videos." Find the mistake and
correct it.
Q6. Scenario. A desktop powers on — the fans spin and a light shows — but the screen stays black. List three things you
would check, in order from simplest to most complex.
Q7. Short answer. Why must the PSU never be opened, even when it is faulty?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. The motherboard is the main circuit board that connects all the other components; the CPU carries out the instructions of
programs (the "brain"); RAM is the fast temporary workspace that holds data currently in use and empties when the power goes
off.
A2. (c) Storage (HDD/SSD).
A3. RAM holds only what is being used right now and loses everything when the power goes off, like items on a kitchen prep
table cleared at closing. Storage keeps files even with no power, like goods in a store room. Everyday example: an open, unsaved
document lives in RAM; once you save it, it goes to storage and survives a power cut.
A4. The HDD (hard disk drive). It has moving parts — spinning platters and a read/write head — so a ticking sound and slow
file access suggest a mechanical problem. (Advise protecting the customer's data before further use.)
A5. The mistake is confusing storage with RAM. RAM does not store files; it holds programs and data being used right now and
empties on power-off. To store more videos you need a bigger storage drive (or an external drive), not more RAM. (More RAM
helps when many programs are open at once.)
A6. Accept any sensible, simplest-first order such as: (1) check the display cable is connected and in the correct port
(motherboard vs graphics card); (2) check the monitor has power and is switched on; (3) reseat the RAM (a common cause of
no display). GPU comes only after these.
A7. The PSU contains components that can hold a dangerous electrical charge even when unplugged from the wall. Opening it
risks a serious or fatal shock, and repairing it is outside the scope of a beginner course. If it is faulty, it is replaced, never opened.
Marking: Award up to 2 marks per question (Q3 and Q6 up to 3 each). Total ≈ 16–18; scale proportionally. Full credit
requires the correct component name and correct reasoning.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned what is inside a computer and what each part does. You met the motherboard (the
board that connects everything), the CPU (the part that does the work, kept cool by a heatsink and fan), RAM (fast temporary
memory that empties when the power goes off), storage (permanent memory — HDD and SSD), the PSU (the part that
supplies electricity and must never be opened), the GPU (the part that paints the screen), the cooling fans, and the ports and
connectors that bring the outside world in and out. You also learned how data flows from a keypress to the screen, how a
desktop differs from a laptop, and how to handle parts safely by their edges with anti-static protection.
Five important takeaways:
1. A computer is a team of parts, each with one main job — none works alone.
2. RAM is temporary; storage is permanent. Never mix them up.
3. HDD is mechanical and slower; SSD is electronic and faster; NVMe is the fastest SSD.
4. The PSU supplies all the power and is never opened — replace it, never repair it.
5. Always handle parts by the edges, protect against static, and unplug before opening any case.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I name at least eight components inside a computer without looking?
Can I explain RAM vs storage to a customer in one sentence?
Can I trace how a keypress becomes a letter on the screen?
Can I state why the PSU must never be opened?
Connection to the next session: Now that you know the parts, next session you will learn how to work around them safely —
how to protect yourself from electricity and from ESD, how to choose and use the right tools, and how to set up a clean, safe
workspace. Knowing the parts without knowing safety is dangerous; Session 2 makes you safe.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner names all major components without prompting and gives each a correct one-line function.
The learner spontaneously holds RAM/boards by the edges and does not touch gold contacts.
The learner correctly separates RAM from storage and HDD from SSD in their own words.
The learner can trace the data flow from input to output and name the parts at each step.
The learner treats the PSU with respect and states it must never be opened.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"The monitor is the computer." → The system unit is the computer; the monitor is output only.
"RAM and hard disk are the same." → Temporary vs permanent; GB of workspace vs GB of store room.
"The CPU is the whole computer / the case." → The CPU is one chip under the cooler.
"A graphics card is only for games." → Used for design and video too; and integrated graphics exist.
"The PSU can be fixed." → It is replaced; opening it is dangerous and forbidden.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Component flash cards: write each component on one card and its function on another; match them, then shuffle and
repeat.
2. Draw-and-label: sketch a simple desktop with three parts and label them; repeat from memory.
3. Data-flow relay: learners physically stand in a line holding component name cards (keyboard, port, CPU, RAM, GPU,
monitor) and pass a paper "signal" along the line in the correct order.
4. Analogy practice: ask the learner to invent their own kitchen/market analogy for three components.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed Component Chart (names filled, functions to finish) and work alongside
the learner at the stations; pair with a careful partner.
Level 2 (standard): complete the exercise as written, independently with the group.
Level 3 (challenge): research and explain one additional component (for example a sound card, network card, optical drive,
or the CMOS battery), and describe the data flow for saving a file, not just typing a letter.
