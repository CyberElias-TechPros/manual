# Understanding the Computer

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
Field Detail
Session Number 1

Field Detail

Duration 105 minutes
Difficulty Absolute Beginner
Prerequisite Knowledge None

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Name and identify the five main hardware components of a desktop computer (monitor, keyboard, mouse, CPU/system
unit, speakers) and explain what each one does.
2. Explain the difference between a desktop computer and a laptop computer.
3. Power on a computer correctly by pressing the power button and waiting for it to start.
4. Log in to a Windows user account by clicking their name and typing a password.
5. Identify the desktop, taskbar, Start button, and system tray on a Windows screen.
6. Shut down a computer correctly using the Start menu (not by pulling the plug).
7. Explain why shutting down correctly matters (prevents file damage and data loss).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
The Mystery Box
Instructor Setup: Before class, place a computer mouse inside a closed cardboard box. Do not tell the learners what is inside.
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Welcome to Cyber Elias Academy. Before we turn anything on, I want to play a
quick game. I have a box here. Inside this box is something that you will use every single day of this course. I am going to pass
the box around. Gently shake it, listen to it, feel how heavy it is — but do not open it. When it reaches you, whisper your guess
to the person next to you. We will reveal the answer together."
What Happens: The box is passed around. Learners guess. Common guesses: "a phone," "a book," "a mouse," "a keyboard."
Reveal: Open the box and hold up the mouse. Say: "This is a computer mouse. It is one of the five main parts of a computer. By
the end of today, you will know all five parts, what they do, and how to use them. You will also turn on a computer for the first
time — and turn it off properly. Let us begin."
Expected Learner Response: Excitement, curiosity, some nervousness. The activity breaks the ice and introduces the first
hardware component in a memorable way.

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: What Is a Computer? (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "A computer is a machine that takes information in, works on it, and gives information back out. That is all it
does. It is like a very fast, very obedient assistant. You tell it what to do, and it does it — exactly what you say, not what you
meant. If you tell it the wrong thing, it does the wrong thing. That is why we learn to give it clear instructions.
Think of a computer like a kitchen. The kitchen has tools — a knife, a stove, a fridge. The tools do not cook by themselves. A
person uses the tools to prepare food. In the same way, a computer has tools — a screen, a keyboard, a mouse. You use these
tools to do work: writing letters, sending messages, finding information, keeping records.
The word 'computer' comes from 'compute,' which means to calculate. The first computers were built to do mathematics.
Today, computers do much more than calculate. They store photographs, play music, connect people across the world, and run
businesses. But at the heart, they are still machines that follow instructions."
Key Point: A computer is a tool. It is not magic. It is not alive. It does exactly what you tell it to do.
Topic 2: Desktop vs Laptop (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Look around the room. The computers you see are called desktop computers. They are called 'desktop'
because they sit on a desk. They have separate parts: a monitor (the screen), a keyboard, a mouse, and a system unit (the box
that does the thinking). These parts are connected by cables.
A laptop computer is different. A laptop is one single unit. The screen, keyboard and thinking-box are all folded together. You
can carry a laptop to a different room, to a café, to another city. A desktop stays on the desk.
Here is a simple way to remember: a desktop is like a landline phone — it stays in one place. A laptop is like a mobile phone —
you can carry it with you.
Both types do the same work. The skills you learn today work on both. If you use a laptop at home, everything I teach you today
will still apply."
Show: Hold up a picture of a desktop and a laptop (or point to the desktops in the room and then hold up a laptop if available).
Topic 3: The Five Main Hardware Components (15 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Every computer — desktop or laptop, cheap or expensive, old or new — has five main parts. Let us learn
them one by one. I will hold up each part, tell you its name, and tell you what it does."
Component 1: The Monitor (Screen) "This is the monitor. It is the screen. It shows you what the computer is doing. It is like a
television screen, but instead of showing programmes, it shows your work. Without a monitor, you cannot see what the
computer is doing. The monitor does not think — it only shows. It is like a window: you look through it to see what is
happening inside the computer."
Component 2: The Keyboard "This is the keyboard. It is how you talk to the computer. When you press a key, the computer
receives a letter, a number or a command. The keyboard is like a typewriter, but with extra keys for special jobs. You will learn
the keyboard in detail in the next session."
Component 3: The Mouse "This is the mouse. It is how you point at things on the screen. When you move the mouse on the
desk, a small arrow on the screen moves too. You use the mouse to click on things, to select things, to open things. You will
learn the mouse in the next session."
Component 4: The CPU / System Unit "This box is called the CPU, or the system unit, or sometimes just 'the computer.' CPU
stands for Central Processing Unit. This is the part that thinks. Everything the computer does — every calculation, every image,
every word — is processed inside this box. The monitor, keyboard and mouse are just tools for talking to this box. The box is
the brain. On a laptop, this brain is hidden underneath the keyboard."
Component 5: Speakers "These are speakers. They let the computer play sound — music, videos, notification sounds. Some
computers have speakers built into the monitor. Some have separate speakers like these. Some people use headphones instead.
Speakers are optional — a computer works fine without them — but they make the experience more pleasant."
Summary Table (write on whiteboard):
What It Does Real-Life Comparison
Monitor Shows you what the computer is doing A television screen
Keyboard How you type instructions to the computer A typewriter
Mouse How you point and click on the screen A finger pointing
CPU / System Unit The brain — does all the thinking The engine of a car
Speakers Plays sound A radio
Key Point: The monitor, keyboard, mouse and speakers are called "peripherals" — they are the tools you use to talk to the
brain (the CPU). The CPU is the actual computer. The peripherals are the computer's face, hands and voice.
Topic 4: Powering On and Logging In (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Now let us turn on the computer. This is the moment. I want you to watch carefully, because there is a right
way and a wrong way to do this.
The right way:
1. Look at the system unit (the box). Find the power button. It is usually a round button with a symbol that looks like a circle
with a line at the top. That symbol means 'power.'
2. Press the power button once. Do not hold it down. Just press it once, like pressing a doorbell.
3. Wait. The computer will make some sounds. The screen will light up. This is called 'booting up.' It takes about 30 to 60
seconds. Be patient.
4. When the screen stops changing, you will see a login screen. This is a picture with a name and a password box.
5. Click on your name. A box will appear where you type your password.
6. Type your password. The letters will appear as dots — this is normal, it is a security feature so people cannot see your
password over your shoulder.
7. Press Enter. The screen will change again, and then you will see the desktop.
The wrong way:
Do not press the power button repeatedly. One press is enough.
Do not unplug the computer to turn it off. We will learn the right way to turn it off in a few minutes.
Do not press every button on the keyboard hoping something will happen. Wait for the computer to finish starting."
Key Point: Press the power button once, wait patiently, then log in with your password.
Topic 5: The Windows Desktop (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "What you see now is called the desktop. It is called the desktop because it is like the top of a real desk. On a
real desk, you have papers, folders, a calculator, a pen. On the computer desktop, you have icons, a taskbar, and a Start button.
Let me point out the main parts:
The Desktop Background: This is the picture or colour behind everything. It is called the wallpaper. You can change it if you
want.
Icons: These are the small pictures on the desktop. Each icon represents a program, a file or a folder. For example, the Recycle
Bin is an icon. We will learn about that later.
The Taskbar: This is the long bar at the bottom of the screen. It shows which programs are currently open. It also has the Start
button on the left side.
The Start Button: This is the Windows logo, usually in the bottom-left corner. Clicking it opens the Start menu, which is like a
table of contents for everything on the computer.
The System Tray: This is the area in the bottom-right corner of the taskbar. It shows the time, the date, the volume icon, the
Wi-Fi icon, and other small indicators.
The Clock: The time and date are shown in the bottom-right corner. This is useful for knowing when you started a task and how
long it took."
Key Point: The desktop is your workspace. The taskbar shows what is open. The Start button is how you find programs.
Topic 6: Shutting Down Correctly (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "When you finish using the computer, you need to turn it off. But you must do it the right way. The wrong
way is to pull the plug or press the power button and hold it down. That is like slamming a door — it works, but it damages
things over time.
The right way to shut down:
1. Click the Start button (the Windows logo in the bottom-left corner).
2. Click the Power icon (a circle with a line at the top).
3. Click 'Shut down.'
4. Wait for the computer to turn off completely. The screen will go black. The system unit will stop making noise.
5. You can now leave.
Why does this matter? When you shut down correctly, the computer saves its settings, closes all programs properly, and
prepares for the next time you use it. If you just pull the plug, you can lose work, damage files, and cause problems that are
expensive to fix.
Think of it like this: shutting down correctly is like packing your bag carefully before leaving home. Pulling the plug is like
running out the door and leaving everything on the floor."
Key Point: Always shut down through the Start menu. Never pull the plug.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Demonstration 1: Identifying Components (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A clear view of the system unit, monitor, keyboard, mouse and speakers
A whiteboard or flip chart to draw a simple diagram
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to point to each part of the computer and say its name. Repeat after me."
1. Point to the monitor. "This is the monitor. It shows me what the computer is doing. Monitor."
2. Point to the keyboard. "This is the keyboard. I use it to type. Keyboard."
3. Point to the mouse. "This is the mouse. I use it to point and click. Mouse."
4. Point to the system unit. "This is the system unit, or CPU. It is the brain. CPU."
5. Point to the speakers. "These are speakers. They play sound. Speakers."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor pointing to each component and saying its name clearly.
Expected Result: Learners can repeat the five names after the instructor.
Questions the Instructor Can Ask:
"Who can point to the monitor?" (Select a learner.)
"What does the CPU do?" (Answer: It thinks / it is the brain.)
"If I want to type a letter, which part do I use?" (Answer: The keyboard.)
Demonstration 2: Powering On and Logging In (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A machine at the front of the room connected to a projector or large screen
The login screen displayed
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Watch carefully. I am going to turn on the computer and log in. Count the steps with
me."
1. "Step 1: I find the power button on the system unit. It is this round button here. I press it once." (Press the power button.)
2. "Step 2: I wait. The computer is starting. I can hear it. The screen is changing. I do not touch anything. I wait." (Wait for the
login screen to appear.)
3. "Step 3: I see the login screen. I click on my name." (Click the user account.)
4. "Step 4: I type my password. Watch — the letters appear as dots. This is normal." (Type the password.)
5. "Step 5: I press Enter. The computer is logging me in. I wait for the desktop to appear." (Press Enter and wait.)
6. "Step 6: I am now logged in. I can see the desktop. I am ready to work."
What Learners Should Observe: The full sequence from power button to desktop, with the instructor narrating each step.
Expected Result: Learners can describe the six steps of powering on and logging in.
Common Errors and Corrections:
If the learner presses the power button multiple times: "One press is enough. The computer heard you the first time."
If the learner types the password wrong: "Check that Caps Lock is not on. Passwords are case-sensitive, which means capital
and small letters matter."
If the learner cannot find the power button: "Look for the symbol that looks like a circle with a line at the top. It is usually on
the front of the system unit."
Demonstration 3: Navigating the Desktop (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
The desktop displayed on the projector
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Now I am going to show you the desktop. I will point to each part and tell you its name."
1. "This is the desktop background. It is the picture behind everything." (Point to the wallpaper.)
2. "These are icons. Each icon is a shortcut to a program or file." (Point to several icons.)
3. "This is the taskbar. It is the long bar at the bottom." (Point to the taskbar.)
4. "This is the Start button. It is the Windows logo in the bottom-left corner." (Point to the Start button.)
5. "This is the system tray. It shows the time, date, volume and Wi-Fi." (Point to the system tray.)
6. "This is the clock. It tells me the time." (Point to the clock.)
"Now I will open the Start menu. Watch what happens." (Click the Start button.) "This is the Start menu. It shows all the
programs on the computer. I can find anything from here." (Close the Start menu by clicking elsewhere.)
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor identifying each desktop element and opening/closing the Start menu.
Expected Result: Learners can point to the desktop, taskbar, Start button and system tray on their own machines.

The learner can explain their answers to a partner

The desktop.

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "Adaeze's First Day at Work"
Situation: Adaeze is 24 years old. She has just been hired as a receptionist at a small accounting firm in Port Harcourt. On her
first day, her boss shows her to a desk and says, "This is your computer. Please type up the client list and email it to me by 5pm."
Adaeze has never used a computer before. She is terrified. She does not want to look foolish, so she nods and sits down.
The Problem: Adaeze does not know how to turn on the computer. She does not know what a desktop is. She does not know
how to type. She does not know what email is. She sits at the desk for 20 minutes, afraid to touch anything. Her boss walks past
and sees her staring at the screen. He is not impressed.
Why It Matters: Adaeze could lose her job on the first day. Not because she is not intelligent, but because she was never taught
how to use a computer. This happens to thousands of people in Nigeria every year. Employers assume everyone knows how to
use a computer. They do not teach. They expect.
Relevant Concept: The five hardware components and the correct power-on procedure. If Adaeze had taken this course, she
would have known exactly what to do: press the power button, wait, log in, and start working.
Worked Solution: If Adaeze had taken this course, here is what she would do:
1. She would press the power button on the system unit once.
2. She would wait for the computer to start.
3. She would log in with her password.
4. She would see the desktop and know she is ready to work.
5. She would open a word processor (taught in the next course) and type the client list.
6. She would save the file (taught in Session 3).
7. She would open her email (taught in Session 5) and send the file to her boss.
Reasoning: Each step builds on the previous one. Without knowing the hardware, she cannot power on. Without powering on,

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Pressing the Power Button Repeatedly
Symptoms: The learner presses the power button five times in a row. Likely Cause: Nervousness or impatience. The learner
thinks one press did not work. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "Did you hear the computer start? Did the screen change?"
Safe Corrective Steps: Explain that one press is enough. The computer heard the first press. Pressing again does not make it
start faster. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner presses once and waits patiently. How to Prevent
Recurrence: Demonstrate the correct technique at the start of every session until it becomes habit.
Mistake 2: Typing the Password with Caps Lock On
Symptoms: The password does not work. The learner is sure they typed it correctly. Likely Cause: Caps Lock is on, so all letters
are capitalized. Passwords are case-sensitive. How to Investigate: Look at the keyboard. Is the Caps Lock light on? (On most
keyboards, there is a small light above the Caps Lock key.) Safe Corrective Steps: Press the Caps Lock key once to turn it off.
Type the password again. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner successfully logs in. How to Prevent
Recurrence: Teach learners to check the Caps Lock light before typing a password. Show them that passwords are case-
sensitive.
Mistake 3: Confusing the Monitor Power Button with the System Unit Power Button
Symptoms: The learner presses a button and nothing happens. Likely Cause: The learner is pressing the monitor power button,
not the system unit power button. Or vice versa. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "Which button did you press? Show me."
Safe Corrective Steps: Explain that the monitor and the system unit each have their own power button. Both need to be on.
How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The screen lights up and the computer starts. How to Prevent Recurrence: Label
the power buttons with small stickers during the first session. Remove the labels in Session 2 to test if learners can find them
without help.
Mistake 4: Pulling the Plug to Turn Off the Computer
Symptoms: The learner reaches behind the computer to unplug it. Likely Cause: The learner does not know the correct
shutdown procedure. How to Investigate: Ask, "What are you doing?" The learner will say, "Turning it off." Safe Corrective
Steps: Explain the correct shutdown procedure. Explain why pulling the plug is harmful. How to Confirm the Problem Is
Resolved: The learner shuts down through the Start menu. How to Prevent Recurrence: Demonstrate the correct shutdown at
the end of every session. Make it a habit.
Mistake 5: Thinking the Computer Is Broken When It Is Just Slow
Symptoms: The learner says, "It is not working. Nothing is happening." Likely Cause: The computer is still starting up. The
learner is impatient. How to Investigate: Look at the screen. Is there a loading animation? Is the hard drive light blinking? Safe
Corrective Steps: Explain that computers take time to start. Compare it to a car engine on a cold morning — it needs a
moment to warm up. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner waits patiently and the computer finishes starting.
How to Prevent Recurrence: Set a timer during the first session. Show learners that the computer starts in less than one
minute. After that, they know what "normal" looks like.

## M. KNOWLEDGE CHECK (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
Questions
1. Multiple Choice: Which part of the computer is the "brain"? a) Monitor b) Keyboard c) CPU / System Unit d) Mouse
Answer: c) CPU / System Unit
2. Short Answer: What is the difference between a desktop computer and a laptop computer?
Answer: A desktop sits on a desk and has separate parts (monitor, keyboard, mouse, system unit). A laptop is one single unit
that you can carry around.
3. Multiple Choice: What is the correct way to turn off a computer? a) Pull the plug b) Press and hold the power button c) Click
Start, then Power, then Shut down d) Close the laptop lid
Answer: c) Click Start, then Power, then Shut down
4. Explain in Your Own Words: Why is it important to shut down a computer correctly instead of pulling the plug?
Answer: Shutting down correctly saves settings, closes programs properly, and prevents file damage and data loss. Pulling the
plug can cause problems that are expensive to fix.
5. Practical Identification: Point to the monitor, keyboard, mouse, system unit, and speakers on your computer.
Answer: The learner correctly identifies all five components.
6. Scenario-Based: Your friend says, "I pressed the power button but nothing happened." What are two things you would tell
your friend to check?
Answer: (1) Check that the monitor is turned on — it has its own power button. (2) Check that the power cable is connected to
the system unit and to the wall socket.
7. Error-Detecting: A learner writes in their assignment: "To turn off the computer, pull the plug from the wall." What is wrong
with this, and what should they write instead?
Answer: Pulling the plug is the wrong way to shut down. It can damage files and cause problems. The correct way is: Click the
Start button, click the Power icon, and click "Shut down."
