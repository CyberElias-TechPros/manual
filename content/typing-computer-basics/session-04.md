# Internet Safe Browsing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
Field Detail
Session Number 4
Field Detail
Title The Internet & Safe Browsing
Week 2
Module Digital Independence
Duration 105 minutes
Difficulty Beginner
Prerequisite Knowledge Sessions 1–3 — full computer operation, file management, typing
Connection to Previous Session Builds on Sessions 1–3 by extending the learner's reach from the local machine to the
global internet
Preparation Bookmarked websites, scam scenarios, tested download links, functional Wi-Fi

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what the internet is in simple terms and how it differs from the World Wide Web.
2. Open a web browser (Chrome or Edge) and navigate to a website by typing a URL.
3. Distinguish between typing a URL and using a search engine.
4. Use a search engine effectively by typing clear, specific queries.
5. Identify safe and unsafe downloads by examining file types, sources and website credibility.
6. Recognize common online scams including phishing emails, fake websites and advance-fee fraud.
7. Identify a secure website by looking for the padlock icon and HTTPS in the address bar.
8. Create and use bookmarks to save frequently visited websites.
9. Explain basic digital safety habits that protect them and their information online.
10. Apply the "stop and think" rule before clicking links, downloading files or sharing personal information.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
The "Road Trip" Analogy
Instructor Setup: Draw a simple map on the whiteboard: a house in the centre, with roads leading to different buildings (a
bank, a market, a school, a library, a stranger's house).
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Welcome to Week 2. Today we are going to explore the internet. But before we go
online, I want you to imagine something.
Imagine you are in a new city. You have a car and a tank full of fuel. You can drive anywhere. There are roads to a bank, a
market, a school, a library — all the places you need. But there are also roads to places you should not go: a stranger's house, a
dark alley, a building with a sign that says 'Free Money Inside.'
The internet is like that city. It is a network of roads connecting millions of computers. You can drive to any website in the world.
But just like in a real city, there are safe places and dangerous places. Today, you will learn how to tell the difference.
Look at this map. [Point to the whiteboard.] This house is your computer. These roads are the internet. These buildings are
websites. Your job is to learn which roads to take and which to avoid. Let us begin."
Expected Learner Response: Recognition that they have already encountered some of these "roads" (perhaps on their phones).
Curiosity about how to tell the difference between safe and unsafe.

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: What Is the Internet? (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The internet is a giant network of connected computers. It connects millions of computers all over the
world. When you go online, your computer is connected to this network. You can send messages, share files, watch videos and
access information from any computer that is also connected.
The Internet vs The World Wide Web: People often say 'internet' when they mean 'the web.' They are not the same thing.
The internet is the network — the cables, satellites and computers that connect everything.
The World Wide Web (or just 'the web') is the collection of websites and pages that you access through the internet.
Think of it this way: the internet is the road system. The web is the buildings along the roads. You need the roads to reach the
buildings, but the roads and the buildings are not the same thing.
How Does It Work? When you type a website address into your browser, your computer sends a request through the internet
to the computer that hosts that website. That computer sends the website back to you. This happens in seconds. It is like
sending a letter, but much, much faster.
A Brief History: The internet was invented in the 1960s by the United States military. It was originally called ARPANET. In the
1990s, it became available to the public. The World Wide Web was invented in 1989 by a man named Tim Berners-Lee. He
wanted a way for scientists to share documents. He created the first web browser and the first website. The rest is history."
Topic 2: Browsers and the Address Bar (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "A browser is a program that lets you view websites on the web. It is like a vehicle that drives you along the
internet roads. The two most common browsers are Google Chrome and Microsoft Edge. Both are free and pre-installed on
most computers.
Opening a Browser:
1. Look at the taskbar at the bottom of the screen.
2. Find the browser icon. Chrome is a circle with red, yellow, green and blue. Edge is a blue and green wave.
3. Click it once. The browser opens.
The Address Bar: At the top of the browser, there is a long box. This is the address bar. This is where you type the address of the
website you want to visit. The address is called a URL (Uniform Resource Locator). A URL is like a street address — it tells the
browser exactly where to go.
For example:
google.com takes you to Google
punchng.com takes you to Punch newspaper
jumia.com.ng takes you to Jumia
Typing a URL:
1. Click in the address bar.
2. Type the URL (e.g., google.com ).
3. Press Enter.
4. The browser takes you to that website.
Tabs: A browser can have multiple tabs open at the same time. Each tab is like a separate page. You can open a new tab by
clicking the plus (+) icon next to the existing tab. You can switch between tabs by clicking on them. This is useful when you
want to keep multiple websites open at once.
The Back, Forward and Refresh Buttons:
The back button (left arrow) takes you to the previous page you visited.
The forward button (right arrow) takes you to the next page.
The refresh button (circular arrow) reloads the current page. Use this if a page is not loading correctly."
Topic 3: Search Engines (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "A search engine is a website that helps you find other websites. It is like a librarian — you tell it what you are
looking for, and it shows you a list of results.
The most popular search engine is Google. Others include Bing, Yahoo and DuckDuckGo. We will use Google in this course.
How to Search:
1. Go to google.com (or click the Google search bar if it is your homepage).
2. Type what you are looking for in the search box.
3. Press Enter.
4. Google shows you a list of results. Each result has a title, a short description and a URL.
Search Tips:
Be specific. Instead of typing 'shoes,' type 'buy black leather shoes in Port Harcourt.' The more specific your search, the
better the results.
Use quotes for exact phrases. If you are looking for an exact phrase, put it in quotes. For example, "Cyber Elias Academy
Port Harcourt" will find pages with that exact phrase.
Use simple words. Google does not understand complex sentences. Use simple keywords. For example, instead of typing
'Where can I find a good restaurant in Port Harcourt?' type 'restaurant Port Harcourt.'
Check the URL. Before clicking a result, look at the URL. Does it match what you are looking for? If you searched for 'Jumia'
and the result says jumia.com.ng , that is probably correct. If it says jumia-discounts-scam.com , be careful.
Common Mistake — Clicking the First Result: Many people click the first result without reading it. This is a mistake. The first
result is not always the best. Sometimes it is an advertisement. Always read the title and description before clicking.
Google Search Results Page: At the top of the results, you may see results labelled 'Ad.' These are advertisements. They are not
organic search results. They are paid for by companies. They may or may not be relevant to your search. Below the ads, you will
see the organic results. These are the actual search results."
Topic 4: Safe vs Unsafe Downloads (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "One of the most dangerous things you can do online is download a file from an untrusted source. A
download is when you save a file from the internet to your computer. It could be a document, an image, a program or anything
else.
Safe Downloads:
Downloads from official websites (e.g., Microsoft.com, Adobe.com)
Downloads from trusted sources (e.g., your bank's website, your school's portal)
Files with common, safe extensions (.pdf, .docx, .jpg, .png, .mp3, .mp4)
Unsafe Downloads:
Downloads from pop-up windows that say 'Your computer is infected! Click here to fix it!'
Downloads from websites you have never heard of
Files with dangerous extensions (.exe, .bat, .scr, .zip from unknown sources)
Downloads that arrive as email attachments from people you do not know
The Golden Rule: If you did not go looking for a file, do not download it. If a pop-up tells you to download something, close
the pop-up. If an email tells you to download an attachment, do not download it unless you are expecting it and you trust the
sender.
How to Download Safely:
1. Go to the official website of the company or organisation.
2. Look for a 'Downloads' or 'Resources' section.
3. Click the download link.
4. The file will be saved to your Downloads folder.
5. Open the file and verify it is what you expected.
Virus Scanners: A virus scanner is a program that checks files for malware (malicious software). Windows comes with a built-in
virus scanner called Windows Defender. It runs automatically in the background. You do not need to do anything — it is always
working. But it is not perfect. The best protection is you — do not download files from untrusted sources."
Topic 5: Recognizing Scams and Phishing (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "A scam is a trick designed to steal your money or your information. Scams have existed for thousands of
years. The internet has made them easier to spread and harder to detect.
Common Online Scams:
1. Advance-Fee Fraud (419): Someone contacts you and says you have won a lottery, inherited money from a distant relative,
or been selected for a business opportunity. But there is a catch — you need to pay a small fee first. You pay the fee. They
disappear. Your money is gone. This is called advance-fee fraud because you pay the fee in advance.
2. Phishing: Phishing is when a scammer pretends to be a trusted organisation (your bank, your employer, a government
agency) and tricks you into giving them your password, PIN or personal information. They usually do this through a fake
website or a fake email.
For example, you receive an email that looks like it is from your bank. It says: 'Your account has been compromised. Click here
to verify your identity.' The link takes you to a fake website that looks exactly like your bank's website. You enter your password.
The scammer now has your password and can access your real account.
3. Fake Online Stores: A scammer creates a website that looks like a legitimate online store. It advertises products at very low
prices. You pay, but the products never arrive. The website disappears.
4. Romance Scams: A scammer creates a fake profile on a dating site or social media. They build a relationship with you over
weeks or months. Then they ask for money — for a medical emergency, for travel expenses, for a business opportunity. You
send the money. They disappear.
How to Protect Yourself:
Stop and think. Before you click a link, download a file or share personal information, stop and think. Is this legitimate?
Does this make sense?
Check the sender. If you receive an email from your bank, check the sender's email address. Is it from the real bank or from
a fake address?
Never share your password. No legitimate organisation will ever ask for your password by email or text message.
Use strong passwords. Use a different password for every account. Use a mix of letters, numbers and symbols.
Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, it probably is.
The Most Important Rule: If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is. If someone is pressuring you to act quickly, stop and
think. Scammers rely on urgency and emotion. Do not let them rush you."

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Demonstration 1: Navigating to a Website (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
Chrome or Edge open on the projector
The address bar visible
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to navigate to a website by typing a URL. Watch carefully."
1. "I click in the address bar. I type google.com . I press Enter. Google loads."
2. "Now I will navigate to a different website. I click in the address bar. I type punchng.com . I press Enter. The Punch newspaper
website loads."
3. "Now I will open a new tab. I click the plus (+) icon next to the current tab. A new tab opens. I type jumia.com.ng . Jumia

4. "I switch back to the previous tab by clicking on it. I am back at Punch."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor typing URLs into the address bar and navigating between websites and tabs.
Expected Result: Learners can navigate to a website by typing a URL.
Questions the Instructor Can Ask:
"What is the difference between a URL and a search?" (Answer: A URL is a specific website address. A search is when you
type keywords and the search engine shows you results.)
"How do you open a new tab?" (Answer: Click the plus (+) icon next to the current tab.)
Demonstration 2: Using a Search Engine (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
Google open on the projector
A few pre-planned search queries
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to search for something on Google. Watch how I do it."
1. "I click in the Google search box. I type Cyber Elias Academy Port Harcourt . I press Enter. Google shows me results. I can see
the CEA website in the results. I click on it. The CEA website loads."
2. "Now I will search for something more specific. I type buy laptop under 100000 naira in Nigeria . I press Enter. Google shows
me results from Jumia, Konga and other stores."
3. "Now I will search for an exact phrase. I type "best restaurants in Port Harcourt" (with quotes). I press Enter. Google shows
me pages that contain that exact phrase."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor typing clear, specific queries and reading the results before clicking.
Expected Result: Learners can use a search engine effectively.
Common Errors and Corrections:
If the learner types a full sentence: "Google does not understand full sentences. Use simple keywords. Instead of 'Where can
I find a good restaurant in Port Harcourt?' type 'restaurant Port Harcourt.'"
If the learner clicks the first result without reading: "Always read the title and description before clicking. Make sure the
result is relevant to your search."
Demonstration 3: Identifying Safe vs Unsafe Websites (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
Two websites open in separate tabs: one legitimate (e.g., gtbank.com) and one fake (e.g., a projected screenshot of a
phishing page)
The padlock icon visible in the address bar
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to show you how to tell if a website is safe or unsafe. Watch the address bar
carefully."
1. "This is the real GTBank website. Look at the address bar. It says gtbank.com . Do you see the padlock icon? [Point to the
padlock.] This padlock means the website is secure. It means the connection between your computer and the website is
encrypted — no one can spy on it."
2. "Now look at this other website. [Switch to the fake tab.] It looks similar, but look at the address bar. It says gtbank-verify-
login.com . That is not the real GTBank website. That is a fake website designed to steal your password. There is no padlock."
3. "Here is what to check:
The URL: Does it match the real website? Look for misspellings or extra words.
The padlock: Is there a padlock icon in the address bar? If not, the site is not secure.
The design: Does the website look professional? Are there spelling mistakes? These are signs of a scam."
What Learners Should Observe: The difference between a legitimate website (correct URL, padlock, professional design) and a
fake website (wrong URL, no padlock, poor design).
Expected Result: Learners can identify safe and unsafe websites.
Questions the Instructor Can Ask:
"What does the padlock icon mean?" (Answer: The website is secure. The connection is encrypted.)
"How can you tell if a website is fake?" (Answer: Check the URL for misspellings, look for the padlock, check the design for
mistakes.)

"How many results did Google find?" (Answer: Google shows the number of results at the top of the page.)
Part 3: Identify Safe and Unsafe Websites (5 minutes)
10. "Go to Google. Type GTBank online banking and press Enter. Find the real GTBank website. Click on it. Look at the address
bar. Do you see the padlock?"
11. "Now I am going to show you a fake website. [The instructor projects a screenshot of a phishing page.] Look at the address
bar. What is wrong?" (Answer: The URL is wrong. It is not gtbank.com.)
12. "Here is your challenge. I will describe a website. You tell me if it is safe or unsafe:
A website with the URL paypal.com and a padlock. (Safe.)
A website with the URL paypa1.com (with a number 1 instead of the letter l) and no padlock. (Unsafe.)
A website with the URL amazon.com and a padlock. (Safe.)
A website with the URL arnazon.com (with an r instead of an m) and no padlock. (Unsafe.)"
Instructor Prompts:
"What are three things you should check before entering your password on a website?" (Answer: The URL, the padlock, and
the design.)
Part 4: Create a Bookmark (5 minutes)
13. "Go to Google. Search for Cyber Elias Academy . Click on the CEA website."
14. "Now bookmark this page. Click the star icon in the address bar. A dialog box appears. Click 'Done.' The page is now
bookmarked."
15. "To see your bookmarks, click the three dots in the top-right corner of the browser. Click 'Bookmarks.' You will see the CEA
website in the list."
Instructor Prompts:
"Why is it useful to bookmark a website?" (Answer: So you can find it again quickly without searching for it.)
Expected Result: Every learner has navigated to multiple websites, used a search engine, identified safe and unsafe websites,
and created a bookmark.
Troubleshooting Guidance:
If the learner cannot connect to the internet: "Check the Wi-Fi icon in the system tray (bottom-right corner). Is it
connected? If not, ask the instructor for help."
If a website does not load: "Try clicking the refresh button. If it still does not load, try a different website."
If the learner cannot find the bookmark star: "It is in the address bar, on the right side. It looks like a star outline."
Completion Checklist:
Navigated to at least three websites by typing URLs
Opened and closed multiple tabs
Used Google to search for specific information
Identified safe and unsafe websites
Created a bookmark for the CEA website

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Exercise: "Internet Safety Detective"
Scenario: "You are helping your uncle, who runs a small business. He receives many emails every day. He is not sure which ones
are safe and which ones are scams. He asks you to help him identify the safe and unsafe emails. Below are five email
descriptions. For each one, decide if it is safe or unsafe, and explain why."
Task: Read each email description and write down:

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "Okonkwo's Costly Mistake"
Situation: Okonkwo is a trader in Port Harcourt. He has a smartphone and uses WhatsApp and Facebook, but he has never used
a computer or email. One day, he receives an email on his phone. The email says it is from First Bank. It says his account has
been restricted and he needs to click a link to restore access. Okonkwo clicks the link. The website looks exactly like First Bank.
He enters his account number and PIN. Within minutes, ₦150,000 is withdrawn from his account.
The Problem: Okonkwo did not check the sender's email address. He did not check the URL. He did not check for the padlock.
He acted out of fear — the email said his account was restricted, and he panicked. He gave his personal information to a
scammer.
Why It Matters: Okonkwo lost ₦150,000. That is a huge amount of money. It could take him months to recover from this loss.
This is not a hypothetical story — it happens to thousands of Nigerians every year. The scammers are getting better. Their
emails and websites look more realistic. But the warning signs are still there. You just need to know what to look for.
Relevant Concept: Phishing and safe browsing. If Okonkwo had taken this course, he would have known:
1. Banks never ask for your password by email.
2. Always check the sender's email address.
3. Always check the URL for misspellings.
4. Look for the padlock icon.
5. Stop and think before clicking a link.
Worked Solution: If Okonkwo had taken this course, here is what he would have done:
1. He would have looked at the sender's email address. It would not be from First Bank.
2. He would have checked the URL. It would not be firstbanknigeria.com.
3. He would have seen there was no padlock.
4. He would have realised it was a phishing attempt and deleted the email.
5. He would have contacted his bank directly (by phone or in person) to verify the email.
6. He would have kept his ₦150,000.
Reasoning: Scammers rely on emotion — fear, excitement, urgency. They want you to act without thinking. The antidote is to
stop and think. Check the facts. Verify the source. If in doubt, do not click.
Possible Mistake: Okonkwo might have realised it was a scam but done nothing. He might have thought, "I will just delete the
email and forget about it." But scammers count on people doing nothing. If you receive a phishing email, report it to your bank.
Warn your friends and family. Do not just delete it and move on.
Correction: Report phishing emails to your bank and to the relevant authorities. Warn others. The more people know about
these scams, the fewer people will fall for them.
Transfer Exercise: "Your friend Adaeze receives an email that says she has won a free iPhone. All she needs to do is click a link
and enter her phone number and address. Should she do it? Why or why not?"
Professional Connection: Every professional who uses email is a target for phishing. Office workers, business owners,
government employees — everyone. Knowing how to identify a phishing email is not just a personal safety skill — it is a
professional safety skill.

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Typing URLs into the Search Box
Symptoms: The learner types a URL (e.g., google.com ) into the Google search box instead of the address bar. Likely Cause: The
learner does not understand the difference between the address bar and the search box. How to Investigate: Ask the learner,
"Where did you type the URL?" If they point to the search box, they are confused. Safe Corrective Steps: Show the learner the
address bar (at the very top of the browser) and the search box (in the middle of the Google homepage). Explain that the address
bar is for URLs and the search box is for keywords. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner types URLs into the
address bar. How to Prevent Recurrence: At the start of every browsing exercise, remind the learner: "Address bar for URLs.
Search box for keywords."
Mistake 2: Clicking Pop-Up Ads
Symptoms: The learner clicks on pop-up windows that say "You have won a prize!" or "Your computer is infected!" Likely
Cause: The learner does not know that pop-ups are often scams. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "What did you click?" If
they say "a pop-up," they need to learn about pop-up safety. Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to close pop-ups by
clicking the X in the top-right corner of the pop-up. Never click "OK," "Download," or "Claim Now" on a pop-up. How to
Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner closes pop-ups without clicking on them. How to Prevent Recurrence:
Remind the learner at the start of every browsing exercise: "Never click on pop-ups. Close them immediately."
Mistake 3: Downloading Files from Untrusted Sources
Symptoms: The learner downloads a file from a pop-up or an untrusted website. Likely Cause: The learner does not understand
the risks of downloading files. How to Investigate: Check the learner's Downloads folder. Are there files from untrusted
sources? Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner to only download files from official, trusted websites. Show them how to
check the file type before opening it. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner only downloads files from trusted
sources. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner at the start of every session: "If you did not go looking for a file, do
not download it."
Mistake 4: Sharing Personal Information Online
Symptoms: The learner shares their password, PIN, address or phone number on a website or in an email. Likely Cause: The
learner does not understand what information is safe and unsafe to share. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "What
information did you share?" If they shared a password or PIN, they need to learn about information safety. Safe Corrective
Steps: Teach the learner the golden rule: never share your password or PIN with anyone, online or offline. Only share personal
information on secure, trusted websites. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner protects their personal
information. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner at the start of every session: "Your password is your secret. Never
share it with anyone."
Mistake 5: Ignoring Browser Security Warnings
Symptoms: The learner clicks "Continue" or "Proceed" when the browser shows a security warning. Likely Cause: The learner
does not understand what browser security warnings mean. How to Investigate: Ask the learner, "Did you see a warning? What
did it say?" Safe Corrective Steps: Teach the learner that browser security warnings are there to protect them. If the browser
says "This site is not secure," do not proceed. Close the tab. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner heeds
browser security warnings. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner: "If your browser shows a security warning, stop.
Do not proceed."
