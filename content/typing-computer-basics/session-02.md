# Keyboard Mouse Typing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
Field Detail
Session Number 2
Field Detail
Title Keyboard, Mouse & Typing Foundations
Week 1
Module The Hands
Duration 105 minutes
Difficulty Absolute Beginner
Prerequisite Knowledge Session 1 — can power on, log in, identify components
Connection to Previous Session Builds on Session 1 by teaching the learner to use the input devices they identified
Preparation Notepad open, typing website bookmarked, handouts printed

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Use the mouse confidently to perform left-click, right-click, double-click, drag-and-drop, and scroll.
2. Identify the main key regions on a QWERTY keyboard (letter keys, number keys, function keys, navigation keys, modifier
keys).
3. Place their fingers correctly on the home row (left hand: A S D F; right hand: J K L ;).
4. Type using the correct finger for each key on the home row.
5. Use four essential keyboard shortcuts: Ctrl+C (copy), Ctrl+V (paste), Ctrl+Z (undo), Ctrl+S (save).
6. Measure their typing speed in words per minute (WPM) and accuracy percentage.
7. Explain why correct finger placement matters for speed and accuracy.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
The "Type Your Name" Challenge
Instructor Setup: Open Notepad on all machines. Make sure the cursor is blinking in the Notepad window.
Instructor Script: "Good morning, everyone. Today we are going to learn how to use the keyboard and mouse properly. But
first, I want to see how you currently type. Open Notepad — it should already be open on your screen. If it is not, click the Start
button, type 'Notepad,' and press Enter.
Now, type your full name. First name and last name. Use whatever method you normally use — one finger, two fingers,
whatever feels natural. Go ahead."
What Happens: Learners type their names. Some use one finger. Some use two fingers. Some hunt and peck. Some are very
slow.
Instructor Script (after 2 minutes): "Stop. Look at your hands. How many fingers did you use? [Pause.] Most people use one
or two fingers. That is called 'hunt and peck' typing — you hunt for each key and peck it with one finger. It works, but it is slow.
By the end of today, you will know how to use all ten fingers. You will not be fast yet — that takes practice — but you will be
using the right technique. And the right technique is the foundation of speed.
Let us begin."
Expected Learner Response: Amusement at their own slow typing, curiosity about the "right way," some nervousness about
learning a new method.

## F. FULL LECTURE NOTES (35 minutes)
<!-- kind:lecture -->
Topic 1: The Mouse — Your Pointing Tool (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The mouse is how you talk to the computer without typing. You move the mouse on the desk, and the
arrow on the screen moves too. Let us learn the five main things you can do with a mouse.
1. Left-Click This is the most common mouse action. You press the left button once. You use left-click to select things, to open
things, to click buttons. Think of it like pointing at something and saying 'this one.'
2. Right-Click You press the right button once. Right-click opens a menu of options. It is like asking 'what can I do with this?'
For example, if you right-click on the desktop, a menu appears with options like 'View,' 'Sort by,' 'Refresh.' We will use right-
click in the next session.
3. Double-Click You press the left button twice quickly. Double-click opens things. For example, double-clicking an icon on the
desktop opens that program. It is like saying 'open this now.' The key word is quickly — two clicks in fast succession. If you
wait too long between clicks, the computer thinks you did two single clicks.
4. Drag and Drop You press and hold the left button, move the mouse to a new position, then release the button. This is how
you move things from one place to another. For example, you can drag a file from one folder to another. It is like picking up a
piece of paper and putting it in a different folder.
5. Scroll Most mice have a wheel between the two buttons. You roll the wheel up and down to move the page up and down.
This is called scrolling. It is like turning the pages of a book, but with your finger.
Mouse Grip: Hold the mouse gently. Do not squeeze it. Your hand should rest on top of the mouse, with your index finger on
the left button and your middle finger on the right button. Your thumb and ring finger rest on the sides. The mouse should
move with your whole hand, not just your wrist.
Common Mistake — The Death Grip: Some learners hold the mouse so tightly their knuckles turn white. This causes hand
pain and slow movement. Tell learners: 'Hold the mouse like a small bird — firmly enough that it does not fly away, gently
enough that you do not hurt it.'"
Topic 2: The Keyboard — Your Typing Tool (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "The keyboard is how you type letters, numbers and commands into the computer. Let us learn the main
parts of the keyboard.
The QWERTY Layout: The keyboard is called QWERTY because the first six letters on the top row are Q, W, E, R, T, Y. This layout
was designed in the 1870s for typewriters. It has not changed much since then. Every keyboard you will ever use — on a
desktop, a laptop, a phone — is based on this layout.
Key Regions:
1. Letter Keys: These are the main keys in the middle. They type the letters A to Z.
2. Number Keys: These are the keys along the top. They type the numbers 0 to 9.
3. Function Keys: These are the keys along the very top, labelled F1 to F12. They do special things depending on the program
you are using. We will not use them much in this course.
4. Navigation Keys: These are the arrow keys (up, down, left, right) and the Home, End, Page Up, Page Down keys. They move
the cursor around the screen.
5. Modifier Keys: These are the Shift, Ctrl (Control), Alt, and Windows keys. They do nothing on their own, but when you hold
them down and press another key, they change what that key does. For example, holding Shift and pressing 'a' types a
capital 'A.' Holding Ctrl and pressing 'C' copies something.
6. Enter Key: This is the big key on the right. It confirms what you typed or moves to a new line.
7. Backspace Key: This key deletes the character to the left of the cursor. It is like an eraser.
8. Delete Key: This key deletes the character to the right of the cursor.
9. Space Bar: This is the long bar at the bottom. It types a space.
10. Caps Lock Key: This key turns capital letters on or off. When it is on, every letter you type is capital. When it is off, letters are
small. There is usually a light on the keyboard that shows when Caps Lock is on.
The Home Row: The most important keys on the keyboard are the home row keys. These are the keys in the middle row: A, S,
D, F, G, H, J, K, L, and the semicolon (;). Your fingers rest on these keys when you are not typing other keys. They are called the
home row because they are home base — your fingers always return to them.
Here is the correct finger placement:
Left hand: pinky on A, ring finger on S, middle finger on D, index finger on F
Right hand: index finger on J, middle finger on K, ring finger on L, pinky on ;
Thumbs rest on the space bar
Why This Matters: When your fingers are on the home row, every key is within reach. You do not have to look at the keyboard.
You can type fast and accurately because your fingers know where to go. This is the foundation of all typing."
Topic 3: Typing Technique — The Right Way (10 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Now let us learn the correct typing technique. This is the most important skill you will learn in this course. It
will take practice, but if you master it, you will type faster than most people who have been using computers for years.
Step 1: Sit Up Straight Sit with your back straight, feet flat on the floor. Your screen should be at eye level. Your keyboard
should be at a comfortable height — not too high, not too low. Good posture prevents back pain and helps you type longer.
Step 2: Place Your Fingers on the Home Row Left hand: pinky on A, ring finger on S, middle finger on D, index finger on F.
Right hand: index finger on J, middle finger on K, ring finger on L, pinky on ;. Thumbs on the space bar.
Step 3: Type Each Key with the Correct Finger Each finger is responsible for specific keys. For example:
Left pinky: A, Q, Z, 1, Shift (left side)
Left ring finger: S, W, X, 2
Left middle finger: D, E, C, 3
Left index finger: F, R, V, G, T, B, 4, 5
Right index finger: J, U, M, H, Y, N, 6, 7
Right middle finger: K, I, comma, 8
Right ring finger: L, O, period, 9
Right pinky: ;, P, /, 0, -, =, [, ], ', Enter (right side), Shift (right side)
Thumbs: space bar
Step 4: Return to Home Row After Each Key After you press a key, your finger returns to its home row position. This is like a
spring — you press and release, press and release. Your fingers are always ready for the next key.
Step 5: Do Not Look at the Keyboard This is the hardest part. You will want to look down. Do not. Trust your fingers. If you
make a mistake, use Backspace to correct it and keep going. Speed comes with practice, not with looking.
Why This Works: When you use the correct finger for each key, your hands do not have to move far. Each finger only reaches
for keys that are close to its home position. This is much faster than using one finger to reach across the whole keyboard.
Common Mistake — The One-Finger Typist: Many people type with one or two fingers. They are slow because their fingers
have to travel long distances. They also make more errors because they are not using the muscle memory that comes from
correct finger placement. The fix: go slow. Type slowly with the correct fingers. Speed will come later."
Topic 4: Essential Keyboard Shortcuts (5 minutes)
Instructor Script: "Keyboard shortcuts are combinations of keys that perform common actions. They save you time because
you do not have to move your hand to the mouse and click. Let us learn the four most important shortcuts.
Ctrl+C — Copy Hold the Ctrl key and press C. This copies whatever you have selected (text, a file, an image) to the clipboard.
The clipboard is a temporary storage area in the computer's memory. The copied item stays on the clipboard until you copy
something else.
Ctrl+V — Paste Hold the Ctrl key and press V. This pastes whatever is on the clipboard to the current location. It is like taking a
photocopy of something and putting it somewhere else.
Ctrl+Z — Undo Hold the Ctrl key and press Z. This undoes your last action. If you made a mistake — deleted something you
did not mean to delete, typed something wrong — Ctrl+Z brings it back. It is like an eraser for your actions.
Ctrl+S — Save Hold the Ctrl key and press S. This saves your current work. You should save often — every few minutes — so
you do not lose work if the computer crashes or the power goes out.
Why These Matter: These four shortcuts are used in every program — Word, Excel, your browser, everything. They save you
hours of time over the course of a career. Learn them now and use them always."
Key Point: Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V, Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+S. Copy, Paste, Undo, Save. These are the four most important keyboard shortcuts in the
world.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Demonstration 1: Mouse Control (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A clear view of the mouse and the screen
The desktop displayed on the projector
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Watch my hand on the mouse. I am going to demonstrate the five mouse actions."
1. Left-Click: "I press the left button once. See how the icon is now selected? It changed colour. That means I selected it."
(Left-click on an icon.)
2. Right-Click: "I press the right button once. A menu appeared. This is called a context menu. It shows me what I can do with
this icon." (Right-click on the desktop.)
3. Double-Click: "I press the left button twice quickly. The program opened. That is double-click." (Double-click on an icon to
open it, then close it.)
4. Drag and Drop: "I press and hold the left button, move the mouse, and release. The icon moved to a new position. That is
drag and drop." (Drag an icon to a new position on the desktop.)
5. Scroll: "I roll the wheel up and down. The page moved. That is scrolling." (Open a web page and scroll up and down.)
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor's hand position on the mouse and the result of each action on the screen.
Expected Result: Learners can describe the five mouse actions and what each one does.
Questions the Instructor Can Ask:
"What is the difference between left-click and right-click?" (Answer: Left-click selects or opens. Right-click opens a menu.)
"How many clicks in a double-click?" (Answer: Two, quickly.)
"What is the wheel on the mouse for?" (Answer: Scrolling.)
Demonstration 2: Home Row Finger Placement (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
A large keyboard diagram (poster or projected image)
The learner's own keyboard
What the Instructor Says and Does: "Watch my hands. I am going to show you the correct finger placement on the home
row."
1. "Left hand: pinky on A, ring finger on S, middle finger on D, index finger on F." (Place fingers on the home row.)
2. "Right hand: index finger on J, middle finger on K, ring finger on L, pinky on semicolon." (Place fingers.)
3. "Thumbs rest on the space bar." (Rest thumbs.)
4. "Now I type the home row letters. Watch which finger moves for each letter." (Type: a s d f g h j k l ;)
5. "See? Each finger only moves a little. It reaches for the key and returns home. That is the technique."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor's fingers on the home row and the small movements for each key.
Expected Result: Learners can place their fingers on the home row correctly.
Common Errors and Corrections:
If the learner's fingers are on the wrong keys: "Move your left pinky to A. Now your ring finger to S. Good. Now your middle
finger to D. Good. Now your index finger to F. Perfect. Now the right hand."
If the learner is looking at the keyboard: "Look at the screen, not your hands. Trust your fingers."
If the learner's hands are tense: "Relax. Hold the keyboard like you hold a phone — gently."
Demonstration 3: Typing a Sentence (5 minutes)
What the Instructor Prepares:
Notepad open on the projector
Fingers on the home row
What the Instructor Says and Does: "I am going to type a sentence using the correct finger placement. Watch my fingers, not
the screen."
1. "My fingers are on the home row. I am going to type: 'The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.'"
2. "I type slowly. I do not look at the keyboard. I look at the screen. If I make a mistake, I use Backspace and correct it."
3. (Type the sentence slowly, narrating which finger is moving for each key.)
4. "Done. That took me about 30 seconds. With practice, I can type it in 10 seconds. You will get there too."
What Learners Should Observe: The instructor typing slowly with correct finger placement, looking at the screen, not the
keyboard.
Expected Result: Learners understand that typing is a skill that improves with practice.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (20 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Exercise: "Hands On"
Practical Objective: The learner will practise all five mouse actions, place their fingers on the home row correctly, type the
home row letters, and use the four essential keyboard shortcuts.
Starting Materials:
Notepad open on each machine
Fingers on the home row
Instructions:
Part 1: Mouse Practice (5 minutes)
1. "Move your mouse. Watch the arrow on the screen. Move it to the top-left corner. Now to the bottom-right. Now to the
centre. Good."
2. "Left-click on an icon. It is selected. Left-click somewhere else to deselect it."
3. "Right-click on the desktop. A menu appeared. Press Escape to close it."
4. "Double-click on the Recycle Bin. It opened. Close it by clicking the X in the top-right corner."
5. "Drag an icon from one side of the desktop to the other. Release it. Good."
6. "Open a web page. Scroll down. Scroll up. Good."
Instructor Prompts:
"What happens when you right-click?" (Answer: A menu appears.)
"How do you close a window?" (Answer: Click the X in the top-right corner.)
Part 2: Home Row Practice (10 minutes)
7. "Place your left pinky on A. Left ring finger on S. Left middle finger on D. Left index finger on F. Right index finger on J. Right
middle finger on K. Right ring finger on L. Right pinky on semicolon. Thumbs on the space bar."
8. "Now type: a s d f — using your left hand only. Look at the screen, not your fingers."
9. "Now type: j k l ; — using your right hand only."
10. "Now type: a s d f j k l ; — using both hands."
11. "Now type: f j f j f j f j — alternating left and right index fingers."
12. "Now type: a s d f g h j k l ; — the full home row."
13. "Now type: the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog — slowly, using the correct fingers."
Instructor Prompts:
"Which finger types the letter D?" (Answer: Left middle finger.)
"Which finger types the letter K?" (Answer: Right middle finger.)
"What do your thumbs type?" (Answer: The space bar.)
Part 3: Keyboard Shortcuts (5 minutes)
14. "Type your name in Notepad. Now select it by clicking and dragging over it with the mouse. The text should be
highlighted."
15. "Hold Ctrl and press C. You copied it. You cannot see anything happen, but it is copied."
16. "Click at the end of the line. Press Enter to go to a new line. Hold Ctrl and press V. Your name appeared again. That is paste."
17. "Hold Ctrl and press Z. The pasted text disappeared. That is undo."
18. "Hold Ctrl and press S. A dialog box appeared asking where to save. Click 'Cancel' for now — we will learn about saving in
the next session."
Instructor Prompts:
"What does Ctrl+Z do?" (Answer: It undoes the last action.)
"Why should you save often?" (Answer: So you do not lose work if something goes wrong.)
Expected Result: Every learner has practised all five mouse actions, typed the home row letters, and used the four keyboard
shortcuts.
Troubleshooting Guidance:
If the learner cannot select text by clicking and dragging: "Press and hold the left mouse button, move the mouse across the
text, then release. The text should be highlighted."
If the learner's fingers keep slipping off the home row: "Slow down. Accuracy is more important than speed right now."
If the learner cannot type the semicolon: "It is the key next to L. Use your right pinky."
Completion Checklist:
Performed all five mouse actions correctly
Placed fingers on the home row correctly
Typed the home row letters (a s d f j k l ;)
Typed "the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog"
Used Ctrl+C, Ctrl+V, Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+S

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (10 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Exercise: "Typing Speed Test"
Scenario: "You are applying for a data entry job. The employer asks you to take a typing test. The test measures how many
words you can type in one minute and how accurate you are. Let us take a practice test now."
Task: Go to the typing practice website (bookmarked in your browser). Take a one-minute typing test. Record your results:
Words per minute (WPM): __________
Accuracy percentage: __________
Requirements:
Use the correct finger placement
Do not look at the keyboard
Type as fast and as accurately as you can in one minute
Record your results on a piece of paper
Time Allowance: 5 minutes (1 minute for the test, 4 minutes to record results and reflect)
Success Criteria:
The learner completes the test
The learner records their WPM and accuracy
The learner can explain what WPM means
Hints Available If the Learner Gets Stuck:
Hint 1: "WPM means words per minute. If you typed 15 words in one minute, your WPM is 15."
Hint 2: "Accuracy means how many words you typed correctly. If you typed 20 words but 2 were wrong, your accuracy is
90%."
Hint 3: "Do not worry about being slow. This is your baseline. You will improve with practice."
Complete Answer Key for the Instructor:
There is no single correct answer for a typing speed test. The learner's result is their personal baseline. However, the instructor
should expect:
Most absolute beginners: 5–15 WPM
Learners with some experience: 15–25 WPM
The instructor should record each learner's baseline and compare it to their result in Session 6.
What the Instructor Should Do:
Record each learner's WPM and accuracy in a spreadsheet or notebook.
Do not announce results publicly. Typing speed is personal. Some learners will be embarrassed if their speed is announced.
Praise effort, not speed. "You typed 12 words in one minute using the correct technique. That is a solid foundation. With
daily practice, you will double that in a month."

## J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Case: "Emmanuel's Data Entry Job"
Situation: Emmanuel is 22 years old. He has just been hired as a data entry clerk at a logistics company in Port Harcourt. His job
is to type waybill information into a spreadsheet. He is expected to type at least 30 words per minute with 95% accuracy.
Emmanuel has never taken a typing course. He uses the hunt-and-peck method with two fingers. He types at 12 WPM with 80%
accuracy.
The Problem: Emmanuel cannot meet the job requirement. He is slow and makes many errors. His supervisor is not happy.
Emmanuel is stressed. He considers quitting.
Why It Matters: Emmanuel's job depends on his typing speed and accuracy. If he cannot improve, he will lose the job. This is
not hypothetical — data entry jobs across Nigeria have minimum typing speed requirements. Employers do not have time to
train typists. They expect you to already have the skill.
Relevant Concept: Correct finger placement and home row technique. If Emmanuel had learned the correct technique from
the beginning, he would be typing at 30+ WPM within a few months of practice.
Worked Solution: If Emmanuel had taken this course, here is what he would do:
1. He would place his fingers on the home row correctly.
2. He would practise typing for 15 minutes every day.
3. He would use a typing practice website to track his progress.
4. Within one month, he would reach 25 WPM.
5. Within two months, he would reach 35 WPM.
6. He would meet the job requirement and keep his job.
Reasoning: Typing is a physical skill, like learning to ride a bicycle. You cannot learn it by reading about it. You learn it by doing
it — every day, with the correct technique. The correct technique is the foundation. Without it, you will always be slow.
Possible Mistake: Emmanuel might try to type fast before he has mastered the correct technique. This leads to bad habits that
are hard to break.
Correction: Go slow. Master the correct technique first. Speed comes naturally with practice. As the saying goes: "Slow is
smooth, and smooth is fast."
Transfer Exercise: "Your friend Chinasa types at 20 WPM using two fingers. She wants to reach 40 WPM. What advice would
you give her?"
Professional Connection: Every office job that involves a computer requires typing. The faster and more accurately you can
type, the more productive you are. This is true for receptionists, administrators, data entry clerks, writers, programmers, and
every other computer user.

## K. COMMON MISTAKES AND TROUBLESHOOTING (5 minutes)
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Mistake 1: Looking at the Keyboard
Symptoms: The learner keeps looking down at the keyboard while typing. Likely Cause: Lack of confidence. The learner does
not trust their fingers. How to Investigate: Watch the learner's eyes. Are they moving between the screen and the keyboard?
Safe Corrective Steps: Place a piece of cardboard over the learner's hands so they cannot see the keyboard. Force them to look
at the screen. How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner types without looking down. How to Prevent
Recurrence: Remind the learner at the start of every typing exercise: "Eyes on the screen."
Mistake 2: Using the Wrong Finger
Symptoms: The learner types a key with the wrong finger (e.g., using the left index finger to type S). Likely Cause: The learner
has not memorised which finger types which key. How to Investigate: Watch the learner's hands. Which finger is moving for
each key? Safe Corrective Steps: Stop the learner. Place their fingers on the home row. Type the key together. Say: "This is the
letter S. Which finger types S? The left ring finger. Good. Now type S with your left ring finger." How to Confirm the Problem Is
Resolved: The learner uses the correct finger for each key. How to Prevent Recurrence: Drill the home row letters at the start
of every session until the correct finger placement becomes automatic.
Mistake 3: Pressing Too Hard
Symptoms: The learner presses the keys so hard that their fingers make loud clicking sounds. Likely Cause: Nervousness or
habit from typing on a phone. How to Investigate: Listen to the typing. Is it very loud? Safe Corrective Steps: Tell the learner:
"The keys are sensitive. You only need to press them gently. Try typing as quietly as possible." How to Confirm the Problem Is
Resolved: The learner types quietly. How to Prevent Recurrence: Remind the learner to relax their hands. Tension causes
fatigue and slows typing.
Mistake 4: Not Returning to Home Row
Symptoms: The learner's fingers drift away from the home row and do not return. Likely Cause: The learner has not developed
the habit of returning to home base. How to Investigate: Watch the learner's hands after they type a key. Do the fingers return
to the home row? Safe Corrective Steps: Stop the learner. Place their fingers on the home row. Type one key together. Say:
"After typing this key, where does your finger go? Back to the home row. Good. Now type another key and return." How to
Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner's fingers return to the home row after each key. How to Prevent Recurrence:
Drill the home row letters slowly, emphasising the return to home after each key.
Mistake 5: Confusing Left and Right
Symptoms: The learner uses the left hand for right-hand keys and vice versa. Likely Cause: The learner has not memorised
which hand types which keys. How to Investigate: Watch the learner's hands. Are they crossing over? Safe Corrective Steps:
Drill left-hand-only and right-hand-only exercises separately. "Type a s d f with your left hand only. Good. Now type j k l ; with
your right hand only. Good. Now both hands together." How to Confirm the Problem Is Resolved: The learner uses the correct
hand for each key. How to Prevent Recurrence: Continue separate-hand drills until the learner can type the home row without
crossing hands.

## M. KNOWLEDGE CHECK (5 minutes)

Questions
1. Multiple Choice: Which finger types the letter F? a) Left pinky b) Left ring finger c) Left middle finger d) Left index finger
Answer: d) Left index finger
2. Short Answer: What are the five main things you can do with a mouse?
Answer: Left-click, right-click, double-click, drag-and-drop, scroll.
3. Multiple Choice: What does Ctrl+Z do? a) Copy b) Paste c) Undo d) Save
Answer: c) Undo
4. Explain in Your Own Words: Why is it important to use the correct finger for each key when typing?
Answer: Using the correct finger for each key means your hands do not have to move far. Each finger only reaches for keys
close to its home position. This makes typing faster and more accurate.
5. Practical Identification: Place your fingers on the home row. Type: a s d f j k l ;
Answer: The learner types the home row letters using the correct fingers.
6. Scenario-Based: Your friend says, "I type with two fingers and I am slow. What should I do?" What advice would you give?
Answer: Learn the correct finger placement on the home row. Practise typing with all ten fingers. Go slow at first — accuracy is
more important than speed. With daily practice, speed will improve.
7. Error-Detecting: A learner writes in their assignment: "I will practise typing for a while every day." What is wrong with this,
and what should they write instead?
Answer: "A while" is vague. The learner should specify exactly how many minutes per day they will practise (e.g., "I will practise
typing for 15 minutes every day at 7pm").
