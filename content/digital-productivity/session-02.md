# Email Management with Gmail

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module: Cloud Storage and Email

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (builds on the organisation mindset of Session 1)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 (Google Drive and File Organisation). Learners must be able to log into a Google account, name and organise files, and share with Viewer/Commenter/Editor. They must also be able to send a basic email.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 we organised files in Drive. Email is the other great source of digital clutter — most people's inboxes are a dumping ground. This session applies the same "structure, naming, least privilege" thinking to email, so a client's order never gets buried under 2000 unread messages.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged in; Chrome open; every learner logged into Gmail; the instructor has prepared a practice inbox — either a shared demo account filled with about 12 fake emails (see Section C), or a set of sample email screenshots; the email-template examples and label-structure handout printed; projector connected; internet working.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words why an organised inbox matters, and describe the cost of an inbox left in chaos.
2. Navigate the Gmail interface — Inbox, Starred, Snoozed, Sent, Drafts, Spam, Trash, the Compose button, the category
tabs (Primary, Social, Promotions, Updates, Forums) and the search bar.
3. Create labels, including nested labels (e.g. Clients/Invoices ), and apply them to messages.
4. Create a filter that automatically labels, archives, stars or marks incoming mail, and explain what each filter action does.
5. Apply the inbox-zero strategy — process each email using archive, delete, reply, label or snooze — and clear a practice
inbox to near zero.
6. Search Gmail effectively using operators such as from: , subject: , has:attachment and is:unread .
7. Distinguish between archiving, deleting and trashing an email, and explain when each is appropriate.
8. Create a professional signature and save an email template (Templates feature).
9. Schedule an email to send later, and snooze an email to return at a chosen time.
10. Explain the difference between Gmail labels and folders, and why labels are more flexible.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a screenshot (or the live demo account) of an inbox with a large number of unread
messages — something like 2,347 unread. You can make this yourself by sending many test emails, or use a realistic mock
screenshot. Do not show a neat inbox; show the chaos.
What the instructor says:
"Look at this inbox. Two thousand, three hundred and forty-seven unread emails. Tell me — if a customer sent you an
order this morning, could you find it in here? [Pause.] No. It is buried.
Let me tell you what happens to real people. Mr. Eze runs a small business. He uses one Gmail for everything — bank
alerts, church announcements, Jumia adverts, school notices, supplier price lists, and real customer orders. All in one pile.
One day, a big customer emails to place a large order. But Mr. Eze is busy; the email sits, unread, under a hundred
promotions. Three days later the customer gets tired, and takes the business to someone else. Mr. Eze never even knew he
had lost the order.
The problem was not that Mr. Eze was lazy. The problem is that his email had no system. Today, you will build a system so
that a client order is never lost again. You will learn labels, filters, search, templates, signatures — the same idea as
organising your Drive, but for your inbox."
Expected learner response: Learners may laugh nervously, admit their own inbox is the same, or describe a time they missed
something important. Accept all answers. Connect their stories back to the theme: it is not about being tidy for tidiness's sake
— it is about not losing money or opportunities.
Quick hook — demonstrate search: On the projector, in the demo account, click the search bar and type is:unread
subject:order and press Enter. Show how the invisible order pops out of the noise instantly. Say:
"In 5 seconds, we found what a scrolling person would miss in 20 minutes. By the end of today, you will know how to do
that and much more."
Why this opener works: It starts from a pain every learner recognises (an overflowing inbox) and attaches a real consequence (a
lost customer) that matters in the Nigerian small-business context. It ends with a live "wow" moment — search cutting
through the noise — that motivates the rest of the session.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "From Session 1 — what is the difference between local storage and cloud storage?"
Expected answer: Local files live on one computer; cloud files live on Google's servers and can be reached from any device
with internet.
Common gap: learners may describe cloud as "saving online" without the safety idea. Re-state that cloud means the file
survives even if the computer dies.
Question 2: "What does least privilege mean when sharing, and which permission should you use to let someone read but not
change a file?"
Expected answer: Give the lowest access that still allows the job; use Viewer to read only.
Common gap: learners default to Editor. Remind them: Viewer to read, Commenter to review, Editor to co-edit.
Question 3: "Why do we name files with YYYY-MM-DD at the front?"
Expected answer: So they sort in true chronological order; the year first keeps files from different years in the right order.
Common gap: thinking the date must be today's date; remind them it is the date the file is about.
Question 4: "How do you find a specific file in Drive quickly?"
Expected answer: Use the search bar with the file name or an operator like type:pdf , owner:me or is:starred .
Common gap: scrolling folders by hand. Reinforce that search is faster and smarter.
Question 5: "What is the danger of sharing with 'Anyone with the link'?"
Expected answer: Anyone with the link — even strangers — can open the file, which effectively publishes it.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Notice the pattern in what you just answered: organise into containers, name clearly, give least access, and search instead
of scrolling. Everything you did for files, we now do for email. The containers become labels, the organising rule becomes a
filter, and the search bar stays the search bar. Same brain, new tool."
If the room struggles with Question 2 or 4, spend a few extra minutes reinforcing those before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
These notes are the actual teaching. Read them as written.
F.1 Why an organised inbox matters
The idea. Email is not just messages; in most workplaces it is the front door of the business. Orders arrive by email, invoices are
sent by email, job offers come by email, bank alerts come by email. If the front door is a pile of rubbish, real opportunities get
buried and lost.
Plain-English definition. Inbox management is the habit of processing your email deliberately — deciding quickly what each
message is, where it belongs, and what action it needs — instead of letting it pile up.
Why it exists as a skill. Email has no natural structure. Unlike a shop with shelves, an inbox is one long, endless list. The provider
(Google) does not know what matters to you; only you do. So the user must supply the structure.
Real-life comparison — the letterbox versus the mailroom. A house letterbox just collects everything: bills, adverts,
personal letters, junk. A professional office has a mailroom: someone sorts each item and sends it to the right department —
accounts get the bills, sales get the orders, and the rubbish goes in the bin. Most people's inbox is the letterbox. An organised
inbox is the mailroom.
What an organised inbox gives you:
Nothing important is missed. Orders and deadlines stand out.
Speed. You find an old email in seconds with search, not minutes with scrolling.
Peace of mind. Opening your email is calm, not stressful.
Professionalism. A prompt, tidy email reply signals that you are reliable.
Worked example — Adaeze Fashion. Adaeze gets about 30 emails a day: customer enquiries, fabric supplier invoices, bank
alerts, Jumia adverts, church announcements, school notices for her children. Without a system, she missed a ₦150,000 fabric
order because it arrived during a Jumia sale flood. With a simple system (a Customers label, a Finance label, and a filter that
sends adverts straight to Promotions ), her real orders rise to the top and she never loses one again.
Common misconception. Learners think inbox zero means "delete every email". It does not. Inbox zero means your inbox is
empty of things that still need a decision — not that you have no emails. Most useful emails get archived (kept, but out of
the inbox) or labelled, not deleted. We cover this in Section F.6.
Honest limitation. No system can make you read your email. The tools only remove friction. If you do not check in at least
once a day, even the best setup fails. Email management is a habit first, a technology second.
F.2 The Gmail interface — a guided tour
How to get there. In Chrome, type mail.google.com and press Enter. If you are logged into your Google account, your inbox
opens.
The main parts of the screen (menu wording can vary slightly between Google versions, and between the web version and the
phone app — the ideas are stable):
1. Compose button (top-left) — the big button to write a new email.
2. The left menu (sidebar):
Inbox — new messages.
Starred — messages you have starred.
Snoozed — messages you have told Gmail to bring back later.
Sent — everything you have sent.
Drafts — messages you started but have not sent.
More (a dropdown) — contains Spam, Trash, All Mail, Important, and Categories.
Labels — your own custom labels, listed underneath. This is where your system shows up.
3. Category tabs (at the top of the inbox). Gmail can sort incoming mail into tabs: Primary (real people and important mail),
Social (social networks), Promotions (adverts and offers), Updates (receipts, confirmations), Forums (group discussions). These
are not folders, and they are configurable in Settings → Inbox. They are a first, automatic layer of sorting.
4. The search bar (top). Types a search term or an operator to find messages. This is the most powerful tool in Gmail.
5. The settings gear (top-right) → See all settings. Where labels, filters, signatures, templates and many options live.
6. Right side panels. Calendar, Keep and Tasks can appear here. We meet Tasks properly in Session 4, but note that they live
beside your email.
Say to the class:
"Gmail is not complicated. On the left, your places: Inbox, Sent, Drafts, Spam, Trash and your Labels. On top, the search bar
and the settings gear. Everything else, Google does for you."
Common misconception. Learners sometimes think the Promotions and Social tabs are folders that hold the "real" email. They
are only Gmail's automatic guess at sorting. A genuine bank alert sometimes lands in Updates, and an important customer
email can accidentally land in Promotions. So do not ignore those tabs — check them, or better, create a filter to send
important senders to the Primary tab.
F.3 Labels and nested labels — Gmail's flexible filing system
The idea. A label is a tag you stick on an email to say what it is about. Instead of one big pile, you can mark an email as
"Customers", "Finance", "School", and so on.
Why labels instead of folders? This is the key difference and a very common exam question.
Folders (old-style email) Labels (Gmail)
An email lives in Exactly one folder Can carry many labels at once
Example An order from a customer that is also a payment goes in
either "Customers" OR "Finance"
The same email gets both
"Customers" and "Finance"
Removing it from the inbox You must move or delete You archive it; it keeps its labels
Because a real email often fits more than one category, labels are more powerful. An email with a receipt for a client order is
both about a customer and about money. With folders you would have to choose; with labels you can apply both.
Analogy. Think of coloured stickers, not boxes. A folder is a box — an item goes in one box only. A label is a sticker — you can
put three different stickers on the same envelope.
Nested labels. You can group labels under a parent using a forward slash / . For example:
Clients (parent)
```text
Clients/Orders
```
```text
Clients/Invoices
```
```text
Clients/Complaints
```
Finance (parent)
```text
Finance/Bank-Alerts
```
```text
Finance/Receipts
```
```text
Personal
```
```text
Personal/School
```
```text
Personal/Church
```
When you create a label named Clients/Orders , Gmail automatically creates Clients as its parent. Nested labels keep the
sidebar tidy instead of showing a long flat list.
How to create a label (exact steps):
1. In the left sidebar, scroll down to the Labels section.
2. Click Create new label (or the + sign, depending on version).
3. Type the label name. To nest it, tick Nest label under and choose the parent, or simply type the full name with a slash, e.g.
Clients/Orders .
4. Click Create.
How to apply a label to an email:
Open the email, click the label icon (looks like a tag) in the toolbar, tick the labels you want, and click Apply. Or
Select emails in the list with the checkbox, then click the label icon. Or
Drag an email from the list onto a label in the sidebar.
How to colour a label: in the sidebar, hover over the label, click the three-dot ⋮ → Label color, and pick one. Colours are
visual only; they help your eyes triage quickly.
Recommended starter set for a beginner (7–10 labels maximum):
Action (needs a reply or work — red)
Waiting (you are waiting for someone — yellow)
Clients (with nested Clients/Orders , Clients/Invoices )
Finance (bank, receipts, payments — green)
School or Work
```text
Personal
```
Reference (keep for later)
Promotions (offers and adverts — grey)
Common misconception. Learners create 30 labels and then feel overwhelmed. Fewer, well-chosen labels beat many. If a label
has fewer than a handful of emails and probably always will, you do not need it — use search instead.
Limitation. A label is only as useful as your discipline in applying it. A label you never use, or apply inconsistently, adds clutter
rather than removing it. Start small and grow only when a real need appears.
F.4 Filters and rules — making Gmail work automatically
The idea. A filter is a rule you set once. It tells Gmail: "When an email arrives matching this condition, do these actions
automatically." Labels are manual; filters are automatic. Filters are what turn a system from "I tidy up every day" into "the
tidying happens by itself".
Why they matter. You cannot manually label and file every future email forever. A filter handles the recurring things — bank
alerts, newsletters, invoices from a regular supplier — without you lifting a finger.
Familiar analogy — the automatic gate. A filter is like an automatic gate with a number-plate reader: "when this car arrives,
open the gate and let it into this compound." You set the rule once; the gate obeys forever.
Filters have two halves:
1. The condition (criteria) — what the incoming email must match. Options include:
From (sender's address)
To
Subject (words in the subject line)
Has the words (words anywhere in the email)
Doesn't have
Has attachment (yes/no)
Size (bigger/smaller than a number)
Date within (a time period)
or a raw search box where you can type operators directly.
2. The action — what Gmail should do. Options include:
Apply the label (choose one of your labels)
Skip the Inbox (Archive it) — the email is filed but does not clutter the inbox
Mark as read (so it is not "unread")
Star it
Apply to matching conversations (also affects existing old emails)
Forward it to another address
Delete it
Never send it to Spam
Always mark it as important
Categorize as (Primary, Social, Promotions, etc.)
How to create a filter (exact steps):
1. Click the settings gear (top-right) → See all settings.
2. Click the Filters and Blocked Addresses tab.
3. Scroll down and click Create a new filter.
4. Fill in the condition, e.g. From: alerts@bank.example .
5. Click Create filter. (A small dialog offers "Create filter" on the search box directly — this is a shortcut.)
6. Tick the actions you want, e.g. Apply the label: Finance/Bank-Alerts and Skip the Inbox (Archive it).
7. If you want it to also affect old emails already in the inbox, tick Also apply filter to matching conversations.
8. Click Create filter.
Worked examples:
Bank alerts: From alerts@bank.example → apply label Finance/Bank-Alerts , Skip the Inbox. Result: money messages instantly
filed; inbox stays clean.
Shopping adverts: From contains jumia → apply label Promotions , Skip the Inbox. Result: adverts never touch your inbox.
Invoices: Subject contains Invoice → apply label Finance/Receipts , Star it. Result: every invoice is flagged and filed
automatically.
Important client: From bigclient@example.com → apply label Clients , Never send it to Spam, Always mark as important.
Result: this client never gets lost, never gets flagged as junk.
Say to the class:
"Think about what lands in your inbox every single week — bank alerts, adverts, newsletters, invoices from the same
person. Name those. Each one deserves a filter. Then those emails sort themselves, and your inbox only holds the things
that truly need your attention today."
Common misconceptions:
"A filter only works on new email." By default yes — but you can tick Also apply filter to matching conversations to catch
old ones too.
"A filter can only do one thing." No — a single filter can label, archive, star and mark as read all at once.
"Creating a filter that archives everything is smart." It is dangerous if the condition is too broad. If you type has:attachment
and Skip the Inbox, every email with an attachment disappears from your inbox. Always test a filter on a small condition
first.
Limitation. Filters match on rules, not meaning. Gmail cannot know that this advert is important while that one is not. Keep
filters simple and specific; a filter that is too clever often misfires.
F.5 Searching Gmail effectively
The idea. The search bar is your fastest route to any email, even years old. Learn a few operators and you can find almost
anything in seconds.
Basic search. Type a word and press Enter — Gmail searches senders, recipients, subjects, and the body of messages.
The most useful operators:
What you type What it finds

F.6 Inbox zero — the three homes for an email
The idea. Inbox zero is a method, not a number. When you open an email, you decide, once, which of three homes it goes to
— and you empty your inbox of undecided mail.
The three homes and three fates for any email:
Fate What happens Use it when
Archive The email leaves the inbox but is kept in All Mail.
You can always find it by search or label.
The email needs no further action but you may want it
later (receipts, references, confirmations).
Delete (Trash) The email moves to Trash and is permanently
removed automatically after about 30 days.
Junk, spam, things of no value.
Keep / Act The email stays or gets a label, a star, or a snooze,
because it needs an action.
Real orders, replies you owe, deadlines.
Crucial distinction — archive vs delete:
Archive = "remove from my inbox, but keep the email." Most of your email should end up archived, not deleted.
Delete = "send to Trash, remove for good after 30 days." Only for genuinely worthless mail.
The inbox-zero workflow — process one email at a time:
1. Open the top email.
2. Ask: Does this need me to do something?
No, and I do not need it → Delete (junk).
No, but I might need it later → Archive (and optionally label it).
Yes, but I can do it in under 2 minutes → Do it now, then archive.
Yes, but it will take longer → Star or Snooze it, or add a task, and archive it out of the inbox so it does not nag you.
3. Repeat until the inbox is empty or nearly empty.
The 2-minute rule. If a task takes less than two minutes, do it immediately rather than filing it. It is quicker to act than to
manage the reminder.
How to archive: select the email and click the archive icon (a box with a downward arrow), or press E if keyboard shortcuts
are on. To find it later, click All Mail or search.
How to delete: click the trash icon, or press # .
Real-life comparison. Inbox zero is like washing plates after each meal instead of letting a mountain grow in the sink. A few
minutes a day keeps the whole kitchen usable. Letting it pile up turns a small chore into a dreaded task.
Common misconception. "Inbox zero means I must reply to everything." No — many emails need only to be archived. Inbox
zero means nothing is left undecided, not that you replied to all 30 messages.
Limitation. Inbox zero is a habit, and habits need rhythm. Plan a fixed time — say, morning and evening — to process email. If
you only do it once a week, the pile returns.
F.7 Snooze and Schedule Send — time-shifting email
The idea. Gmail lets you move email through time, not just space.
Snooze — make an email come back later. If an email needs action but not now, snooze it. It disappears from the inbox and
reappears at the time you chose — like an assistant handing it back to you at the right moment.
How to snooze: hover over the email in the list, click the clock icon, and choose a time (Later today, Tomorrow, Next week, or a
custom date and time). The email moves to Snoozed and returns automatically.
Use it for: "I will reply to this supplier tomorrow morning"; "Remind me about this deadline next Monday".
Schedule Send — write now, send later. If you need to send an email but want it to arrive at a specific time (e.g. 8am when
the office opens, or after you have finished something), do not wait around. Write it and schedule it.
How to schedule send:
1. Click Compose and write your email.
2. Instead of clicking Send, click the small down arrow beside the Send button.
3. Choose Schedule send.
4. Pick a date and time, then confirm.
The email sits in the Scheduled area until that time, then sends itself. This is useful for sending a reminder the night before but
having it arrive in working hours, or for sending across time zones.
Worked example — Adaeze. Adaeze wants to send a follow-up to a client but it is 11pm. She does not want to seem to be
working all night, and she wants the client to see it first thing. She writes it and uses Schedule send for 8:00am the next day. It
arrives at a professional hour.
Common misconception. Learners think a snoozed email is deleted. It is not — it is waiting in Snoozed and will return exactly
as promised.
Limitation. Snooze and schedule rely on your phone or computer being on with a connection to display the reminder. They are
powerful but not magic; check your Snoozed list occasionally to make sure nothing is stuck.

The idea. Two features save enormous time and make you look professional.
A signature is a block of text (your name, role, phone, business) that Gmail can automatically add to the bottom of every email.
It stops you retyping your details.
How to create a signature (exact steps):
1. Click the settings gear → See all settings.
2. Stay on the General tab; scroll down to Signature.
3. Click Create new, name it (e.g. "Main"), and click Create.

A template (the feature is called Templates; it used to be called Canned Responses) is a ready-made email you can insert into
any message with two clicks. Perfect for repeated messages: order confirmations, price enquiries, follow-ups, introductions.
How to turn on Templates first:

Common misconception. Learners expect templates to auto-personalise ("dear Ada", "dear Emeka"). Templates are static —
you insert them and then edit the blanks. (Smart personalisation needs more advanced tools we do not cover here.)
Limitation. A template used without editing looks careless. Always fill in the specific details before sending.
F.9 Attachments and Drive integration
The idea. You can attach files to email, and you can link to files in Drive. Knowing when to attach and when to link is important.
Attaching a file: in the Compose window, click the paperclip icon, choose the file from your computer, and it uploads with the
email. The recipient gets a copy.
Linking from Google Drive: in the Compose window, click the Google Drive icon (a triangle) in the toolbar. A panel opens
showing your Drive files; select one and choose whether to attach it as a copy or insert it as a link. Inserting a link is often better
for big files, and it means the recipient always sees the latest version.
Why link instead of attach?
Large files send faster and do not fill anyone's inbox.
The recipient always opens the current version, not an old copy.
You control access — but remember Section 1: only share with people who should have access.
Worked example. Adaeze sends a customer a 20-page catalogue. Instead of attaching a heavy file, she uploads the catalogue
to Drive once and sends the link. If she updates pages later, the customer's link always shows the latest version. If she had
attached it, she would have to resend every update.
Common misconception. Learners think a Drive link is automatically private. It follows the sharing setting from Session 1 —
keep it Restricted and add only the intended people.
Limitation. A link depends on the recipient's access. If the file is Restricted and you have not shared it with them, the link will
not open. Check the sharing before you send.
F.10 Summary of the lecture
An organised inbox is a mailroom, not a letterbox — it protects you from losing money and opportunities.
Gmail uses labels (tags), not folders — one email can have many labels. Use nested labels like Clients/Orders .
Filters are automatic rules: condition + action (label, archive, star, mark read).
Search with operators: from: , subject: , has:attachment , is:unread , newer_than:7d .
Inbox zero = archive, delete or act; nothing left undecided. Archive vs delete is the key distinction.
Snooze brings mail back later; Schedule send sends it later.
A signature is your automatic professional block; Templates save repeated emails.
Attach for small copies; link from Drive for large files and always-latest versions.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Mrs. Eucharia is the bursar (finance officer) at a small secondary school in Port Harcourt. She uses one Gmail account
for everything: fee receipts, parent enquiries, staff memos, ministry circulars, supplier invoices, bank alerts, and dozens of
newsletters and prayer-chain forwards. During the busy start-of-term period she receives over 150 emails a day. In one week, she
missed two important messages: a ministry deadline for submitting enrolment figures, and a parent's complaint about a wrong
fee charge. Both was buried under promotional mail.
Relevant facts.
The ministry deadline email came from a known government sender with "Enrolment Return" in the subject.
The parent complaint came from a parent's personal address with "Wrong charge" in the subject.
She receives regular bank alerts from a single sender, and a daily newsletter she rarely reads.
She is not a "computer person" but she is willing to learn, and she does everything in a browser.
Decision point. How can Mrs. Eucharia make sure that high-value email (government, parents, bank) stands out, while low-
value email (newsletters, adverts) stops cluttering her inbox — without learning anything complicated?
Possible approaches and trade-offs.
1. Create 30 labels and try to file everything by hand daily. Powerful but fragile: she will fall behind, and manual filing is
exactly what failed. Trade-off: too much effort, not sustainable.
2. A small system: 5–7 labels plus 3–4 filters that auto-file the recurring mail. Low effort, high payoff. Government
and parent mail get labelled and never sent to Spam; bank alerts auto-file; newsletters auto-archive. Trade-off: a little setup
time now, a calm inbox forever.
3. Set up a second email account and tell people to use it. Confusing for parents and government; creates two places to
check. Trade-off: adds complexity, not removes it.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. A small, targeted system:
Labels: Ministry , Parents , Finance/Bank-Alerts , Staff , Newsletters , Reference .
Filters:
From the ministry sender → label Ministry , Never send to Spam, Always mark important, plus a star.
From any address with "Wrong charge" in the subject → label Parents , mark important.
From the bank alert sender → label Finance/Bank-Alerts , Skip the Inbox.
From the newsletter sender → label Newsletters , Skip the Inbox.
She sets aside two fixed times each day (morning and after school) to process the inbox to zero using the 2-minute rule and
snooze.
Trade-offs of the recommendation. Filters only catch what she plans for; a brand-new sender she did not anticipate still lands
in the inbox — but because everything is labelled and the inbox is processed twice daily, nothing sits for long. She must keep
the filters updated as senders change.
Professional connection. This is precisely the work of an administrative officer, bursar, secretary or personal assistant. The
ability to design a resilient inbox — labels, filters, search, templates — is a directly employable skill. When Mrs. Eucharia shows
her system to the school board, she is demonstrating digital productivity competence, not just "knowing how to use email".

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this as the session's live troubleshooting guide. Point learners to the row; make them confirm the cause before fixing.
K.1 Too many labels — sidebar chaos
Symptoms: Dozens of labels; the sidebar is harder to read than the inbox. Likely cause: Labeling every conceivable topic at
once. How to investigate: Count the labels; ask whether each is used weekly. Corrective steps: Delete unused labels (right-
click → Remove label — this does not delete emails, only the tag); merge similar ones; use search instead of micro-labels.
Prevention: Start with 5–7 labels; grow only on real need.
K.2 A filter that archives everything
Symptoms: Expected emails vanish from the inbox. Likely cause: The filter condition is too broad (e.g. matching a common
word). How to investigate: Settings → Filters; read the condition of each filter. Check All Mail or the relevant label for the
"missing" mail. Corrective steps: Edit the filter to be specific (match a full sender address, not a common word); or delete it.
Prevention: Test a filter on new mail before applying it to old conversations.
K.3 Archived email "disappears"
Symptoms: Learner thinks archiving deleted the email. Likely cause: Misunderstanding archive vs delete. How to investigate:
Look in All Mail or search for it. Corrective steps: Explain archive keeps it; find it by search or a label. Reassure. Prevention:
Teach that archive = "out of inbox, still kept"; delete = "to Trash for 30 days".
K.4 Missing important email — it went to Spam
Symptoms: A genuine email is never seen. Likely cause: Gmail guessed it was junk; meanwhile the learner never checks Spam.
How to investigate: Search in:anywhere or open Spam. Corrective steps: Mark it Not spam; create a filter for that sender with
Never send it to Spam; add them to contacts. Prevention: Check Spam weekly; add trusted senders to contacts.
K.5 Signature not appearing
Symptoms: New emails have no signature. Likely cause: Settings not saved; or signature defaults set to only one mode (e.g.
only replies). How to investigate: Reopen Settings → General → Signature and check the box is filled and Save Changes was
clicked. Corrective steps: Edit the signature, set defaults for new mail and replies, save. Prevention: Always scroll to the
bottom of Settings and click Save Changes.
K.6 Template option is missing
Symptoms: No Templates choice in the compose ⋮ menu. Likely cause: Templates not enabled, or advanced settings not
saved. How to investigate: Settings → Advanced tab → check Templates = Enable. Corrective steps: Enable and Save
Changes; refresh Gmail; reopen Compose. Prevention: Enable Templates at the start of setup, before creating any.
K.7 Attachments too large to send
Symptoms: Gmail refuses a large attachment. Likely cause: The file exceeds the size limit (Gmail's limit is around 25 MB; larger
files require Drive). Corrective steps: Upload the file to Drive, set sharing to Restricted with the recipient added, and insert the
Drive link instead of attaching. Prevention: For any large file, default to a Drive link.
K.8 Deleting the wrong email
Symptoms: An important message was deleted. Likely cause: Mistaken click or a careless bulk delete. How to investigate:
Open Trash. Corrective steps: Select the message and click Move to Inbox, or right-click → Move to → Inbox. Prevention:
Archive instead of delete when unsure; Trash keeps mail for about 30 days.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed. The likely cause is a hypothesis; it becomes confirmed only after checking — see the filter's
condition, see the email in Spam, see Templates enabled. Teach learners to verify before fixing.

## M. Knowledge Check

(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key that follows.)
Q1. Short answer. In one or two sentences, explain the difference between a Gmail label and a traditional email folder.
Q2. Multiple choice. Which search would find all unread emails that have an attachment? a) is:unread from:attachment b)
has:attachment is:unread c) subject:unread attachment d) unread:yes file:yes
Q3. Explain in your own words. What is the difference between archiving an email and deleting it, and which should you do
more often?
Q4. Practical identification. Name the filter action you would choose for each situation: a) You want every bank alert to leave
your inbox automatically. b) You want every invoice email to be flagged for attention. c) You want a bulk newsletter out of your
inbox but kept for later.
Q5. Error detection. A learner sets up a filter with the condition Has the words: sale and the action Skip the Inbox. What is
the problem, and how would you fix it?
Q6. Scenario. Adaeze wants to send a follow-up email at 8:00am tomorrow but it is 11:00pm tonight. Which Gmail feature
should she use, and how is it different from Snooze?
Q7. Short answer. Why is it a bad idea to create thirty labels on your first day?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. A folder holds an email in one place only; a label is a tag, so a single email can carry many labels at the same time. Labels
are more flexible because real email often belongs to several categories at once.
A2. (b) has:attachment is:unread .
A3. Archive removes the email from the inbox but keeps it in All Mail, findable by search or label. Delete moves it to Trash,
where it is permanently removed after about 30 days. You should archive far more often than you delete — most useful email
should be kept, just out of the inbox.
A4. a) Apply label + Skip the Inbox (Archive it); b) Star it (and/or apply a label); c) apply a label + Skip the Inbox (keeps it for
later without cluttering the inbox).
A5. The condition is far too broad: any email containing the word "sale" — including genuine messages — will vanish from
the inbox. Fix it by matching a specific sender or phrase instead (e.g. From deals@shop.example ), or delete the filter.
A6. Schedule Send (the down arrow beside Send). It sends a new email at a chosen future time. Snooze is different: it hides an
email that already arrived and brings it back at a chosen time; it does not send anything.
A7. Too many labels recreate clutter and become hard to maintain; you will not use most of them consistently. Start with 5–7
meaningful labels and grow only when a real, repeated need appears; use search for one-off lookups.

## N. Reflection and Recap

Plain-English summary. Today you turned your inbox from a letterbox into a mailroom. You learned the parts of Gmail, and the
big idea that Gmail uses labels (tags), not folders — so one email can belong to several categories at once, including nested
labels like Clients/Orders . You built filters that file and flag email automatically, learned to search with operators like from: ,
subject: , has:attachment and is:unread , and adopted the inbox-zero habit — deciding each email's fate (archive, delete or
act) so nothing is left undecided. You also set up a professional signature, saved reusable templates, and learned to snooze
and schedule send. Finally, you saw when to attach a file and when to link to Drive instead.
Five important takeaways:
1. An organised inbox protects money and opportunities; it is a mailroom, not a letterbox.
2. Labels (including nested ones) beat folders because one email can carry many labels.
3. Filters are automatic rules — condition plus action — that file your recurring email for you.
4. Archive vs delete: archive keeps email out of the inbox but safe; delete sends it to Trash for 30 days. Archive more than you
delete.
5. Search, not scrolling: from: , subject: , has:attachment , is:unread find almost anything in seconds.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner explains labels vs folders correctly, including that one email can have several labels.
The learner creates a nested label on the first try.
The learner designs a filter with a specific condition and a sensible action, and can explain the risk of a broad condition.
The learner reaches inbox zero in the practice inbox using archive more than delete.
The learner uses search operators instead of scrolling.
The learner sets up a signature and saves a template without prompting.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Labels are the same as folders." → They are tags; many per email.
"Archiving deletes the email." → It keeps it in All Mail.
"Inbox zero means I must reply to everything." → It means nothing left undecided.
"A filter with 'Skip the Inbox' is safe for any condition." → Broad conditions hide important mail.
"Spam is always junk." → Real mail sometimes lands there; check weekly and mark 'Not spam'.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Label-sorting drill: give ten email subjects and ask which label each deserves; draw the nested structure.
2. Filter-building drill: give five recurring senders and ask the learner to write the condition and action for each.
3. Archive-or-delete drill: give ten emails and ask "archive or delete, and why?"
4. Search drill: give four "find this" tasks and ask for the operator(s).
5. Template drill: have the learner turn a common reply into a reusable template.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide the label names and one filter condition on a card; the learner creates them and the signature
only.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): design a complete filter system of five rules for a fictional shop (bank, supplier, customers, adverts,
government), write a one-page "Email Handling Policy" (how often to check, response-time targets, what to archive vs
delete), and set up a Google Tasks reminder linked from a starred email.
Timing guidance: If the room is slow, reduce practice to 8 emails and skip the Drive attachment sub-demonstration (Section
F.9) to a mention; protect the filter creation and inbox-zero practice, which are the core skills. If Wi-Fi is weak, pre-send the test
emails before class so they are already waiting.
