# Workflow and Automation

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 3: Task Management and Workflow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–4. The learner should be able to organise Drive folders, use Gmail labels and filters, write in Docs, make a simple Sheet formula and keep a Google Tasks list. If a learner cannot create a Gmail filter, the instructor reviews Section F.4 with them individually.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 we learned to capture and organise tasks. But many tasks are done the same way every day and week — retyping the same email, filing the same receipt, writing the same invoice. This session looks at those repeated tasks, writes them down as a workflow, and makes free tools do part of the work automatically.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged into Gmail; a clean demo inbox with at least ten sample emails for filter practice; printed "As-Is / To-Be" workflow template; printed keyboard-shortcut cheat sheet; a demo Google Form linked to a Google Sheet; examples of a Docs invoice template and a recurring calendar event.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define what a workflow is and give three examples of recurring workflows from a Nigerian small-business, school or
church setting.
2. Document the current "as-is" workflow of a real recurring task using numbered steps.
3. Identify at least three repetitive, time-wasting activities in that workflow.
4. Design an improved "to-be" workflow that removes or shortens the wasted steps.
5. Create a Gmail filter that automatically labels and archives incoming mail, and apply it to existing messages.
6. Use a Google Drive template ("Make a copy") so the same document is never retyped from zero.
7. Set up a recurring Google Calendar event for a repeated routine.
8. Use a curated set of keyboard shortcuts in Chrome, Gmail, Docs and Sheets to work without the mouse.
9. Build and write a daily productivity routine that ties Drive, Gmail, Docs/Sheets and Tasks together.
10. Explain why automation should come after organisation, and describe the limits of what free tools can automate.

D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes) — "The Two Hours That Disappear"
Instructor setup: Put a simple time budget on the projector:
```text
Funmi's typical day (office admin, 8 hours):
- Searching for files ...... 40 min
- Retyping the same replies.. 40 min
- Deciding what to do next... 30 min
- Actually useful work....... the rest
```
What the instructor says:
"This is Funmi. She is not lazy — she works a full day. But look: forty minutes hunting for files that have no clear home.
Forty minutes retyping the same 'thank you for your order' email she has written a hundred times. Thirty minutes staring
at her screen deciding what to do first. That is nearly two hours a day doing work that a machine could do, or that she
could avoid entirely.
Two hours a day is ten hours a week. That is a whole extra working day, every week, thrown away. Today we do two things:
we write down how we actually work, and we make free tools do the boring parts."
The hook (do this live): In the demo Gmail, show an email from a supplier. Ask: "How long would it take Funmi to label this and
file it by hand?" Then create a filter that labels and archives all such mail automatically. Say: "That one filter will now do that job
forever, for one minute of setup."
Expected learner response: Some learners will say "I do this too", "I didn't know email could do that", or "I just use my
memory". Accept all answers. Confirm that almost everyone wastes time on repetitive work, and that the fix is not to work
harder but to remove the repetition.
Why this opener works: It puts a number (₦ or hours) on the hidden cost of poor workflow, which makes the value obvious
and urgent, then gives an instant win with the filter.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as an oral round-robin with the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a Gmail filter, in one sentence?"
Expected answer: a rule that automatically acts on incoming mail — for example, applying a label or skipping the inbox —
based on criteria you set.
Common gap: learners confuse a label (a tag you apply) with a filter (an automatic rule). Re-state: a label is a box; a filter is a
machine that fills the box for you.
Question 2: "From Session 4, what are your three MITs?"
Expected answer: the three Most Important Tasks you chose to do today, starred and moved into the Today list.
Common gap: learners forget that MITs are limited to three. Reinforce the "rule of three".
Question 3: "In Google Docs, if you want the same invoice again next month, would you type it from scratch?"
Expected answer: no — you would make a copy of a template ( File > Make a copy ) and edit the copy.
Common gap: learners still retype documents. This question sets up today's automation lesson.
Question 4: "In Google Sheets, what does the formula =SUM(B2:B10) do?"
Expected answer: adds all the numbers from B2 down to B10 .
Common gap: forgetting the = sign.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Notice that everything we have learned so far makes storing and capturing easy. Today we make doing easy. A workflow
is just the steps you follow to do a recurring job — and once you can see the steps, you can remove the wasted ones and
let tools handle the rest."
If more than a third of the class cannot explain a Gmail filter, spend 8 extra minutes re-demonstrating filter creation before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a workflow actually is
Plain-English definition. A workflow is a repeatable sequence of steps you follow to complete a recurring task. "Recurring" is
the key word — it is a job you do again and again, not a one-off.
Why it exists. When a job repeats, we stop noticing the steps. We do it on autopilot, including all its wasteful habits (hunting
for a template, retyping, forgetting a step). Writing the workflow down turns a fog into a map — and a map can be improved.
What it does. A documented workflow lets you: see the whole job, find the slow parts, decide which parts a tool can do, and
hand the routine to someone else (or to a new version of yourself) without explaining from the beginning each time.
Real-life comparison. A workflow is a recipe. A good cook does not invent jollof rice from nothing each time; she follows a
sequence she has perfected. If the recipe has a wasteful step, she changes the recipe — not her energy.
Everyday workflow examples:
Order-to-delivery (tailor): receive order on WhatsApp → take measurements → write details → collect deposit → sew →
notify customer → deliver → collect balance → file the receipt.
Weekly school routine (student): check lecturer's WhatsApp group → note assignments → add to Google Tasks → do
research in Drive → submit on the portal → move file to an Archive folder.
Church announcements (admin): collect notices → draft in Google Docs → get approval → send by email → post on
WhatsApp → file the final copy in Drive.
Important distinction. A workflow is not the same as a task. A task is a single action; a workflow is the sequence of actions that
repeats. "Send the invoice" is a task; "the way we prepare and send invoices every week" is a workflow.
F.2 Documenting your current workflow — the "as-is" map
The idea. Before you improve anything, you must see it as it really is. This is called the as-is workflow. Do not write the version
you wish you followed — write the honest version.
How to map it (a simple method anyone can use):
1. Pick one recurring job (start with the most annoying one).
2. Write the steps in order as numbered lines, each starting with a verb.
3. Next to each step, write who does it and roughly how long it takes.
4. Mark the points where you lose time, lose information or make mistakes — put a star or a circle on them.
Worked example — Adaeze's "send weekly invoices" as-is map:
```text
AS-IS: Send weekly invoices (Adaeze Fashion)
1. Remember which customers owe money ........ (5 min, memory — often forgotten) *
2. Look for last month's invoice to copy ..... (15 min, searching Drive) *
3. Retype the invoice into a new Doc ......... (20 min, retyping) *
4. Type each customer's name and amount ...... (10 min, error-prone) *
5. Email each invoice one by one .............. (10 min)
6. File nothing; emails stay in the inbox .... (0 min, messy later) *
Total: ~60 minutes, every Monday.
```
Say this to the class:
"Notice that only the last steps are real work. Half an hour is spent remembering, searching and retyping. That is the waste
we are hunting."
Common misconception. Learners are tempted to write an ideal workflow immediately. Stop them. You cannot improve a
process you refuse to look at honestly.
F.3 Spotting repetitive, time-wasting activities
The idea. Once the as-is map exists, look for patterns. Most time-waste falls into five families:
Type of waste What it looks like Example
Retyping Typing the same content again and again Writing the same welcome email
Re-searching Hunting for a file, template or old message Finding last month's invoice
Re-entering data Moving information from one place to another by hand Copying names from WhatsApp into a sheet
Manual reminding Remembering to do something on a set day Weekly stock check
Re-deciding Making the same decision from scratch "Which customer gets a discount?"
The test to apply to every step. Ask: "If I had to do this step a hundred times, would I want to do it by hand?" If the answer is
no, it is a candidate for automation.
Estimate the cost. Multiply the minutes wasted by how often it repeats. Adaeze wastes about 30 minutes per week on the
invoice process = 26 hours a year. Put in naira terms: if her time is worth ₦1,500 an hour, that is almost ₦40,000 of her year
spent retyping.
Say this to the class:
"You do not need to automate everything. Pick the steps that are frequent and boring. Automating a rare, complicated
task is not worth it."
F.4 Simple automation with free Google tools
Automation means letting a tool do a repeated step for you. You do not need to write code — the Google tools already include
automation. Here are the four patterns we learn today.
Pattern 1 — Gmail filters for auto-labelling and archiving
A filter is a rule: when a new email matches these conditions, do these actions automatically.
Common conditions: From, To, Subject, Has the words, Doesn't have, Has attachment, Size, Date. Common actions: Apply a
label, Skip the Inbox (Archive it), Mark as read, Star it, Apply a label, Forward, Delete, Never send to Spam.
How to create a filter (exact steps, wording may vary slightly by version):
1. In Gmail, click the search bar and fill in the criteria (for example, From: orders@adanmafoods.com ). Alternatively click the
sliders icon at the right of the search bar to open search options.
2. At the bottom of the search results, or in the drop-down, click Create filter.
3. Choose the actions you want, for example:
Apply the label: choose or create Orders (you can create a new label straight from this menu).
Skip the Inbox (Archive it): so the message is labelled but does not clutter the inbox.
Also apply filter to matching conversations: to tidy the messages already in your inbox.
4. Click Create filter.
Another route: Settings (gear) > See all settings > Filters and Blocked Addresses > Create a new filter.
Worked example — auto-file bills:
```text
Condition:  Subject contains "your statement is ready"  (or From: your bank)
Action:     Apply label "Finance/Bills"  +  Skip the Inbox  +  Mark as read
Result:     Every bank statement files itself and never clutters your inbox.
```
Say this to the class: "A filter is a servant that never sleeps. You teach it once, it works forever."
Pattern 2 — Google Forms feeding Google Sheets
The idea. A Google Form is an online form you can share as a link. Every submission is automatically collected into a linked
Google Sheet — you do not type anything.
Why it matters. This removes the "re-entering data" waste. Instead of a customer sending details by WhatsApp and Adaeze
copying them into her book, the customer fills a form and the row appears in the sheet automatically.
How it works (exact steps, wording may vary):
1. Go to forms.google.com and click Blank (or start from a template).
2. Add questions: Customer name, Phone, Item ordered, Quantity, Delivery date.
3. Click the Responses tab, then click the green Sheets icon to "Create a new spreadsheet". All answers now flow into that
Sheet.
4. Share the form link on WhatsApp status or a business page. Orders arrive in a tidy row-by-row list.
Worked example — a small shop order form: the shop posts a form link; every order becomes a row with a timestamp, so
the shop has a permanent record and can total orders with =SUM .
Pattern 3 — Drive and Docs templates ("Make a copy")
The idea. Instead of retyping the same document, keep one perfect template and make a copy each time.
How it works:
1. Create the master document (for example Invoice Template ) with your business name, a table for items, a total cell and your
account details.
2. Keep it in a Templates folder in Drive.
3. For each new invoice: right-click the template in Drive and choose Make a copy, or open it and use File > Make a copy.
4. Rename the copy with the naming convention and fill in the details.
Worked example: Adaeze's Invoice Template already has the table, the logo and a =SUM total formula. Creating a new invoice
becomes a one-minute job of changing three cells instead of a twenty-minute retype.
Tip: Keep the Templates folder read-only to yourself so you never accidentally overwrite the master.
Pattern 4 — Recurring Google Calendar events
The idea. Anything you must do on a fixed schedule — a weekly review, a monthly stock check, paying staff — becomes a
recurring event with an automatic reminder.
How to set a recurring event (exact steps, wording may vary):
1. Open Google Calendar and click Create (or click the day to begin an event).
2. Add a title, for example Weekly Review — Friday 4pm .
3. Set the date and the time.
4. Click the drop-down that says Does not repeat and choose Weekly (or Daily / Monthly / Yearly / Custom).
5. Choose when the repetition should end (or "never").
6. Click Save.
Worked example: A church admin sets Prepare Sunday bulletin to repeat every Thursday. The reminder appears on the right
day without anyone remembering it.
Honest limits of automation
Google's built-in filters and forms are powerful but simple. They cannot, for example, "read" a WhatsApp message on their
own.
Advanced tools (Zapier, Make, IFTTT) and Google Apps Script can connect more apps, but they are optional, beyond this
course, and can cost money. You do not need them to be highly productive.
Automate after you organise, never before. Automating a messy process just makes the mess happen faster.

F.9 Building your daily productivity routine
Automation handles repeated steps. A daily routine handles the repeated habits that tie your tools together. The routine below
uses everything from the whole course.
Morning (about 15 minutes):
1. Open Google Calendar — see what is fixed today.
2. Open Google Tasks — run your daily review (check lists, choose three MITs, move them to Today ).
3. Open Gmail — triage the inbox: delete spam, archive reference, reply to quick items, turn action emails into tasks.
Midday — the work block (roughly 90 minutes): 4. Pick one MIT and work on it with notifications off (close WhatsApp Web,
silence your phone). 5. Use Docs/Sheets to do the actual work, working from your templates.
End of day — shutdown (about 10 minutes): 6. File finished documents into the right Drive folder with a proper name. 7.
Update Google Tasks — tick off what is done, reschedule what is not. 8. Plan tomorrow — preview the calendar and pre-pick
your three MITs.
Weekly (about 30 minutes, same day each week — put it in Calendar as recurring): 9. Run the weekly review of your task
lists (Session 4). 10. Check your Templates folder and update anything out of date. 11. Check your Gmail filters still make sense.
12. Back up any important local files to Drive.
Say this to the class:
"A routine is not a prison — it is a railway track. It carries you through the day without you having to decide, again and
again, what to do next. That repeated deciding is one of the biggest hidden time-wasters."
F.10 Summary of the session
A workflow is a repeatable sequence of steps for a recurring task.
Document the as-is honestly, then design the to-be.
Hunt five kinds of waste: retyping, re-searching, re-entering, manual reminding, re-deciding.
Automate with Gmail filters, Forms → Sheets, Drive/Docs templates and recurring Calendar events.
Learn keyboard shortcuts for Chrome, Gmail, Docs and Sheets; the full list lives at ? and Ctrl + / .
Tie it together with a daily routine: morning triage, a focused work block, and an end-of-day shutdown.
Automate after you organise, never before.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
G.1 What the instructor prepares
Chrome signed into the demo Gmail account with a messy demo inbox (ten-plus messages).
The Tasks side panel, a Templates folder in Drive with an Invoice Template Doc, and Google Calendar open.
Gmail shortcuts already enabled (Settings > See all settings > General > Keyboard shortcuts on).
The projector showing the screen.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — Map as-is, then build a Gmail filter (7 minutes)

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
K.1 The filter catches too much (or nothing)
Symptoms: After creating a filter, unrelated mail is labelled, or nothing matches. Likely causes: The criteria are too broad (for
example "has the words: order") or too narrow (a spelling mistake, or the wrong sender). How to investigate: Go to Settings >
Filters and Blocked Addresses and read the criteria; test with search first. Corrective steps: Edit the filter (click Edit), narrow or
correct the criteria, and save. How to confirm resolved: Only the intended mail is affected. Prevention: Use a specific sender or
an exact subject phrase; test with search before creating the filter.
K.2 "My emails disappeared!"
Symptoms: A learner thinks archived mail is deleted. Likely cause: Choosing Skip the Inbox (Archive it) moved mail out of the
inbox. Corrective steps: Find the mail under the new label, or open All Mail. Prevention: Explain early that archive protects
mail; only "Delete" removes it (and even then it goes to Trash).
K.3 Shortcuts do nothing
Symptoms: Pressing c , e or j has no effect in Gmail. Likely cause: Gmail keyboard shortcuts were never enabled.
Corrective steps: Settings > See all settings > General > Keyboard shortcuts on > Save Changes. Prevention: Enable them
once at the start.
K.4 Automating a messy process
Symptoms: A filter or form is set up, but the underlying process is still chaotic. Likely cause: Automation was applied before
organisation. Corrective steps: Go back to the as-is map, fix the structure first, then re-automate. Prevention: Remember the
rule — organise first, automate second.
K.5 Recurring event repeats at the wrong time or day
Symptoms: The weekly reminder shows on the wrong day. Likely cause: The wrong repeat option (Daily instead of Weekly) or
the wrong start date. Corrective steps: Open the event, click Edit, fix the repeat drop-down and start date, and Save.
Prevention: Check the start day before choosing the repeat option.
K.6 The template keeps getting overwritten
Symptoms: The Invoice Template now contains a specific customer's data. Likely cause: The learner edited the master instead
of a copy. Corrective steps: Rebuild the clean master; move it to a Templates folder. Prevention: Always Make a copy and edit
the copy; keep the master read-only.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Always confirm the cause (read the filter criteria, check the repeat setting) before changing
anything.
