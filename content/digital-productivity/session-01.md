# Google Drive and File Organisation

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module: Cloud Storage and Email

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (basic computer skills assumed)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Basic computer skills: creating and saving files, using a mouse and keyboard, basic internet browsing. If a learner cannot create a folder on a Windows desktop or save a file, give them the 15-minute Desktop Refresher in Appendix 1A before this session.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session. It builds directly on the Typing & Computer Basics course — learners already know how to save a file to a folder on a computer. This session moves that same idea into the cloud.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged in; Google Chrome installed and updated; every learner has a working Gmail account (create in the first 10 minutes for anyone who lacks one — see Section C note); Wi-Fi tested and working; the 20 sample files loaded into a folder Desktop\CEA Samples on each practice machine (file list in Section C); instructor computer connected to the projector; learner workbooks and the file-naming cheat sheet printed.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words what cloud storage is, using an analogy, and give at least two reasons why it is safer and more
convenient than keeping files only on one computer.
2. Name and locate the main parts of the Google Drive interface — My Drive, Shared with me, Recent, Starred, Trash and the
+ New button — without help.
3. Build a logical folder structure of at least five folders with nested subfolders for a fictional business, following the rule of
shallow, predictable, numbered folders.
4. Apply a consistent file naming convention using the YYYY-MM-DD - Project - Description - v1 format, and explain why
final_final_v3 and Document1 are bad names.
5. Upload files and a folder to Google Drive using the + New button and by dragging and dropping, then move each file into
the correct folder.
6. Share a file and a folder and set the correct permission level (Viewer, Commenter or Editor), explaining the security danger
of "Anyone with the link".
7. Find any file within 30 seconds using Drive search, search operators ( type: , owner: , is:starred ) and filters.
8. Use starring and folder colours to mark and visually organise important items.
9. Recover a file from the Trash and explain the 30-day deletion rule.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, open a plain photo of a physical flash drive (a stock image or a photo of one plugged into a
laptop). Do not open Drive yet. Just show the flash drive.
What the instructor says:
"Let me tell you a short story. Mama Ngozi runs a provision shop near Mile 3 market here in Port Harcourt. She keeps all her
records — sales, records of who owes her, supplier prices — in a notebook, and every night she types them into a small
old laptop, then copies them to a flash drive for safety.
One Monday morning, the laptop is stolen from the shop. The flash drive was inside the laptop bag. Gone. Two years of
records — gone. Her customers still owe her money but she cannot prove how much. Her suppliers' records are lost.
Now here is the sad part: she did a reasonable thing. She backed up to a flash drive! But the backup was in the same bag
as the thing it was backing up. If fire, theft or water hits that spot, you lose both.
So what should she have done? [Pause. Let learners answer.] … Yes — she should have kept the records somewhere that
is not in her shop at all. Somewhere a thief cannot reach, water cannot reach, fire cannot reach. Somewhere she can open
from anywhere — from her phone at the market, from a computer at a cyber café, from any device.
That place is called cloud storage. Today you will set up your own, using a free Google service called Google Drive."
Expected learner response: Learners may say "she should have emailed it to herself", "save it on phone", "Google", or stay
quiet. Accept all answers. Praise anyone who mentions Google or online backup, and gently correct "save on phone" by
explaining that a phone can also be stolen — the point is that the copy lives on Google's servers, not on any one device.
Hook to close the opener (demonstrate live):
"Watch this." [Change the photos/videos you streamed to cover full opening and closing — see below.] Open Chrome, log
in to your own Gmail, go to drive.google.com . Say: "This is my Drive. Everything I put here lives on Google's computers, not
on this machine. If I take a hammer to this computer right now, my Drive files still exist. In the next 105 minutes, each of you
will build your own organised Drive."
Why this opener works: It starts from a real, emotionally true Nigerian scenario (stolen laptop, lost records, debts unprovable)
that learners immediately understand, and it creates the gap the session will fill: how to keep your digital life safe and organised.
It ends with a live demo, so learners see the promised tool within 90 seconds.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this quickly as an oral round-robin. Put the four questions on the projector. Call on learners by name so everyone is awake,
and keep the pace fast.
Question 1: "On a Windows computer, how do you create a new folder on the Desktop?"
Expected answer: Right-click on the Desktop → New → Folder, then type a name and press Enter.
Common gap: some learners only know how to save a file to the Desktop and let the computer auto-create files with no
organisation. Remind them that on a computer a folder is just a container for files — exactly the same idea exists in Drive.
Question 2: "What does Ctrl + S do, and why does it matter?"
Expected answer: It saves the file you are working on. Without saving, your changes are lost when the power goes off or the
app closes.
Common gap: learners who only use phones may not know keyboard shortcuts. Show Ctrl + S physically and note that
on the cloud we will see a very different behaviour — auto-save.
Question 3: "What is a file name, and what is a file extension?"
Expected answer: The file name is what you call the file; the extension is the letters after the dot that tell the computer the
file type — e.g. .pdf , .jpg , .docx .
Common gap: confusing the two, or not knowing that the extension matters. Clarify that the extension tells the computer
(and often us) what kind of file it is.
Question 4: "What does a file's location mean?"
Expected answer: The path to where it lives — the folder and subfolders that contain it, for example Desktop\CEA-
Samples\Invoice-001.pdf .
Common gap: learners who save everything to one place ("It is on my Desktop somewhere") — this is exactly the habit we
are correcting today.
Bridge to today (say this):
"Everything you just described — folders, file names, extensions, locations — exists in Google Drive too. The difference is
where the files physically are. On your computer, they sit on a metal disk inside the machine in front of you. In the cloud,
they sit on Google's computers, and you reach them through the browser. Same ideas, safer home. Let us go."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1 (creating a folder), spend 15 extra minutes on the Appendix 1A
Desktop Refresher before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
These notes are the actual teaching. Read them as they are written — do not summarise.
F.1 What cloud storage actually is
The idea. Normally, when you save a file, it is written onto a physical piece of hardware — the hard disk inside the computer in
front of you. That is called local storage. The file lives in one place, on one machine.
Cloud storage means the file is saved on computers owned by a company (in this case Google) somewhere else in the world,
and you reach it over the internet. Your computer becomes a window through which you look at files that actually live far away.
Plain-English definition. Cloud storage is a service where your files are kept on powerful computers ("servers") in a data centre,
and you use any device with internet — a desktop, a phone, a tablet — to upload, open, edit and retrieve them.
The word "cloud". There is no actual cloud in the sky. The word is a picture. When engineers draw a network diagram, they
draw the internet as a fluffy cloud shape, because "the internet" is a big, vague, everywhere-and-nowhere network. So "cloud
storage" simply means "storage on the internet somewhere". The files are on real, solid, air-conditioned machines in real
buildings. Google has data centres on several continents.
Why it exists. Local storage has three weaknesses:
1. It is fragile. The machine can be stolen, dropped, get wet, or the disk can fail. Then the files are gone.
2. It is trapped in one place. To use a local file you must be sitting at that one computer.
3. It is hard to share. To give someone a local file you must copy it to a flash drive, or email an attachment — and then there
are two copies that drift apart.
Cloud storage was invented to fix all three at once.
Familiar analogy — the generator and the public power supply. Think of the electricity in your area. Years ago, every shop
that wanted power needed its own small generator — noisy, fuelled, and if the generator spoilt, the shop was in darkness.
Today many people connect to the public power grid; the power is generated far away and delivered to everyone. Cloud storage
is the "public grid" for files. Instead of every person running their own little "file generator" (their PC's disk), everybody can plug
into one big reliable system. Your files stay safe even when your own machine has a problem.
What cloud storage does for you (the four benefits):
Access anywhere. Log in from any computer, tablet or phone.
Backup and safety. Even if your computer is stolen or breaks, the files survive.
Easy sharing. Send a link instead of an attachment; the person sees the latest version.
Version history. You can recover an older version of a file if you make a mistake.
Worked example — Adaeze types a CV. Adaeze is a learner at CEA and runs a small sewing business, Adaeze Fashion. On
Monday she types her CV on the academy computer and saves it to Google Drive (not just the Desktop). On Friday she is at
home; she opens her phone, opens the Drive app, and the CV is there. On Sunday she goes to a job interview and prints it at a
business centre. She never copied it to a flash drive, never emailed it to herself, never lost it. The same file, reachable from three
different places.
Common misconception #1 — "The cloud is not safe; I have heard of hackers." This is understandable but confused. Your
personal Drive is protected by your Google password, and Google encrypts your data. The realistic danger is not Google's servers
— those are far more secure than a shop laptop. The real danger is your own password habits: a weak password, or sharing
files with the wrong setting. We teach safe sharing in Section F.7.
Common misconception #2 — "If I have no internet, my files are gone." In fact your files are still safe on Google's servers;
you simply cannot reach them at that moment. Google Chrome can also be set up for offline access, so that files you have
opened recently stay available even with no internet. Treat internet as the road to your files, not the files themselves.
An honest limitation. Cloud storage depends on internet and on Google staying in business. Google is one of the largest
companies in the world, but for truly critical records a wise person keeps a second copy in a different place as well — for
example a printed copy or an external drive stored elsewhere. One backup is good; the rule of professionals is: important data
lives in two places. Cloud storage is one strong place to keep a copy.
F.2 The Google Drive interface — a guided tour
How to get there. Open Google Chrome. In the address bar type drive.google.com and press Enter. If you are already logged
into Gmail, Drive opens straight away. If not, it asks you to sign in with your Google account.
The main parts of the screen (menu wording may vary slightly between Google versions and between a computer and a
phone, but these are the standard parts):
1. My Drive — your personal space. This is where your own files and folders live. When people say "my Drive", they usually
mean this.
2. Shared with me — files and folders other people have shared with you. Important: these files are not yours; they live in the
other person's Drive and were simply shared to you. If that person removes your access, they disappear from your list. Do not
confuse "Shared with me" with your own backup.
3. Recent — the files you last opened, newest first. A shortcut for "where was I?".
4. Starred — files and folders you have marked with a star, like a bookmark or a favourite. Use it for your most important
items.
5. Trash — deleted files. In Drive, deleting a file does not destroy it immediately; it moves to Trash, where it stays for about 30
days before being permanently removed. This is your safety net. You can restore a file from Trash, or empty the Trash to delete
forever.
6. Storage — shows how much of your free space you are using. With the free plan you currently get about 15 GB shared
across Gmail, Drive and Photos.
7. The + New button (top-left) — the main way to create things. Clicking it gives: New folder, File upload, Folder upload,
and shortcuts to create Google Docs, Google Sheets, Google Slides, Google Forms, and more.
8. The Search bar (top) — type a file name or a search operator to find anything. We cover this in Section F.6.
9. View options — a toggle between List view (rows, good for seeing details) and Grid view (icons, good for photos). A Sort
option lets you sort by name, last modified or last opened.
10. The Details pane (an ⓘ information icon, top-right) — shows information about the selected file: who owns it, when it
was modified, its size, and its sharing status.
Say to the class:
"Do not be afraid of this screen. It has only a few real places: your stuff (My Drive), other people's stuff shared with you
(Shared with me), your recent and starred shortcuts, and the bin (Trash). Everything else is decoration."
Common misconception. Learners sometimes think files in Shared with me are now safe in their own Drive. They are not. If
your friend deletes the file, it deletes for you too. If you need it permanently, make your own copy: right-click the file → Make a
copy.
F.3 Creating a logical folder structure
The idea. A folder structure is the way you group files so that (a) you can find things quickly and (b) a stranger could guess
where a file belongs. Bad structure creates digital noise; good structure creates calm.
Why it matters. If all 200 of your files sit loose in My Drive with no folders, finding one becomes a hunting game and you will
waste minutes every day. A good structure means you always know where to look and where to put things.
Familiar analogy — the market stall versus the supermarket. A roadside seller who throws everything in one big bag cannot
find a single sachet of milk without emptying the bag. A supermarket arranges goods into sections — beverages, provisions,
toiletries — and inside each section by type. You walk straight to what you need. Your Drive is your digital supermarket; folder
structure is your shelving.
The rules of a good structure:
1. Shallow, not deep. Two to three levels is plenty. A folder inside a folder inside a folder inside a folder becomes a maze. If
you are five levels deep, flatten it.
2. Numbered top folders for sort order. Prefix main folders with 01- , 02- , 03- so they always appear in the order you
want, instead of alphabetically.
3. Predictable names. Anyone should be able to guess which folder a file goes into.
4. One folder per purpose. Do not mix invoices and photos in the same folder.
5. Consistent style. If you use hyphens in one folder name, use hyphens in all. If you use Title Case, keep it. Consistency is
what makes a system feel professional.
Suggested scheme — Adaeze Fashion (our running example). Adaeze sews and sells clothes and does a little social-media
marketing. A simple, professional structure for her:
```text
My Drive
└── Adaeze Fashion
    ├── 01-Admin
    │   ├── Invoices
    │   ├── Receipts
    │   └── Tax-and-Licences
    ├── 02-Designs
    │   ├── Logos
    │   └── Sketch-Photos
    ├── 03-Customers
    │   ├── Orders
    │   └── Measurements
    ├── 04-Marketing
    │   ├── Flyers
    │   └── Social-Media-Posts
    └── 05-Archive
```
Why this works. Adaeze has a main folder named after the business. Inside it, five numbered areas cover everything she does:
Admin (money paperwork), Designs (creative work), Customers (people and their orders), Marketing (selling), Archive (old
stuff). Any new file has an obvious home. A shop assistant she hires tomorrow can guess the system.
A second, different scheme — organising by year. If your work is mostly per-year (school, church, yearly reports), you may
prefer a date-first structure:
```text
My Drive
└── Church-Office
    ├── 2025
    │   ├── 01-Services
    │   ├── 02-Events
    │   └── 03-Finance
    └── 2026
        ├── 01-Services
        ├── 02-Events
        └── 03-Finance
```
How to decide. Ask: "Do I mostly look for things by what they are (invoices, photos) or by when they happened (2025, 2026)?"
Business admin usually goes by type; records and reports often go by year. Either is fine — the bad option is having no system
at all.
How to create a folder in Drive (exact steps):
1. Click + New (top-left).
2. Click New folder.
3. Type the name, e.g. Adaeze Fashion .
4. Click Create.
5. To make a subfolder, double-click the parent folder to open it, then repeat + New → New folder.
To rename or recolour a folder: right-click it → Rename, or right-click → Change color and pick a label colour. Colour is only
visual — it does not change who can access the folder — but a consistent colour scheme (red for urgent, green for money)
helps your eyes find things fast.
Common misconception. New users often create all folders at the top level, so My Drive becomes a cluttered list of twenty
folders. Instead, group them: one business folder, with the categories inside it. The top level should have only a handful of big
ideas.
Limitation to be honest about. There is no single "correct" structure that Google enforces; Drive does not stop you from
making a mess. Your structure is only as good as your consistency in keeping it. The system collapses the moment you dump a
file in the wrong place and stop caring.
F.4 File naming conventions
The idea. A naming convention is a rule you follow every time you name a file, so that the name itself tells you (and anyone
else) exactly what the file is, when it was made, and which business it belongs to — without opening it.
Why it matters. The file name is the first and cheapest information you get. A name like Document1.docx forces you to open the
file to find out what it is. A name like 2026-10-09 - AdaezeFashion - Invoice-001 - v1.pdf tells you everything at a glance.
Familiar analogy — labelling jars in a kitchen. A good cook labels every jar: "Rice", "Beans", "Sugar — 2025". An unlabelled
jar could be anything and risks a nasty surprise. File names are your labels.
The recommended CEA convention:
```text
YYYY-MM-DD - ProjectName - Description - vN.ext
```
Break it down:
YYYY-MM-DD — the date, written year first. 2026-10-09 means 9 October 2026. Writing the year first makes files sort in true
chronological order when you sort by name; if you wrote 09-10-2026 they would sort by day, which scrambles them.
ProjectName — the business or project, e.g. AdaezeFashion .
Description — what the file actually is, e.g. Invoice-001 , Colour-Sketch , Customer-Feedback .
vN — the version number, e.g. v1 , v2 , v3 . This replaces the chaotic final , final2 , final_final_FINAL_use-this-one .
.ext — the file extension, which the computer adds for you.
Worked examples:
```text
2026-10-09 - AdaezeFashion - Invoice-001 - v1.pdf
```
```text
2026-10-12 - AdaezeFashion - Customer-Measurements-Ngozi - v2.xlsx
```
```text
2026-11-01 - ChurchOffice - Harvest-Programme-Flyer - v3.jpg
```
Bad names and why:
Bad name The problem
```text
Document1
```
Tells you nothing. Which document? Whose? When?
```text
final_final_v3_really_final
```
No date, no project, no meaning. A sign of chaos.
```text
New folder (2)
```
Which one is the real one? You will never know.
```text
INVOICE
```
No date, no client, no version — you cannot tell it from last month's invoice.
```text
ada's cv new version latest.docx
```
Spaces and apostrophes are messy; "latest" means nothing in a month.
How to rename a file in Drive: right-click the file → Rename (or click the file, then use the three-dot ⋮ menu → Rename),
type the new name, press Enter.
Good habits to teach:
Rename immediately when you create or upload a file. Never leave Untitled or Document1 .
Use hyphens or underscores instead of spaces where your tools allow, because some systems handle spaces badly in links
and downloads. Hyphens are the CEA preference.
Do not use these characters in a file name: \ / : * ? " < > | . Google Drive will reject or alter them.
Keep version numbers increasing: v1, v2, v3. Never go backwards.
Keep names reasonably short. A 90-character name is hard to read.
Common misconception. Learners think the date in the file name should be the date they created the file. Actually, it is more
useful to use the date the file is about or is due — the date that helps you find it later. For an invoice dated 9 October, that is
the invoice date. For an assignment due on 20 October, many people prefer the due date. Agree on one meaning and keep it
consistent.
Limitation. A naming convention only pays off if the whole team follows it. If you are the only person in your business, you only
have to convince yourself. If you have staff, write the rule down and put it on the wall.
F.5 Uploading files and folders
The idea. Uploading means copying a file from your computer up to Google Drive. After uploading, a copy exists in the cloud;
the original still sits on your computer.
Two ways to upload:
Way 1 — the + New button (precise and safe):
1. Open the folder in Drive where the file should go (double-click into it).
2. Click + New → File upload (for one or more files) or + New → Folder upload (to upload a whole folder including what is
inside it).
3. A Windows file-picker window opens. Navigate to Desktop\CEA-Samples .
4. Select the file(s) — hold Ctrl to select several at once — and click Open.
5. Watch the upload progress in the bottom-right corner. When it says "# uploads complete", the files are in Drive.
Way 2 — drag and drop (fast and visual):
1. Arrange your windows so you can see both the Windows folder and the Google Drive browser window side by side.
2. Select files/folders in Windows and drag them onto the Drive window.
3. Release the mouse when Drive highlights. The upload begins.
Which way to teach first? The + New button, because it is deliberate and easy to explain, and because with drag-and-drop
learners often drop files into the wrong folder without noticing.
Familiar analogy. Uploading is like carrying a photocopy of a document to a safe deposit box in a bank across town. The original
stays with you; a protected copy now lives elsewhere. If your house burns, the bank copy survives.
What "Move to" does (important). If you uploaded a file to the wrong place, do not panic and do not upload again. Right-
click the file → Move to → choose the folder → Move. This relocates the file without making a duplicate.
Common misconception. Learners think uploading moves the file off their computer. It does not — it copies. This is actually a
good thing: you now have the local copy and the cloud copy. But it also means new users can get confused about which copy
is "the real one". Teach the habit: for work files, treat the Drive copy as the master and stop editing the Desktop copy.
Limitation / honest note. Uploading many large files (videos, big photo sets) can be slow and uses your data. The free 15 GB
also fills up. For heavy media work, Google Drive is manageable for beginners but professionals use paid storage or video
platforms.
F.6 Finding files quickly — search and filters
The idea. You should never scroll and hunt. You should search. A well-organised Drive plus a smart search finds almost
anything in seconds.
Basic search. Click the Search bar at the top and type part of the file name or words from inside the file. Drive searches both file
names and (for many file types) the text inside documents.
Advanced search operators. You can type special commands into the search bar. These are the most useful for beginners:
What you type What it finds
```text
type:pdf
```
Only PDF files (also type:image , type:document , type:spreadsheet )
```text
owner:me
```
Only files you own
What you type What it finds
```text
is:starred
```
Only files you have starred
```text
is:trashed
```
Files currently in the Trash
```text
from:adaeze@gmail.com
```
Items shared by that person
```text
to:me
```
Items shared with you
```text
modified:today
```
Files changed today ( modified:2026-10-09 for a date)
```text
before:2026-10-01
```
Files modified before that date
```text
title:invoice
```
Files whose name contains "invoice"
Worked example — Adaeze finds a lost invoice. Adaeze needs the invoice she sent to a client last month. Instead of opening
folder after folder, she clicks the search bar and types:
```text
title:Invoice type:pdf modified:2026-09
```
Drive instantly lists all PDF invoices modified in September 2026. She opens the right one in under 10 seconds.
Filters (the visual alternative). If you prefer clicking to typing, click the small filter icon at the right end of the search bar. A
panel appears letting you filter by Type (folder, document, PDF, image…), People (owner), Modified date, Location, and
Shared to. This does the same job as the operators, without memorising any.
Starring — your personal bookmark. Right-click a file → Add to Starred (or open the file and click the star). Starred files
appear under Starred in the left menu. Use it for the handful of files you open every day.
Say to the class:
"Remember the goal: find any file within 30 seconds. If you cannot, your structure or your naming is letting you down. Fix
the structure once; save time every day."
Common misconception. Learners search for a file they think they uploaded but cannot find it. Usually it was uploaded into the
wrong folder, or it is still uploading, or it was never uploaded. Check Recent (shows newest activity) and the upload progress
area.
F.7 Sharing files and folders — and the security lesson
The idea. Sharing lets another person open your file or folder, without you emailing a copy. They always see the latest version.
How to share a file (exact steps):
1. Right-click the file → Share (or select it and click the Share button).
2. In the box that opens, type the person's email address.
3. Choose their permission level (see below).
4. Optionally add a short message.
5. Click Send. The person gets an email with a link.
How to share a folder: exactly the same, right-click the folder → Share. Everything inside the folder is shared with those
permissions — present and future.
The three permission levels — this is the heart of the lesson:
Level What the person can do Use it when
Viewer Read and download only. Cannot change anything. Sending a final invoice, a CV, a finished flyer.
Commenter Read, and leave comments, but cannot edit the
content.
Asking a colleague for feedback on a draft.
Level What the person can do Use it when
Editor Read, comment, and change or delete the content. Working together on the same document in real
time.
The golden rule — least privilege. Give the lowest level of access that still lets the person do their job. If they only need to
read, give Viewer — never Editor. This protects you from accidental or careless changes.
Link sharing, and the danger of "Anyone with the link". When you click Share, there is also a section usually called General
access. It may say Restricted (only the people you list) or Anyone with the link. If you choose Anyone with the link, a warning
appears that the file can be opened by anyone who has the URL — even people outside the academy or outside Nigeria — and
they do not need to sign in.
Say this clearly and slowly:
"Listen well, because this causes real chaos. If you set a file to 'Anyone with the link' and you paste that link in a
WhatsApp group, you have just published your file to the whole internet. Anyone — a competitor, a scammer, a stranger
— can open your invoice, your customers' phone numbers, your bank statement. It does not matter that they were not
invited. The link is the key."
"So the rule is: use Restricted by default, and add people by email. Only ever use 'Anyone with the link' deliberately, for
something truly public like a flyer you want the whole world to see — and even then, think twice."
Worked example — sharing Adaeze's price list. Adaeze wants a customer, Mr. Okoro, to see her price list. She right-clicks
2026-10-09 - AdaezeFashion - Price-List - v1.pdf → Share → types mr.okoro@example.com → sets Viewer → Send. Mr. Okoro
can read the prices but cannot change them. If he needs to suggest a correction, she could switch him to Commenter instead.
She should not make him an Editor, because then he could accidentally alter her prices.
Common misconceptions:
"If I share as Editor, they can share it to others too." True — an Editor can often re-share, so be careful. When in doubt,
Viewer.
"If I remove the person, the file is safe." Removing access does stop new openings, but if the person already downloaded or
copied the content, you cannot take it back. Share deliberately the first time.
"Shared with me means it is my file." No — as in Section F.2, that file belongs to someone else. Copy it if you need it.
Limitation. Once a file is out, it is out. Cloud sharing is powerful but it removes the friction that used to protect you. Think
before you click Send.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Cloud storage = files on Google's computers, reachable from anywhere, safer than a single local machine.
The Drive screen has: My Drive, Shared with me, Recent, Starred, Trash, the + New button and the Search bar.
Build a shallow, numbered, predictable folder structure — no more than 2–3 levels.
Name every file with the YYYY-MM-DD - Project - Description - v1 convention.
Upload with + New → File upload or drag-and-drop, then use Move to to put files right.
Find with search operators like type:pdf , is:starred , owner:me ; use Starred and folder colours for quick visual access.
Share with Viewer / Commenter / Editor, always using least privilege. Beware "Anyone with the link".
Trash keeps deleted files for about 30 days — your safety net.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu runs Chinedu Electronics, a phone-accessory shop near Garrison in Port Harcourt. He has two staff and keeps
sales records, supplier invoices, warranty cards and customer contact lists. Until now, everything has lived on one office desktop
computer, with a weekly copy onto a flash drive that stays in the office drawer.
Relevant facts.
Last rainy season, water came through the roof and soaked the drawer. The flash drive survived but Chinedu panicked.
His accountant needs the monthly invoices, but Chinedu has to copy them to a flash drive and physically carry them across
town.
A customer once claimed a warranty, and Chinedu could not find the original receipt because it had been saved as
scan1.pdf and was lost among hundreds of files.
He has a smartphone with internet but no laptop of his own.
Decision point. Should Chinedu (a) keep doing what he is doing, (b) move everything to Google Drive with a proper folder
structure and naming, or (c) buy a second external hard drive and keep two local backups?
Possible approaches and trade-offs.
1. Stay local, add a second flash drive. Cheap and familiar, but both copies still live in the same shop, exposed to the same
fire, flood and theft. Finds are still slow because naming is chaotic. Trade-off: lowest cost, highest risk.
2. Move to Google Drive with structure and naming. Free, reachable from his phone, safe from shop disasters, and
shareable with the accountant by link. Requires a little learning and internet. Trade-off: small learning effort today, big daily
time savings and safety.
3. Two external drives kept in two locations. Safer than one, but still manual, still not shareable instantly, still requires
copying for the accountant, and still relies on nobody forgetting to update the backup. Trade-off: better than one drive, but
no access-anywhere and no easy sharing.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2, with a small nod to approach 3: move the working records to Google
Drive, organised as below, and keep one offline copy of the most critical financial records as a printed or external backup
elsewhere. This matches exactly the skills learned today.
```text
Chinedu Electronics
├── 01-Finance
│   ├── Invoices
│   └── Supplier-Receipts
├── 02-Customers
│   ├── Contacts
│   └── Warranty-Cards
├── 03-Products
│   ├── Price-Lists
│   └── Product-Photos
└── 04-Staff
```
Every file named YYYY-MM-DD - ChineduElectronics - Description - v1 . The 02-Customers/Contacts folder shared with the
accountant as Viewer when needed.
Trade-offs of the recommendation. Chinedu must have internet when he wants to open files (though offline access can be
enabled), and he must actually follow the naming rule. If he half-follows it, he is back where he started. The system only works if
it is used consistently.
Professional connection. This is exactly what an office administrator, a small-business manager or a personal assistant does
daily. The skill of turning a chaotic pile of files into a safe, findable, shareable drive is one of the most employable digital skills.
When an employer asks "are you organised?", this is what they mean — and now Chinedu (and the learner) can answer with a
system, not just a promise.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this as the session's live troubleshooting guide. When a learner is stuck, do not give the answer immediately — point them
to the relevant row and ask them to check.
K.1 Everything is dumped in My Drive with no folders
Symptoms: Dozens of loose files at the top level; nothing is grouped. Likely cause: Learner uploads without first opening the
destination folder, or does not believe folders are necessary. How to investigate: Look at the top level; count the loose files.
Corrective steps: Create the folder structure, then select several files (Ctrl-click), right-click → Move to the folder. How to
confirm resolved: The top level shows only a few folders, not files. Prevention: The habit "open the folder before you upload".
K.2 Bad file names — Document1 , Untitled , final_final
Symptoms: Files that mean nothing without opening them. Likely cause: No naming convention applied; learner in a hurry.
Corrective steps: Right-click each file → Rename into YYYY-MM-DD - Project - Description - v1 . Prevention: Rename at the
moment of creation; keep the cheat sheet visible.
K.3 Over-sharing: everything set to Editor or "Anyone with the link"
Symptoms: A recipient can delete files; or a link is open to the whole internet. Likely cause: Default role left as Editor; General
access left as "Anyone with the link". How to investigate: Open Share on the file/folder and read the role and General access
setting. Corrective steps: Change role to Viewer or Commenter; set General access back to Restricted; remove any
unintended people. How to confirm resolved: The Share panel shows only the intended people, at the correct roles, with
General access "Restricted". Prevention: The least-privilege rule; the Visual warning is there for a reason — read it.
K.4 "I cannot find my file"
Symptoms: A learner insists a file exists but cannot locate it. Likely causes: (a) It uploaded to the wrong folder. (b) It is still
uploading. (c) It was never uploaded. (d) It was moved to Trash. How to investigate: Click Recent; check Trash; use search
title: ; check the upload progress area. Corrective steps: Use Move to to put it right; Restore from Trash if needed; re-
upload if it never arrived. Prevention: Confirm "uploads complete"; file immediately after upload.
K.5 The recipient did not receive the share email
Symptoms: You shared a file but the person says they got nothing. Likely causes: (a) Wrong email typed. (b) The email went to
their Spam folder. (c) The person is signed into a different Google account. How to investigate: Re-open Share and check the
exact email; ask them to check Spam; ask which account they signed in with. Corrective steps: Correct the email, resend; ask
them to search "in:anywhere" in Gmail or check Spam. Prevention: Double-check the address before clicking Send.
K.6 Duplicate files everywhere
Symptoms: The same invoice appears three times. Likely cause: Re-uploading instead of moving or copying. Corrective steps:
Keep one master; delete the duplicates (they go to Trash); use Make a copy only when you genuinely need a new independent
copy. Prevention: Search before you upload.
K.7 Storage is full
Symptoms: A message says storage is full; uploads fail. Likely cause: Too many large videos/ photos, or a full Gmail, filling the
shared 15 GB. How to investigate: Click Storage in the left menu to see what is using the space. Corrective steps: Empty the
Trash (it counts toward storage); delete large unwanted files; or upgrade to a paid plan if genuinely needed. Prevention: Keep
media files lean; empty the Trash monthly.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: For every row above, the likely cause becomes a confirmed cause only after you check it —
see the exact role in the Share panel, see the file in Trash, see the upload progress. Teach learners to confirm before they fix, so
they solve the real problem, not a guessed one.

(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key that follows.)
Q1. Short answer. In one or two sentences, what is cloud storage, and how is it different from storing files only on your own

Q2. Multiple choice. Which permission lets a person comment on but not edit your document? a) Viewer b) Commenter c)

Q5. Error detection. A learner shares a file and chooses Anyone with the link, then posts the link in a busy public WhatsApp
group. What is the danger, and how should they fix it?
Q6. Scenario. Adaeze needs to find all PDF invoices she created in September 2026. Write a Drive search that would help, and
name one other way she could narrow the search without typing an operator.
Q7. Short answer. How long does Google Drive keep a deleted file in the Trash, and how would you recover it?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. Cloud storage means files are saved on Google's computers (servers) and reached over the internet, so they are accessible
from any device and survive if one computer is lost or damaged. Local storage keeps files on one machine only, where they are
lost if it is stolen or breaks, and are hard to share.
A2. (b) Commenter.
A3. Shallow (2–3 levels) keeps navigation fast; numbered prefixes keep folders in a chosen order; predictability means anyone
can guess where a file belongs. Ignoring it leads to a maze of folders or a pile of loose files, so finding a file takes minutes
instead of seconds.
A4. a) Viewer; b) Commenter; c) Editor.
A5. The danger is that anyone who has the link — including strangers, competitors and scammers — can open the file; the
link effectively publishes it. To fix it: open Share, change General access back to Restricted, then share explicitly by email to
the intended people at the right permission level. (Note: if someone already downloaded it, you cannot fully take it back —
hence foresight matters.)
A6. A search such as title:Invoice type:pdf modified:2026-09 (accept type:pdf before:2026-10-01 after:2026-08-31 or similar).
Alternative: click the filter icon at the right of the search bar and choose Type = PDF and a Modified date range in September
2026.
A7. About 30 days. Recover it by opening Trash in the left menu, right-clicking the file and choosing Restore.

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned that your files do not have to live dangerously on one computer. You met Google
Drive, free cloud storage tied to your Gmail account, and you learned the five parts of the screen that matter: My Drive, Shared
with me, Recent, Starred and Trash. You built a shallow, numbered folder structure for a fictional business, gave files clean
names with the YYYY-MM-DD - Project - Description - v1 convention, uploaded and moved 20 files into place, shared a folder
with the correct permission, learned the danger of "Anyone with the link", and found files quickly with search. You also learned
that a deleted file lives in the Trash for about 30 days before it is gone for good.
Five important takeaways:
1. Cloud storage keeps your files on Google's computers so they survive theft, fire and computer failure — and are reachable
anywhere.
2. A good folder structure is shallow, numbered and predictable; the top level should hold a few big ideas, not dozens of loose
files.
3. Name every file YYYY-MM-DD - Project - Description - v1 — never Document1 or final_final .
4. Share with least privilege: Viewer to read, Commenter to review, Editor to co-edit. Keep General access on Restricted.
5. Use search ( type:pdf , owner:me , is:starred ) and the Starred list to find anything in under 30 seconds.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner opens drive.google.com and names the five main parts without prompting.
The learner creates a folder structure with logic they can explain ("this folder is for money matters").
The learner renames a Document1 file into the convention on the first try.
The learner chooses Viewer for a read-only share and can say why not Editor.
The learner finds a file by search in under 30 seconds.
The learner reacts to the "Anyone with the link" warning with caution rather than confusion.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Files in Shared with me are backed up in my Drive." → They are not; make a copy if you need them.
"Uploading moves the file off my computer." → It copies; the original stays.
"Editor is the friendliest option, so I always use it." → Use least privilege; Editor can change or delete.
"If the internet is off, my files are lost." → They are safe on Google's servers; you just cannot reach them right now.
"The date in the name is when I made the file." → It is usually more useful as the date the file is about or is due; agree on a
meaning.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Sort-the-files drill: give a printed list of 10 files and 4 folders; ask the learner to draw lines showing where each file goes.
2. Name-fixer drill: give 8 bad file names; ask the learner to rewrite each in the convention.
3. Permission-sorting drill: give 10 sharing situations; ask which permission (Viewer/Commenter/Editor) is right for each.
4. Search drill: give three "find this file" tasks and have the learner write the search operator for each.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the folder structure on a printed card and a partially completed Drive; the learner only uploads
and moves the files and renames five.
Level 2 (standard): the exercise as written.
Level 3 (challenge): Add colour-coding to the top folders by purpose, set up offline access for one folder, and write a one-
page "Drive Use Policy" for a fictional business explaining the naming rule and the sharing rule.
Timing guidance: If the room is slow or the Wi-Fi is weak, reduce the file upload to 10 files (keep the structure and naming),
shorten the case study (Section J) to a 2-minute mention, and protect the guided practical (Section H) — the practical is the
priority. Do not sacrifice Section F.7 (sharing security): that is the single most important safety lesson in the session.
