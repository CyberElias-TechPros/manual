# Task Management

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 3: Task Management and Workflow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3. The learner should already be able to create a Google Drive folder, find a file, use Gmail labels, and write in Google Docs. If a learner cannot sign into Gmail or open Google Tasks, run the 10-minute login refresher in Section E before continuing.

**Connection to previous work:** In Week 1 learners organised what they already had — files in Drive, email in Gmail, documents in Docs and Sheets. But organising what you have is only half of productivity. This session organises what you still need to do — your promises, deadlines and commitments — so that nothing important is forgotten.

**Preparation before class:** Academy PCs on and logged into Gmail; Google Tasks reachable at tasks.google.com and through the Gmail right-side panel; projector ready; printed Eisenhower matrix handout; printed "Task Anatomy" cheat sheet; a set of scenario cards for the guided and independent exercises.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in their own words why human memory and paper notebooks lose commitments, and why a digital task list
prevents this.
2. Distinguish a task from a project, and give three examples of each, using a Nigerian small-business context.
3. Open Google Tasks in two ways — as a full page ( tasks.google.com ) and as the Gmail side panel — and describe the main
parts of the screen.
4. Create a task with a clear title, written details (notes), a due date and a due time.
5. Break a large task into subtasks so that a big job becomes a short, doable checklist.
6. Prioritise using the star, the "Most Important Tasks" (MIT) method, and the Eisenhower matrix.
7. Organise tasks into lists that represent projects or contexts (for example Shop , School , Personal ).
8. Perform a daily review and explain what the weekly review (the "weekly review ritual") adds.
9. Explain how Google Tasks keeps tasks in sync across a phone, a laptop and the academy desktop.
10. Convert a messy scenario (a list of scattered promises) into a clean, organised task system with dates and priorities.

D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes) — "The Promise on Scrap Paper"
Instructor setup: On the projector, hold up (or show on screen) a small torn piece of paper with rough writing on it. Do not
open any app yet.
What the instructor says:
"Look at this paper. On it is written: 'Give Ngozi the wedding dress Friday. Collect ₦15,000 balance.' Adaeze wrote this on
Friday morning at her shop in Rumuola. By afternoon she was busy with three customers. On Saturday she found the
paper inside a folded newspaper and threw the newspaper away. Ngozi never got her dress on Friday. Adaeze lost
₦15,000 and, worse, lost a customer who now tells people, 'That tailor is not reliable.'
Now think about yourself. Where do you keep the things you must not forget — the school assignment due Thursday, the
church meeting on Saturday, the money you promised to pay? A notebook? A phone note? Your head? Today we replace all
of that with one free tool that remembers everything and even reminds you on the right day."
The hook (do this live): Open tasks.google.com on the projector and type one task:
Title: Give Ngozi the wedding dress
Date: the coming Friday Say: "Same promise. But now it can never be lost in a newspaper, and on Friday morning it will
appear at the top of my list."
Expected learner response: Learners may say "I use my phone notes", "I write it in my exercise book", or "I just remember it".
Accept all answers. Point out gently that phone notes and exercise books are a good start, but they do not put the item in front
of you on the correct day, and they do not tell you what to do first. Confirm that today we add dates, priorities and reminders.
Why this opener works: It starts with a small, familiar, painful story (a lost customer) rather than a definition, and it gives an
instant taste of the tool solving the exact problem — so the class begins with doing, not listening.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this quickly as an oral round-robin. Put the four questions on the projector.
Question 1: "In Google Drive, what does a good folder structure look like — deep or shallow?"
Expected answer: shallow — about two to three levels, with a predictable naming pattern (for example 01-Admin , 02-
Designs ).
Common gap: learners create many nested folders. Remind them: if you have to click four times to reach a file, the
structure is too deep.
Question 2: "In Gmail, what is the difference between a label and a folder?"
Expected answer: Gmail uses labels (like tags), and one email can carry more than one label. A traditional folder holds a file
in only one place.
Common gap: learners still picture folders. Re-state: labels let one message belong to many categories at once.
Question 3: "In Google Docs, where does your work go while you type — do you press Save?"
Expected answer: no; Docs saves automatically to the cloud, and version history lets you recover older versions.
Common gap: learners think they must save manually. Clarify that auto-save is on by default.
Question 4: "In Google Sheets, which formula adds up the numbers in cells B2 to B10 ?"
Expected answer: =SUM(B2:B10) .
Common gap: learners forget the = sign at the start. Remind them every formula begins with = .
Bridge to today (say this):
"Week 1 was about putting your things in order — files, emails, documents. Week 2 is about putting your time in order. A
tidy Drive is useless if you still forget to send the invoice that was due yesterday. Today we build the list that remembers for
you."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 8 extra minutes reviewing the Drive folder pattern before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why memory and paper fail
The idea. Every promise you make — to a customer, a lecturer, your church, your family — is an "open loop". It is something
you have started in your mind but not yet finished. Open loops are heavy: they keep pulling at your attention.
Why it matters. The human brain is excellent at thinking and terrible at storing lists. Research on memory (and everyday
experience) shows that a person can hold only a handful of items in mind at once. When you try to hold twenty promises, you
do not remember all of them — you remember the loudest one and forget the rest.
Plain-English definition. A task is a single, specific thing you must do. Task management is the habit of writing every task
down immediately, organising it, and reviewing it so that you act on the right things at the right time.
Real-life comparison. Your brain is a general, not a filing cabinet. It should be used for doing and deciding, not for storing
hundreds of small notes. Writing a task down is like giving a heavy bag to a porter — your hands are free again.
The three ways people lose commitments:
1. Memory — you simply forget.
2. Paper — the notebook is at home, or the page is lost, or the writing is unclear.
3. Chaos — the note exists but lives in one of five places (phone notes, a WhatsApp message to yourself, the back of a
receipt), so you cannot find it when you need it.
A digital task list fixes all three because it is always with you, searchable, and able to put the right item in front of you on the
right day.
F.2 Task versus project — the most important distinction
Many beginners fail at task management because they mix two different things in the same list.
A task is a single action that one person can finish in one sitting. It starts with a verb.
```text
Call Ngozi about the dress fitting
```
```text
Pay NEPA bill
```
```text
Email the invoice to Chinedu Electronics
```
A project is an outcome that needs several tasks to complete. A project is a container of tasks.
Project: "Launch the October collection" — contains tasks such as Buy 10 yards of Ankara , Design the flyer , Sew sample
dress , Photograph samples , Post on WhatsApp status .
Why the difference matters. You cannot "do" a project in one action — you can only do the next task. Beginners write a
project as if it were a task: Prepare October collection . That line sits in the list forever because it never feels "doable". The fix is to
break the project into tasks.
Task Project
Size One action Many actions
Example
```text
Text the tailor to confirm pickup
```
"Produce the October collection"
Lives in Google Tasks as a task in a list a list (the list is the project)
Test Can I start and finish it now? Do I need several steps?
A useful rule: if you are not sure whether something is a task or a project, ask "Can I finish this in one go?" If no, it is a project
— turn it into a list and add the first task.
F.3 Why a digital task list beats memory and notebooks
A notebook is not evil — it is a good first step and many successful people use one. But a digital list has advantages a notebook
cannot match:
Feature Notebook Google Tasks
Always with you Only if you carry it Yes — phone, laptop, anywhere you sign in
Reminder on the due day No, unless you check every morning Yes — appears at the top on the day
Search Slow, page by page Instant
Reorder by urgency Must rewrite the page Drag, or sort by date
Sync One copy only Same list on every device
Lost or damaged Gone Safe on Google's servers
Break into subtasks Rewrite Built-in subtasks
Say this to the class:
"A notebook is a record of what you wrote. A digital task list is a tool that tells you what to do today. Those are different
jobs."
Important limitation (do not over-promise). Google Tasks is simple by design. It does not do complex project scheduling,
Gantt charts or team assignments. For a small business, a church office or a student, simple is usually better — but be honest
that a large company with fifty staff would need a heavier tool.
F.4 Google Tasks versus Google Keep — and why we choose Tasks
Both are free with a Gmail account, but they are built for different jobs.
Google Keep is a note app. It is for information you want to keep and read: ideas, meeting notes, a shopping list, a recipe, a
photo of a receipt, a voice note, a sketch. It has colours, labels, and reminders, and you can copy a note into a Google Doc. It is
excellent for capturing information.
Google Tasks is a to-do app. It is for actions you must complete. It has task lists, due dates, due times, notes on a task, and
subtasks. It is built to answer the question "what do I do next?"
Recommendation for this course: use Google Tasks as your main task system, and use Google Keep only when you need to
store a note, an idea or a shopping list that is not really an action with a deadline.
Common misconception. Learners try to run their whole life inside Keep checklists. That works for shopping but breaks down
for deadlines because Keep is not built around due dates the way Tasks is. Keep the two tools in their proper lanes:
Question Use
"I must DO this by a date" Google Tasks
"I must REMEMBER this information" Google Keep
F.5 Getting around Google Tasks
There are two ways to open Google Tasks, and learners should know both:
1. As a full page — type tasks.google.com in Chrome, or in Gmail click the Tasks icon (a small checklist) in the far-right side
panel.
2. As a side panel in Gmail, Google Calendar or Google Docs — click the Tasks icon on the right edge, or click the blue-and-
yellow checklist symbol to open it beside your email.
Main parts of the screen (wording may vary slightly by version):
Task lists column (left): shows your lists. There is a default list called My Tasks. To add a new list, click Create new list (a
+ sign) at the bottom of the column and type a name.
Main list area (centre): shows the tasks in the selected list.
Add a task box (top of the list): the box that says Add a task. Type a title and press Enter.
The circle (checkbox): to the left of every task. Click it to mark the task complete; the task moves to the Completed section
at the bottom.
The star: to the right of each task title. Click it to mark an important task. This is how you prioritise in Google Tasks.
Task details panel (right): opens when you click a task. Here you find Add details (notes), the date/time button, and Add
subtasks.
Say this to the class:
"Think of a list as a folder for actions. My Tasks is the drawer, and each list inside it — Shop , School , Personal — is a
labelled section. A task lives in exactly one list, but you can move it any time."
F.6 Creating a task the right way — title, details, date, subtasks
A good task title has four parts. Use the "Task Anatomy" rule:
Verb + Object + (Due date) + (Context)
Bad task Why it fails Good task
```text
shop
```
No verb, no object, no date
```text
Buy 10 Ankara bundles from Mile 1 market — Friday 4pm
```
```text
invoice
```
Which invoice? For whom?
```text
Email October invoice to Chinedu Electronics — Tuesday
```
```text
call
```
Call who, about what?
```text
Call Mama Ngozi to confirm cake order — today 2pm
```
```text
assignment
```
Which subject, when due?
```text
Submit Eco 101 assignment — Thursday 9am
```
How to create a task with full details (exact steps):
1. Open Google Tasks at tasks.google.com .
2. Make sure the correct list is selected on the left (for example Shop ).
3. Click Add a task, type the title Buy 10 Ankara bundles from Mile 1 market , and press Enter.
4. Click on the task text to open the details panel on the right.
5. Under the title, click Add details and type the note: Budget ₦50,000. Ask for the blue print. Collect receipt.
6. Click the date/time button. A small calendar opens — click the due date, for example Friday. To add a time, click Add
time and choose 4:00 PM . Close the calendar.
7. Click Add subtasks and type each smaller step, pressing Enter after each: Withdraw ₦50,000 , Confirm market opens , Take a
photo of the print .
8. Click outside the panel to close it. The task now shows the date under its title.
Worked example — breaking down a big job:
Project: "Prepare October Collection" (this is a list). Tasks inside it:
Buy Ankara fabric — subtasks: Write fabric list , Withdraw money , Go to Mile 1 market , Confirm quality .
Design flyer — subtasks: Choose colours , Draft in Canva , Get feedback , Export image .
Sew sample dress — subtasks: Take Adaeze's measurements , Cut fabric , Sew , Iron and finish .
Market the collection — subtasks: Post on WhatsApp status , Send to customer list , Take orders .
Say this to the class:
"A subtask is a big task that has been cut into small pieces. When you look at a subtask, you should feel 'I can do that now'
— not 'this is too big'."
Important limitation. Google Tasks shows only about one level of subtasks. If a step needs its own sub-steps, make it a new
task or a new list.
F.7 Priorities — how to decide what to do first
Here is a fact learners must accept: Google Tasks does not have a numbered "high/medium/low" priority field. Many
beginners look for one and give up. Instead we create priority using three simple tools.
Tool 1 — The star. Tap the star beside a task to mark it important. You can sort the list so starred items appear together.
Tool 2 — The Most Important Tasks (MIT) method. Each day, choose at most three tasks as your MITs. Star them, and do
them before anything else. This is powerful because it stops a long list from paralysing you.
Tool 3 — The Eisenhower matrix. Decide by asking two questions: Is it important? and Is it urgent?
Urgent Not urgent
Important Do it now (customer order due today) Schedule it (learn a new skill, plan the month)
Not important Delegate / do quickly (some phone calls, admin) Delete or park it (aimless scrolling, low-value meetings)
Say this to the class:
"Most people spend their whole day on things that are urgent but not important — other people's emergencies. The matrix
helps you protect time for the important-but-not-urgent work that actually grows your business."
Worked example — Adaeze's Tuesday:
Task Important? Urgent? Decision
```text
Deliver Ngozi's dress today
```
Yes Yes Do now
```text
Learn to use Google Sheets for invoices
```
Yes No Schedule Thursday 4pm
```text
Reply to a supplier's promotional WhatsApp
```
No Yes Do quickly, 2 minutes
```text
Reorganise all old receipts
```
No No Park in Later list
Common misconception. Learners star everything. If every task is important, nothing is important. Guide them to a maximum
of three stars per day.
F.8 Organising tasks by project, list and context
A long flat list of fifty tasks is almost as bad as no list. We organise so the right tasks appear at the right time.
By project. Create a list for each big project: October Collection , Client Orders , Church Programme .
By context. Create lists for where or how you do the work: At Shop , At Computer , Phone Calls , Errands (things to do when
you are out).
By area of life. For a student or a busy worker, School , Work , Personal , Church keeps the lists short and clear.
Recommended setup for this course (keep it to three to five lists):
Today — the tasks you will do today (moved here in your morning review).
Shop (or your business/project name)
School (or Work )
```text
Personal
```
Later — ideas and tasks with no date yet, parked here so they do not clutter your active lists.
How to move a task between lists: drag the task onto the list name on the left, or click the task, open the details panel, and
use the list selector. Wording may vary slightly by version.
The Today list is the most powerful idea in this session. Every morning, you look at all your lists and move the handful of
tasks you will actually do today into Today . Whatever is in Today is your real plan.
F.9 The "capture everything, first" habit
The idea. When a promise, idea or task appears, you must catch it immediately — before it escapes. This is called capture. The
rule is:
Capture first. Organise later.
When a customer calls with an order, do not stop to decide which list it belongs to. Just capture it: open Google Tasks, type the
title, press Enter. Later, in your review, you move it to the correct list with the right date.
Why it works. If you wait to "organise it properly", you will forget it. Capture is a net; organising is sorting the catch. A cheap,
fast net catches more than a slow, careful one.
Real-life comparison. A fisherman does not sort each fish as it lands. He catches them all in the net first, then sorts them on
shore. Capture is the net.
Where to capture:
Google Tasks on your phone (fastest).
The Tasks side panel inside Gmail — you can even drag an email into the Tasks panel to turn it into a task, or use the "Add
to Tasks" option in the email toolbar (wording may vary). This is a favourite trick: an email that needs action becomes a task
in one second.
Common misconception. Learners think capturing "messily" is bad. It is not — an ugly task in the right app beats a perfect task
still in your head.
F.10 The daily review and the weekly review ritual
A task list only works if you look at it. Two reviews keep the system alive.
The daily review (about 5 minutes, every morning):
1. Open Google Calendar and check what is fixed for today (meetings, appointments).
2. Open Google Tasks and look through your lists.
3. Choose your three MITs for the day and star them.
4. Move today's tasks into the Today list.
5. Reschedule anything that cannot be done today to a later date, or move it to Later .
6. Clear the Completed section so the list looks fresh.
The weekly review ritual (about 30 minutes, same day each week — many people use Friday afternoon or Sunday
evening):
1. Go through every list and ask of each task: Is this still needed? Delete the stale ones.
2. Add any tasks you captured during the week but never organised.
3. Check next week's calendar and create tasks for anything coming.
4. Review each project list and decide the next action for each project.
5. Update due dates so nothing important is quietly overdue.
6. Empty the Later list of anything that is now ready to schedule.
Say this to the class:
"The daily review keeps you honest for one day. The weekly review keeps your whole system healthy. Skip the weekly
review for three weeks and your list becomes a graveyard — full of old tasks you no longer believe in."
A metaphor: the daily review is brushing your teeth; the weekly review is going to the dentist. Both are needed, at different
frequencies.
F.11 Keeping tasks synced across devices
Google Tasks is a cloud tool, like Drive and Gmail. This means:
Sign into the same Google account on your phone, your laptop and the academy desktop, and you see the same tasks
everywhere.
Add a task on your phone at the market — it appears on your computer when you sit down.
Changes sync automatically when you are online. If the phone is offline, you can still tick a task, and it will sync when the
internet returns.
The phone app can also show reminders at the task's due time (based on your phone's notification settings).
Important caution. Everything is tied to your Google account. If you sign into a different account, you will see a different,
empty set of tasks. If a learner's tasks "disappear", the first thing to check is: am I signed into the right account?
F.12 Summary of the session
Memory and paper lose commitments; a digital task list remembers, reminds and searches for you.
A task is one action; a project is many tasks. In Google Tasks, a project is a list.
Use Google Tasks for actions and Google Keep for notes.
A good task title = verb + object + due date + context.
Break big work into subtasks.
Prioritise with the star, the MIT method (three per day) and the Eisenhower matrix.
Capture everything first, organise later.
Run a daily review (5 minutes) and a weekly review (~30 minutes).
Tasks sync through your Google account — always check you are signed in correctly.

J. REAL-WORLD CASE STUDY — "The Salon That Forgot Appointments"
Situation. Blessing runs a small hair salon near Rumuokoro, Port Harcourt. She takes appointments on WhatsApp and
"remembers" them. Last month she double-booked two bridal parties on the same Saturday, and forgot a regular customer who
then went to another salon. She is losing both money and trust. She is not lazy — she is busy, and she keeps everything in her
head.
Relevant facts.
Blessing handles 15–20 appointments a week, plus stock, rent and staff pay.
She has a smartphone with internet.
She does not use any task or calendar app.
Her busiest and most profitable jobs (bridal) need the most lead time and are the easiest to forget.
Decision point. How can a simple, free task system prevent double-booking and forgotten customers without slowing her
down when a customer is standing in front of her?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep remembering, but write appointments in a paper diary.
2. Use Google Tasks with a Today list, a Bridal list and a nightly review.
3. Pay for a full salon booking software.
Recommended solution (for this stage). Approach 2. When a customer books, Blessing captures the task in seconds on her
phone: Bridal - Chisom - Sat 10am (deposit paid) . Her nightly five-minute review catches conflicts, and the next morning's
Today list tells her exactly who is coming. She can also use Google Calendar for fixed appointment times.
Example capture lines:
```text
Bridal - Chisom - Sat 10am - deposit ₦5,000 paid
```
```text
Wash and set - Ada - Wed 2pm
```
```text
Order relaxer stock - Friday
```
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 is free but relies on her carrying the diary and remembering to read it; it failed once already.
Approach 2 takes about ten minutes to set up and five minutes a night, removes double-booking, and costs nothing.
Approach 3 is overkill and expensive for a one-person salon; it may be worth it only if she hires staff and opens a second
branch.
Professional connection. Receptionists, administrators and project assistants are paid precisely because they keep systems that
stop a business forgetting things. The skill taught today is the same skill that earns a salary in an office — written tasks, dates
and a review routine.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live troubleshooting guide. When a learner hits an issue, do not give the answer immediately — ask them
to check the relevant row.
K.1 "My tasks have disappeared!"
Symptoms: The learner opens Google Tasks and sees an empty list, or a different set of tasks. Likely causes: (a) They are signed
into the wrong Google account. (b) They are looking at a different list that is genuinely empty. How to investigate: Click the
account circle (top-right) and confirm the email address. Look at the left column to see which list is selected. Safe corrective
steps: Switch to the correct account, or click the correct list. How to confirm resolved: The expected tasks reappear.
Prevention: Always sign into the same account on every device.
K.2 "Everything is marked urgent, I still don't know what to do."
Symptoms: Ten starred tasks; the learner feels paralysed. Likely cause: Over-starring. Star lost its meaning. Corrective steps:
Un-star all but the three MITs for today. Prevention: Rule of three — maximum three stars per day.
K.3 "The task never gets done because it's too big."
Symptoms: A task has been moved forward for two weeks unchanged. Likely cause: It is really a project, not a task. How to
investigate: Ask "Can I finish this in one action?" If not, it is a project. Corrective steps: Make it a list, then add the first small
task. Prevention: Apply the verb + object test when creating every task.
K.4 "The task has no date and keeps sinking."
Symptoms: A task is old but still not done, with no due date. Likely cause: No date, so it never rises to the top. Corrective
steps: Give it a real date, or move it to Later if it is truly not urgent. Prevention: Every active task gets a date; undated ideas
live in Later .
K.5 "I captured the idea but never did anything with it."
Symptoms: The Later list fills up with items that never progress. Likely cause: No weekly review. Corrective steps: Do a
weekly review and either date each item or delete it. Prevention: Protect the weekly review as a fixed appointment in your
calendar.
K.6 "Tasks on my phone are different from my computer."
Symptoms: Two different lists on two devices. Likely cause: Different Google accounts on each device, or the device is offline.
Corrective steps: Sign into the same account everywhere; connect to the internet and wait for sync. Prevention: Use one
Google account for all work.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: In every case, the likely cause becomes a confirmed cause only after you check it (look at the
account email, look at the list name). Teach learners to confirm before fixing.

INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q8)
A1. A task is a single action that can be finished in one sitting; a project is an outcome made up of several tasks. A2. (b) — it
has a verb, an object, a quantity and a date. A3. False. Google Tasks has no numeric priority field; you use the star, the MIT

actions with due dates and subtasks, so it drives "what do I do next" for deadlines. Keep is built for storing and reading
information (ideas, lists, images), so it is the right home for notes that are not actions with dates. A6. Verb; object; date; context
(where/how). A7. Check that the laptop is signed into the same Google account as before. Google Tasks lives in the cloud
behind the account, so the wrong account shows an empty list. A8. (1) Check the calendar; (2) open Tasks and review lists; (3)
choose and star three MITs; (4) move today's tasks into Today ; (5) reschedule anything not doable today; (6) clear the
Completed section.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
The learner distinguishes a task from a project without prompting.
Task titles contain a verb, an object and (usually) a date.
The learner stars only a few tasks and can say why.

Level 1 (supported): provide a partially filled Google Tasks account with lists already created and three tasks filled in; the
