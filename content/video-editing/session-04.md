# Audio Editing and Mixing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Craft

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first audio work)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3 — learners must have their rough cut (from Session 3) with deliberate cuts and a clear story. They must be able to split, trim and reorder in CapCut. No prior audio knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 3 you built a rough cut with deliberate cuts, good pacing and a clear story. Today you make it sound right: you learn relative levels, ducking music under voice, noise reduction, recording a clean voiceover, syncing cuts to a beat, and loudness. The rule you learn first: audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad sound.

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and tested; headphones or earphones for every learner (essential — this is an audio session); Pack C (Event and Music) copied to the shared class folder and verified; a free music track from an approved source (YouTube Audio Library, Pixabay or Mixkit) downloaded and ready; a short voiceover script prepared; the instructor's own audio pass pre built to show the finished result; a simple loudness meter or the platform's loudness normalisation explained. Instructor note on tone: This session changes how learners hear video. Some will realise for the first time that the music in their old videos was louder than the speaking. That realisation is the lesson. Be patient — audio is invisible, so it takes repetition to build the ear. Use the phrase "the voice is the boss" early and often.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why audio matters more than picture for watch-through, and state the rule: "audiences forgive a soft picture, but
they will not watch bad sound."
2. Set relative levels for dialogue, music and effects so the voice is clearly above the music and the music supports rather
than fights.
3. Duck music under voice — lowering the music volume automatically or manually when someone is speaking.
4. Apply noise reduction to a clip with light room noise, and explain when it helps and when it hurts.
5. Record a clean voiceover using a phone or computer, with a simple script and a quiet room.
6. Sync cuts to a music beat and explain why beat-sync makes a video feel professional.
7. Check loudness by meter and by ear, and state what platforms expect.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, play a 30-second video twice — once with the audio mixed badly (music loud, voice
buried, room noise hissing), and once with the audio mixed well (voice clear, music supportive, noise reduced). Use the same
video both times.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Same video, same picture, same cuts. The first version is hard to watch — the music is shouting over the
voice, and there is a hiss in the background. The second version is easy to watch — the voice is clear, the music supports,
and the noise is gone.
Here is the rule that will change how you edit forever: audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad
sound. A slightly blurry video with great audio keeps people watching. A beautiful video with bad audio loses them in ten
seconds.
Today you learn to make video sound right. By the end of this session, you will have done a full audio pass on your rough
cut — and you will hear the difference."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "the first one is annoying," "I can't hear the person," "I didn't know you could fix
that." Accept all answers. Say: "You already have the ear — you can hear the difference. Today we give that ear a method."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"Audio is invisible, which is why beginners ignore it. But the audience feels it. Today you learn the five audio skills: levels,
ducking, noise reduction, voiceover and beat-sync. And you learn the loudness rule that platforms expect."
Why this opener works: It isolates audio as the variable (same video, different sound), it proves learners already have the ear to
hear bad audio, and it frames the session as giving that ear a method.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a J-cut, and why does it make dialogue feel natural?"
Expected answer: A J-cut is when the audio of the next clip starts before its picture — you hear the next speaker before you
see them. It makes dialogue feel natural because it mimics how we actually hear conversations.
Common gap: learners remember the name but not the reason. Clarify: "The technique is simple; the reason is what makes
it professional."
Question 2: "What are the four parts of the short-video story shape?"
Expected answer: Hook, beginning, middle, payoff.
Common gap: learners confuse the story shape with the cut types. Clarify: "Story shape is the structure; cut types are the
tools."
Question 3: "What is B-roll, and what are two things it does?"
Expected answer: B-roll is supplementary footage (cutaways of hands, places, objects). It covers jump cuts and adds
meaning/interest.
Common gap: learners think B-roll is just filler. Clarify: "B-roll with purpose covers jumps and shows what the voice
describes."
Question 4: "Why did we use straight cuts instead of transitions in Session 3?"
Expected answer: Because straight cuts are invisible — they let the story flow without drawing attention to the edit.
Transitions draw attention to themselves.
Common gap: learners still want to use transitions. Clarify: "Transitions are for special moments; straight cuts are the
default."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have a rough cut with good cuts and a clear story. Now we make it sound right. The voice is the boss — everything
else supports it. Today you learn to mix audio so the voice is clear, the music supports, and the noise is gone."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing the J-cut on the projector before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why audio matters more than picture
The idea. Audiences will watch a slightly soft or dark video if the sound is good. They will not watch a beautiful video if the
sound is bad. Audio is the invisible foundation of watch-through.
Plain-English definition. Audio mixing is the process of balancing all the sounds in a video — dialogue, music, effects, room
noise — so the voice is clear and everything supports the story.
Why it matters:
The voice carries the message. If the viewer cannot hear the speaker, the video has failed — no matter how good the
picture is.
Bad audio is physically uncomfortable. Loud music over quiet speech makes the viewer strain. Hissing noise makes them
click away.
Good audio is invisible. When the mix is right, the viewer does not notice the sound — they simply experience the story.
The rule (say it with the class):
"Audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad sound."
Real-life comparison. Audio is like the seasoning in food. You do not see it, but if it is wrong — too much salt, no spice —
the meal fails. A simple meal with good seasoning beats a fancy meal with bad seasoning. A simple video with good audio
beats a beautiful video with bad audio.
Common misconception. "Audio is the sound engineer's job." No. In small productions — and in most freelance work — the
editor is the sound person. You do not need to be a studio engineer; you need to know the five skills in this session.
F.2 Relative levels — the voice is the boss
The idea. Relative levels means setting the volume of each audio element in relation to the others — not just making
everything loud.
Plain-English definition. A level is how loud something is. Relative levels means the voice is the loudest, the music is lower,
and the effects are balanced — so the voice is always clear.
The level hierarchy (teach this order):
Element Job Relative level
Dialogue / voice Carries the message Loudest — the boss
Music Sets the mood Lower — supports the voice
Sound effects Adds detail Balanced — never fights the voice
Room noise / hiss Unwanted Reduced or removed
The practical levels (a starting point, not a rule):
Dialogue: the main voice sits around -12 dB to -6 dB on the meter (CapCut shows this in the audio settings).
Music under voice: around -20 dB to -15 dB — clearly audible but clearly below the voice.
Music without voice: can come up to -12 dB to -6 dB for emotional moments.
How to set levels in CapCut:
1. Select the audio clip on the timeline.
2. In the audio tools, find the volume slider.
3. Adjust while watching the meter and listening on headphones.
4. The goal: the voice is always clearly above the music.
Real-life comparison. Levels are like a conversation in a noisy market. The person speaking (the voice) talks loudly enough to
be heard. The background noise (music) is there, but it does not drown the speaker. If the background noise gets too loud, the
speaker has to shout — and the listener leaves.
Common misconception. "Louder is better." No. A video where everything is loud is exhausting. The goal is balance — the
voice clear, the music present but supportive.
F.3 Ducking — music under voice
The idea. Ducking means automatically lowering the music volume when someone is speaking, and raising it when they stop.
Plain-English definition. Ducking is a technique where the music "ducks" (dips) under the dialogue so the voice is always clear
— then comes back up when the voice stops.
Why it exists. Without ducking, the music and voice compete — the viewer has to strain to hear over the music. With ducking,
the music supports the voice without fighting it.
How to duck in CapCut (two methods):
Method 1 — Automatic ducking (if available):
1. Select the music clip on the timeline.
2. In the audio tools, look for "Ducking" or "Auto ducking".
3. Set the ducking amount (how far the music dips) and the threshold (when it dips).
4. CapCut lowers the music automatically when dialogue is present.
Method 2 — Manual ducking (the reliable way):
1. Split the music clip at the start and end of each dialogue section.
2. Lower the volume of the music clips that sit under dialogue.
3. Raise the music back up for the gaps between dialogue.
4. Add a short fade (a few frames) at each volume change so it does not click.
The rule: duck the music under every section of dialogue — not just the loud parts. The viewer should never have to strain.
Real-life comparison. Ducking is like a good friend at a party. When you are speaking, they lower their voice so you can be
heard. When you stop, they come back up. They do not talk over you.
Common misconception. "Ducking is only for professionals." No. Ducking is one of the easiest and most impactful audio skills
— and it is the difference between an amateur and a professional sound.
F.4 Noise reduction — cleaning the sound
The idea. Noise reduction is a tool that reduces unwanted background hiss, hum or room noise from a recording.
Plain-English definition. Noise reduction listens to the audio and tries to remove the steady background noise (air conditioner
hum, room hiss, distant traffic) while keeping the voice.
When it helps:
Light room noise (a quiet fan, a distant air conditioner).
A steady hum or hiss in the background.
Slightly noisy phone recordings.
When it hurts:
Heavy noise (a busy street, a loud crowd) — noise reduction makes the voice sound underwater or robotic.
Very short clips — there is not enough "clean" audio for the tool to work with.
When the noise is part of the story (a market scene, a football match) — do not remove it.
How to apply noise reduction in CapCut:
1. Select the audio clip (or the video clip with its audio).
2. In the audio tools, find "Noise reduction" or "Denoise".
3. Apply it at a moderate strength — start low and increase until the noise is reduced but the voice still sounds natural.
4. Listen on headphones. If the voice sounds robotic or underwater, reduce the strength.
The rule: noise reduction is a light touch, not a miracle. If the recording is very noisy, the best fix is to re-record in a quieter
place — not to push the tool harder.
Real-life comparison. Noise reduction is like washing a slightly dirty plate. A light wash removes the dust. But if the plate is
covered in mud, washing harder will not make it clean — you need a fresh plate. A fresh recording in a quiet room is always
better than heavy noise reduction.
Common misconception. "Noise reduction can fix any bad recording." No. It helps with light noise; it cannot fix heavy noise.
The best fix is a good recording — which is why the next skill (voiceover) matters.
F.5 Recording a clean voiceover
The idea. A voiceover (VO) is a recorded narration that plays over the video. It is one of the most useful skills in editing — and
one of the easiest to learn.
Plain-English definition. A voiceover is a separate audio recording of a narrator speaking a script, placed on the timeline over
the video.
Why it exists:
To explain what the video shows ("Mama T's Kitchen has been serving Port Harcourt for ten years").
To add information the footage cannot show (prices, dates, calls to action).
To give the video a professional, polished feel.
The five rules of a clean voiceover:
1. Use a quiet room. Turn off fans, air conditioners and televisions. Close windows. Record in a room with soft furnishings
(curtains, carpets) — they absorb echo.
2. Use the phone's voice recorder or a computer microphone. You do not need a professional microphone for a beginner
voiceover.
3. Hold the phone 15–20 cm from your mouth — close enough to be clear, far enough to avoid popping sounds on "p"
and "b".
4. Speak slowly and clearly. Beginners speak too fast when recording. Slow down. Pause between sentences.
5. Record two or three takes. Choose the best one. Delete the others.
How to add a voiceover to the edit:
1. Record the voiceover and transfer the file to the computer.
2. Import the voiceover file into CapCut.
3. Place it on an audio track below the video.
4. Adjust its level so it sits clearly above the music.
5. Trim the start and end so it begins and ends cleanly.
Real-life comparison. A voiceover is like a tour guide at a museum. The exhibits (the video) are interesting, but the guide (the
voiceover) tells you what you are looking at and why it matters. Without the guide, you walk past things without understanding.
Common misconception. "I do not have a good voice for voiceover." No. A clear, warm, honest voice is better than a
"professional" voice that sounds fake. The audience wants to hear a real person — not a radio announcer.
F.6 Syncing cuts to a beat
The idea. Beat-sync means cutting the video on the beat of the music — so the cuts land exactly when the music hits.
Plain-English definition. Beat-sync is the technique of placing your cuts on the strong beats of the music track, so the video
and music feel like one thing.
Why it exists. When cuts land on the beat, the video feels rhythmic and professional. When they land off-beat, the video feels
sloppy — even if the viewer cannot say why.
How to beat-sync in CapCut:
1. Place the music track on the timeline.
2. Listen and tap (or note) where the strong beats fall — usually the drum hits.
3. Mark the beats (CapCut has a "beat marker" feature, or you can add markers manually).
4. Move your cuts so they land on the beat markers.
5. Watch and adjust until the cuts feel locked to the music.
The rule: beat-sync is most important in music-driven videos — promos, event highlights, fashion edits. In dialogue-driven
videos (interviews, sermons), the cuts follow the speech, not the beat.
Real-life comparison. Beat-sync is like dancing. When you move on the beat, you look natural. When you move off-beat, you
look awkward — even if the steps are the same. The music is the rhythm; the cuts are the dance.
Common misconception. "Beat-sync is only for music videos." No. Any video with a music bed benefits from beat-sync — a
restaurant promo, a church highlight, a product ad. The beat gives the video energy.
F.7 Loudness — what platforms expect
The idea. Loudness is how loud the finished video sounds overall. Platforms like YouTube, Instagram and TikTok adjust videos
to a standard loudness — so your video should already be close to that standard.
Plain-English definition. Loudness is the perceived volume of the whole video. Platforms measure it and turn quiet videos up
and loud videos down — so a video that is mixed at the right loudness sounds consistent with everything else on the platform.
The practical guidance:
Do not make your video as loud as possible. Over-compressed, distorted audio sounds terrible and gets turned down by
the platform anyway.
Aim for a balanced mix — the voice clear, the music supportive, nothing clipping (distorting).
Check by meter and by ear. The meter shows the levels; the ear confirms they sound right.
Platform targets (approximate): YouTube normalises to around -14 LUFS; Instagram and TikTok are similar. For this
course, the practical rule is: the voice is clear, the music is balanced, and nothing distorts.
How to check loudness in CapCut:
1. Watch the audio meter while the video plays.
2. The voice should peak around -6 dB — not hitting 0 dB (which distorts).
3. The music should sit lower.
4. Listen on headphones: does it sound balanced and comfortable?
Real-life comparison. Loudness is like the volume of your shop's music. If it is too quiet, nobody notices you. If it is too loud,
people cover their ears and walk past. The right volume makes people stop and listen.
Common misconception. "Louder gets more attention." No. A distorted, over-loud video gets less watch-through because it is
uncomfortable. Balanced and clear wins.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Audio matters more than picture — "audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad sound."
Relative levels — the voice is the boss; music and effects support.
Ducking — music dips under dialogue so the voice is always clear.
Noise reduction — a light touch for light noise; re-record for heavy noise.
Voiceover — a clean recording in a quiet room, slow and clear.
Beat-sync — cuts land on the music's strong beats.
Loudness — balanced, clear, nothing distorting; platforms normalise anyway.

    B[Music level] --> D

    D --> E[Professional sound]

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Do a full audio pass on a rough cut — levels, ducking, noise reduction, voiceover and beat-sync — narrating every
decision so learners see the craft in action.
Setup: CapCut open on the projector. A rough cut ready. Headphones on the instructor. A music track imported. A voiceover
script ready.
Step 1 — The before listen (2 minutes)
Say: "First, I listen to the raw audio. The music is loud, the voice is buried, and there is room noise. I note the problems."
Play the rough cut. Point out the problems: music too loud, voice unclear, noise present.
Ask: "What is the first thing to fix?" → The levels — the voice must be above the music.
Step 2 — Set relative levels (3 minutes)
Say: "The voice is the boss. I lower the music until the voice is clearly above it. I watch the meter and listen on headphones."
Adjust the music volume down; adjust the voice up if needed.
Ask: "How do I know the level is right?" → The voice is clearly audible over the music without straining.
Step 3 — Duck the music (3 minutes)
Say: "Now I duck the music under the dialogue. I split the music at the start and end of each speaking section and lower it
there."
Split the music; lower the volume under dialogue; add short fades.
Ask: "What does ducking do?" → The music dips under the voice so the voice is always clear.
Step 4 — Noise reduction (2 minutes)
Say: "There is light room noise. I apply noise reduction at a moderate strength — enough to reduce the hiss, not so much
that the voice sounds robotic."
Apply noise reduction; listen and adjust.
Ask: "When does noise reduction hurt?" → When the noise is heavy — it makes the voice sound underwater.
Step 5 — Record and add a voiceover (3 minutes)
Say: "I record a short voiceover in a quiet corner — slow and clear. I import it, place it on an audio track, and set its level
above the music."
Play the pre-recorded voiceover (recorded before class); place it on the timeline; adjust the level.
Ask: "What makes a good voiceover?" → A quiet room, slow clear speech, and a level above the music.
Step 6 — Beat-sync one section (2 minutes)
Say: "This section has no dialogue — it is music-driven. I mark the beats and move the cuts to land on them."
Mark the beats; adjust the cuts.
Ask: "Why does beat-sync matter?" → It makes the video feel rhythmic and professional.
Expected result: A rough cut with balanced levels, ducked music, reduced noise, a clean voiceover and beat-synced cuts —
the model learners will follow.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why is the voice the boss?" (Because it carries the message.)
"What does ducking do?" (Music dips under dialogue.)
"When does noise reduction hurt?" (Heavy noise — it makes the voice robotic.)
"What makes a good voiceover?" (Quiet room, slow clear speech, right level.)
"Why beat-sync?" (Cuts on the beat feel professional.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)

Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner does a full audio pass on their rough cut.
Starting materials: The learner's rough cut open in CapCut; headphones on; a music track imported; a voiceover script;
Worksheet 4.1 (Levels Checklist) and 4.2 (Before/After Listening Note).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Put on your headphones. Listen to your rough cut once. Note the audio problems.
2. Set relative levels: lower the music until the voice is clearly above it. Fill in Worksheet 4.1.
3. Duck the music: split the music under each dialogue section and lower it there. Add short fades.
4. Apply noise reduction to one clip with light noise. Listen — adjust if it sounds robotic.
5. Record a voiceover (15 seconds, from the script) in a quiet corner. Import it and place it on an audio track.

7. Beat-sync one section: mark the beats and move the cuts to land on them.

9. Complete Worksheet 4.2: what you heard before, what you hear after, what you changed.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi runs a small bakery in Port Harcourt. She posts videos of her cakes on Instagram, but the videos get few views
and fewer orders. She watches her own videos and notices: the music is loud, her voice explaining the prices is hard to hear, and
there is a constant hum from her mixer in the background. A competitor's bakery posts similar videos — but the voice is clear,
the music is soft, and the videos feel professional. The competitor gets orders every week.
Relevant facts.
Ngozi's videos are well-shot — the cakes look beautiful.
The music is louder than her voice.
There is a steady hum from the mixer in the background.
She records her voice explaining the prices in the same noisy kitchen.
The competitor's videos have clear voice, soft music and no hum.
Decision point. What is Ngozi's problem — and what should she fix first?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy a better phone and reshoot.
2. Use louder music to make the videos more energetic.
3. Fix the audio: lower the music, record the voice in a quiet room, and reduce the hum.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ngozi's problem is not her phone or her cakes — it is her audio. The music is drowning
her voice (which carries the order information), and the hum makes the videos feel amateur. Lowering the music, recording the
voice in a quiet room, and applying light noise reduction will transform the videos — for free.
Reasoning / what Ngozi would do:
Lower the music so her voice is clearly above it.
Record her price explanations in a quiet room (or a quieter corner, with the mixer off).
Apply light noise reduction to reduce the hum.
Duck the music under her voice when she speaks.
The result: a video where the customer hears the prices clearly — and orders.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: a better phone will not fix audio that is mixed badly — the same problems will appear.
Approach 2: louder music makes the problem worse — the voice becomes even harder to hear.
Approach 3: free, immediate, and directly fixes the actual weakness. The customer can finally hear the prices.
Professional connection. A bakery that gets orders from Instagram is worth ₦50,000–₦150,000 a month in extra sales. The
difference between Ngozi and her competitor is not the cakes — it is the audio. An editor who can fix audio is worth
₦15,000–₦30,000 per video to small businesses like this. The skill is free to learn and immediately valuable.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Music louder than voice
Symptoms: The viewer cannot hear the speaker; the video feels uncomfortable. Likely cause: The music level was set too high
relative to the voice. How to investigate: Listen on headphones — can you hear the voice clearly over the music? Corrective
steps: Lower the music until the voice is clearly above it. The voice is the boss. How to confirm resolved: The voice is clearly
audible without straining. Prevention: Set levels with the voice as the reference — everything else sits below it.
K.2 No ducking — music fights the voice throughout
Symptoms: The music and voice compete the whole time; the viewer has to strain. Likely cause: The music was not ducked
under the dialogue. How to investigate: Listen — does the music dip when the voice speaks? Corrective steps: Split the
music under each dialogue section and lower it there. How to confirm resolved: The music supports the voice without fighting
it. Prevention: Duck the music under every section of dialogue.
K.3 Heavy noise reduction — voice sounds robotic
Symptoms: The voice sounds underwater or robotic after noise reduction. Likely cause: The noise reduction strength was too
high for the amount of noise. How to investigate: Listen — does the voice sound natural? Corrective steps: Reduce the
strength; if the noise is heavy, re-record in a quieter place. How to confirm resolved: The voice sounds natural and the noise is
reduced. Prevention: Use noise reduction as a light touch, not a miracle.
K.4 Voiceover recorded in a noisy room
Symptoms: The voiceover has echo, hum or background noise. Likely cause: The recording was made in a noisy room. How to
investigate: Listen to the voiceover alone — is the background quiet? Corrective steps: Re-record in a quiet room — turn off
fans, close windows, use soft furnishings. How to confirm resolved: The voiceover is clean and clear. Prevention: The
recording environment is half the quality — choose it carefully.
K.5 Cuts not on the beat
Symptoms: The video feels sloppy even though the cuts are deliberate. Likely cause: The cuts do not land on the music's
strong beats. How to investigate: Watch with the music — do the cuts land on the beat? Corrective steps: Mark the beats
and move the cuts to land on them. How to confirm resolved: The cuts feel locked to the music. Prevention: Mark the beats
before cutting; cut to the rhythm.
K.6 Audio distorting (clipping)
Symptoms: The audio crackles or distorts on loud moments. Likely cause: The level is too high — hitting 0 dB and clipping.
How to investigate: Watch the meter — is it hitting the red? Corrective steps: Lower the level of the distorting clip. How to
confirm resolved: The meter stays below 0 dB; the audio is clean. Prevention: Keep peaks around -6 dB; never let the meter hit
the red.
K.7 Ignoring loudness — the video is too quiet or too loud
Symptoms: The video sounds inconsistent with other videos on the platform. Likely cause: The overall loudness was not
checked. How to investigate: Compare with a professional video — is yours much quieter or louder? Corrective steps: Adjust
the overall mix to a balanced level; check by meter and ear. How to confirm resolved: The video sounds consistent with
professional content. Prevention: Check loudness at the end of every audio pass.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the meter reading, or the ducked music on the timeline). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
