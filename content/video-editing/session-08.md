# Client Work, Revisions and File Management

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Polish and Deliver

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first professional practice)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–7 — learners must have their export specification sheet and dual-format delivery (from Session 7). They must be able to do a full edit and export to specification. No prior client or business knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 7 you learned to export to specification. Today you learn to work like a professional with clients, revisions and file management: reading a brief, handling revision rounds without endless free rework, versioning files honestly, naming and archiving deliverables, and pricing editing work in naira. The rule: a professional is not just skilled — a professional is organised, honest and clear about scope.

**Preparation before class:** Simulation first: Pack R (6–10 previously unseen clips, under 300 MB, with manifest and consent note) and the printed Rush Brief and rubric (Part 4 §8.3; Part 5 simulation template) ready for the 30-minute SIMULATION — THE RUSH JOB (15%) block below. Then: a projector; printed sample briefs (two different client jobs); printed revision request forms; a sample versioned file set (showing _final , _final2 , _finalfinal vs. the clean convention); a naira pricing worksheet; the instructor's own client job pack pre-built as a model. Instructor note on tone: This session is about professionalism — the habits that turn a skilled editor into a reliable one. The key message: a professional is not just skilled — a professional is organised, honest and clear about scope. Use real naira figures, real scenarios and real consequences. The goal is not to make learners business experts; it is to make them professionals who get hired again.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Extract requirements from a written brief — identifying the deliverables, the audience, the platform, the deadline and
the scope.
2. Handle a revision round — making agreed changes, keeping a revision log, and avoiding endless free rework.
3. Version files honestly — using a clean naming convention ( _v1 , _v2 , _Approved ) instead of _final , _final2 ,
_finalfinal .
4. Name and archive deliverables — so they can be found later and the project can be reopened.
5. Quote a price in naira for a defined editing job — using a clear method based on hours and scope, not a guess.
6. Write a short handover note — telling the client what was delivered, in what format, and what to do with each file.

The professional: MamaT_Promo_v1.mp4 , MamaT_Promo_v2.mp4 , MamaT_Promo_Approved.mp4
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Look at these two sets of file names. The first set is what happens when an editor versions files badly —
'final,' 'final2,' 'finalfinal.' By the time you reach 'finalfinal2,' nobody — including you — knows which file is the real one.
The second set is the professional convention: version 1, version 2, approved. Clean, honest, findable.
Here is the rule for today: a professional is not just skilled — a professional is organised, honest and clear about
scope. Today you learn the habits that turn a skilled editor into a reliable one: reading a brief, handling revisions without
endless free rework, versioning files honestly, naming and archiving deliverables, and pricing your work in naira. By the
end of this session, you will have a complete client job pack — the thing that gets you hired again."
Expected learner response: Learners will laugh at "finalfinal," say "I have files like that," "I never thought about naming."
Accept all answers. Say: "You are not alone — every editor has a 'finalfinal' story. Today you learn to avoid it."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"Clients do not just pay for editing — they pay for reliability. An editor who delivers the right file, on time, at an agreed
price, with honest communication is an editor who gets hired again. That is what you learn today."
Why this opener works: It uses a problem every learner recognises (the "finalfinal" trap), it makes the professional convention
memorable by contrast, and it frames the session as the habits that build a career — not just skills.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the six export settings, and which one is the most important for quality?"
Expected answer: Codec, container, resolution, frame rate, bitrate, aspect ratio. Bitrate is the most important for quality.
Common gap: learners name the settings but forget which matters most. Clarify: "Bitrate is the one that ruins video when
you get it wrong."
Question 2: "What is the professional habit for exporting?"
Expected answer: Export to specification, then check the file.
Common gap: learners export without checking. Clarify: "The habit is the check — not just the export."
Question 3: "What is the story test, and how do you apply it?"
Expected answer: The story test is "does this help the story, or only my ego?" Apply it before keeping any effect.
Common gap: learners apply it to effects but not to client work. Clarify: "The story test applies to client work too — does
this deliverable serve the client's goal?"
Question 4: "What are safe areas, and why do they matter?"
Expected answer: Safe areas are the parts of the screen not covered by the platform's interface. Text outside them is hidden.
Common gap: learners still place text wherever it looks nice. Clarify: "Text in the editor is not text on the platform — check
the safe area."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You can now edit and export to specification. Today we learn to work like a professional — with clients, revisions and files.
A professional is organised, honest and clear about scope. Learn these habits, and you will be the editor clients call back."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing the six export settings on the
projector before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Reading a brief — extracting the real requirements
The idea. A brief is a written document that tells you what the client wants. Reading it carefully is the first professional skill.
Plain-English definition. A brief is a written description of the job: what to make, for whom, for which platform, by when, and
to what standard.
The six things every brief must answer:
Question Why it matters
What is the deliverable? A 60-second promo? A 30-second Reel? A highlights video?
Who is the audience? Customers? Church members? Students?
Where will it be watched? YouTube? Instagram? TikTok? WhatsApp?
When is the deadline? A specific date — not "as soon as possible."
What is the scope? How many revision rounds? What is included?
What is the budget? A price range — or an agreement to quote.
How to read a brief (the method):
1. Read the whole brief once — do not start editing.
2. Highlight the six answers above.
3. If anything is missing, ask before you start.
4. Summarise the brief in your own words and confirm with the client.
Why this matters. Most client problems come from a brief that was not read — or not clarified. A 60-second promo and a 30-
second Reel are different jobs. A church video and a business promo are different jobs. If you do not know what the client
wants, you cannot deliver it.
Real-life comparison. A brief is like a customer's order at a restaurant. If the customer says "rice and chicken," you do not
serve them beans and fish. If they say "no pepper," you do not add pepper. The brief is the order — read it, confirm it, then
cook.
Common misconception. "I can figure it out as I go." No. Guessing is how you deliver the wrong video, waste time, and lose the
client. The brief is the contract — read it first.
F.2 Scope and revisions — the boundary of the job
The idea. Scope is what is included in the job — and what is not. Revisions are changes the client requests after seeing the first
draft. Managing both is the core of professional client work.
Plain-English definitions:
Scope — the agreed work: how many videos, how many lengths, how many revision rounds.
Revision — a change requested by the client after seeing a draft.
The scope rules (teach these exactly):
1. Agree the scope before you start. How many revision rounds? Usually 1–2. What is included? Editing, captions, music,
colour — or only some of these?
2. Revisions are for agreed changes. A revision is a change to the brief — not a new job. If the client asks for a 30-second
Reel when the brief was a 60-second YouTube video, that is a new job — quote for it.
3. Keep a revision log. For each change, write: what was requested, why, and whether it is within scope.
4. Charge for out-of-scope work. If the client asks for more than the agreed rounds, quote for the extra work — politely and
clearly.
Why this matters. The most common beginner trap is endless free rework — the client keeps asking for changes, and the
editor keeps making them for free. This burns time, kills profit, and teaches the client to expect unlimited work. Clear scope
prevents this.
Real-life comparison. Scope is like a building contract. If the client agrees to a three-bedroom house, they cannot later ask for
a fourth bedroom for free. If they want it, they pay for it. The contract protects both sides.
Common misconception. "I must do whatever the client asks." No. A professional does what was agreed — and charges fairly
for what was not. Saying no politely is a professional skill.
F.3 Versioning — naming files honestly
The idea. Versioning is the system for naming different drafts of the same file, so everyone knows which is the latest and which
is approved.
The trap (and why it happens): final , final2 , finalfinal , finalfinal2 — because every time the client asks for a change,
the editor adds a new name. Soon nobody knows which is the real file.
The professional convention (teach this exactly):
```text
ProjectName_Deliverable_v1.mp4
ProjectName_Deliverable_v2.mp4
ProjectName_Deliverable_Approved.mp4
```
v1 is the first draft.
v2 is the second draft (after the first revision round).
Approved is the final file the client has accepted.
Why this works. The names are chronological and honest. Anyone — the client, the editor, a future you — can look at the
folder and know exactly what each file is.
The archive rule: keep all versions. Do not delete the old ones. The client may ask for a change back to an earlier version —
and you will need it.
Real-life comparison. Versioning is like the chapters of a book. A first draft, a second draft, a final edition — each one is
named and kept. A writer does not call the second draft "final" and the third "final2."
Common misconception. "I will remember which is the latest." No. In three months, you will not remember. The naming
system is the memory.
F.4 Naming and archiving — so files can be found
The idea. Naming is giving files meaningful names. Archiving is storing the finished project so it can be reopened later.
The naming convention (from Session 1, now applied to deliverables):
```text
ClientOrProject_Deliverable_vX_Status
```
Examples: MamaT_Promo_v1.mp4 , MamaT_Promo_v2.mp4 , MamaT_Promo_Approved.mp4
The archive structure (from Session 1, now applied to the whole project):
```text
CEA-Edit-YourName/
├── 01_Footage/          (the raw material — keep it)
├── 02_Audio/            (music, SFX, VO — keep it)
├── 03_Exports/          (the delivered files — keep them)
├── 04_ProjectFiles/     (the CapCut project — keep it)
├── 05_Archive/          (old versions — keep them)
└── Docs/                (briefs, revision logs, handover notes — keep them)
```
Why archiving matters. In six months, the client may call and ask for a small change — a different music track, a shorter
version, an updated price. If the project is archived, you can reopen it and make the change in minutes. If it is not, you are
starting over — or turning down the job.
The backup rule (from Session 1): the archive exists in two places — the computer and a USB drive (or cloud). One copy is no
copy.
Real-life comparison. Archiving is like keeping your receipts. You do not think you need them — until you do. A professional
keeps everything organised, so the future is easy.
Common misconception. "I will organise it later." Later never comes — and the client who calls in six months will not care
about your excuses. Archive as you go.
F.5 Pricing in naira — a method, not a guess
The idea. Pricing is what you charge for a job. A professional prices from a method — not from a guess.
Plain-English definition. A price is the total naira amount you charge for a defined job, calculated from the time it takes and the
scope of the work.
The pricing method (teach these steps):
1. Estimate the hours. How long will the job take? Be realistic — include editing, revisions, export and communication.
2. Set an hourly rate. For a beginner in Port Harcourt, a realistic range is ₦2,000–₦5,000 per hour. As you gain
experience and reputation, you charge more.
3. Calculate the subtotal. Hours × rate.
4. Add a scope buffer. Add 10–20% for unexpected changes or extra rounds.
5. Round to a clean figure. Present a round number — not ₦17,350, but ₦18,000.
6. Present a range if unsure. "This job will cost between ₦15,000 and ₦20,000, depending on the revisions."
The realistic naira ranges for common jobs (Port Harcourt market, 2026):
Job Typical price range
30-second social media edit ₦10,000–₦20,000
60-second business promo ₦15,000–₦35,000
3–5 minute event highlight ₦25,000–₦60,000
Church service highlights ₦20,000–₦40,000
Wedding highlights (short) ₦40,000–₦80,000
The rules of professional pricing:
1. Agree the price before you start. No surprise invoices.
2. Take a deposit. 30–50% before you start — this protects you and commits the client.
3. Charge for out-of-scope work. More than the agreed revision rounds? Quote for it.
4. Be honest about what is included. Editing, captions, music, colour — or only some? Write it down.
Real-life comparison. Pricing is like a tailor's quote. A good tailor measures the fabric, estimates the time, and gives a clear
price before starting. A bad tailor says "I will tell you later" — and the customer is surprised by the bill.
Common misconception. "I should charge as little as possible to get clients." No. Undercharging attracts clients who do not
value your work and burns you out. A fair price for a quality job attracts the right clients.
F.6 The handover note — telling the client what they have
The idea. A handover note is a short document that tells the client what was delivered, in what format, and what to do with
each file.
Plain-English definition. A handover note is a one-page summary of the delivery: the files, their formats, and how to use them.
The handover note template:
```text
HANDOVER NOTE
Client: [name]
Job: [what was done]
Date: [delivery date]
```
```text
Delivered files:
1. [filename] — [format, e.g., 1080p MP4 for YouTube] — [what to do with it]
2. [filename] — [format, e.g., 9:16 MP4 for TikTok] — [what to do with it]
```
```text
Notes:
- [any important information — music source, captions, etc.]
- [what the client should do if they need changes]
```
```text
Contact: [your contact information]
```
Why it matters. The handover note is the last impression. It tells the client you are professional, organised and clear. It also
prevents the most common post-delivery question: "Which file do I use?"
Real-life comparison. A handover note is like the instruction manual that comes with a new phone. The phone works
without it — but the manual makes everything clearer. A professional includes it.
Common misconception. "The client will figure it out." No. A client who cannot figure out which file to use is a client who calls
you in a panic — or who uses the wrong file and blames you. The handover note prevents both.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
A brief answers six questions: what, who, where, when, scope, budget.
Scope is the agreed work — agree it before you start.
Revisions are agreed changes — keep a log; charge for out-of-scope work.
Versioning uses a clean convention: _v1 , _v2 , _Approved — not _final , _final2 .
Archiving keeps the project in two places so it can be reopened.
Pricing is a method: hours × rate, plus a buffer, rounded to a clean figure.
A handover note tells the client what they have and what to do with it.

```text

    A[Client brief] --> B[First draft] --> C[Client feedback] --> D{Changes needed?} -->|Yes| B
    D -->|No| E[Final delivery]
```

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ada is a freelance editor in Port Harcourt. Her second client — a fashion boutique — asks for a 30-second
Instagram Reel. Ada agrees to do the job but does not agree on a price or a revision scope. She edits the video and delivers it.
The client loves it but asks for "just one more change" — then another, then another. Ada makes all the changes for free, over
two weeks. The client pays the original agreed price — which was too low — and never calls again. Ada has worked for two
weeks, been paid for one, and lost the client.
Relevant facts.
Ada did not agree on a price or revision scope before starting.
She made all revisions for free.
The agreed price was too low for the work involved.
The client never called again — and told two other boutiques that Ada was "difficult."
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should Ada have done differently?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep working for free — the client is happy.
2. Refuse all future work from this client.
3. Learn the lesson: agree the price and scope before starting, and charge for out-of-scope work.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ada's problem is not the client — it is that she did not agree the price and scope before
starting. Had she agreed a clear price (with a buffer) and a clear scope (two revision rounds), the client would have known what
to expect — and Ada would have been paid fairly for the extra work. The lesson: agree the price and scope before you start —
every time.
Reasoning / what Ada would do:
Agree the price before starting: a 30-second Reel, 3 hours × ₦3,000 = ₦9,000, plus buffer = ₦10,000.
Agree the scope: two revision rounds included; further changes quoted separately.
Keep a revision log: what was requested, why, whether it is within scope.
If the client asks for more than two rounds, quote for the extra work — politely and clearly.
The result: a fair price, a clear scope, and a client who knows the rules.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: working for free burns time and teaches the client to expect unlimited work.
Approach 2: refusing all future work loses a client who could have become a good one.
Approach 3: agreeing the price and scope upfront is uncomfortable for ten minutes — but it protects the whole job and
builds a professional relationship.
Professional connection. Ada's story is the most common beginner mistake — and the most avoidable. Editors who agree the
price and scope before starting get paid fairly, get hired again, and build a reputation. Editors who do not, burn out and lose
clients. The difference is one conversation — and it is worth thousands of naira.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Not reading the brief before starting
Symptoms: The delivered video is not what the client wanted — wrong length, wrong platform, wrong content. Likely cause:
The brief was not read — or not clarified — before starting. How to investigate: Ask: "Did you read the whole brief? Did you
confirm the six answers?" Corrective steps: Stop. Read the brief. Summarise it. Confirm with the client. Then continue. How to
confirm resolved: The six answers are clear and confirmed. Prevention: Read the brief first — every time.
K.2 No agreed scope — endless free rework
Symptoms: The client keeps asking for changes; the editor keeps making them for free. Likely cause: The scope was not agreed
before starting. How to investigate: Ask: "How many revision rounds were agreed? What was included?" Corrective steps:
Agree the scope going forward; quote for out-of-scope work politely. How to confirm resolved: The scope is clear and agreed.
Prevention: Agree the scope before you start — in writing if possible.
K.3 Bad versioning — _final, _final2, _finalfinal
Symptoms: Nobody knows which file is the latest; the client receives the wrong file. Likely cause: Files were named with "final"
instead of the clean convention. How to investigate: Look at the file names — are they v1, v2, Approved? Corrective steps:
Rename the files using the convention; explain the system to the client. How to confirm resolved: The names are
chronological and honest. Prevention: Use the convention from the start — every project.
K.4 No archive — the project cannot be reopened
Symptoms: The client calls for a change in six months; the project is gone or disorganised. Likely cause: The project was not
archived — or not backed up. How to investigate: Can you find the project files, footage and exports? Corrective steps: If the
archive exists, organise it. If not, learn the lesson for the next project. How to confirm resolved: The project can be reopened in
minutes. Prevention: Archive as you go — two places, every project.
K.5 Pricing by guess — too low or too high
Symptoms: The price does not reflect the work; the editor loses money or loses the client. Likely cause: The price was guessed,
not calculated. How to investigate: Ask: "How did you arrive at this price? What is your hourly rate and hour estimate?"
Corrective steps: Calculate the price from hours × rate, plus a buffer, rounded. How to confirm resolved: The price is
justified by a clear method. Prevention: Use the pricing method — every job.
K.6 No handover note — the client is confused
Symptoms: The client does not know which file to use; they call in a panic. Likely cause: No handover note was provided. How
to investigate: Ask: "What did you tell the client about the files?" Corrective steps: Write a handover note — what was
delivered, in what format, what to do. How to confirm resolved: The client knows exactly what they have. Prevention: Write a
handover note for every delivery.
K.7 No deposit — the client does not pay
Symptoms: The editor completes the work but is not paid. Likely cause: No deposit was taken before starting. How to
investigate: Ask: "Did you take a deposit before starting?" Corrective steps: For future jobs, take a 30–50% deposit before
starting. How to confirm resolved: The deposit is agreed and received before work begins. Prevention: Take a deposit —
every job.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the agreed scope, the file names, or the pricing calculation). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What are the six things every brief must answer?
Q2. Explain. What is scope, and why must you agree it before starting?
Q3. Multiple choice. Which is the correct versioning convention? a) final, final2, finalfinal b) v1, v2, Approved c) new, newer,
newest d) draft, draft2, final
Q4. Short answer. What are the steps of the pricing method?
Q5. Scenario. A client asks for a change that is outside the agreed scope. What do you do?
Q6. Short answer. What is a handover note, and why does it matter?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. What (the deliverable), who (the audience), where (the platform), when (the deadline), scope (what is included), budget (the
price or a range).
A2. Scope is the agreed work — what is included and what is not. You must agree it before starting to prevent endless free
rework and to protect both you and the client.
A3. (b) v1, v2, Approved — clean, chronological, honest.
A4. (1) Estimate the hours. (2) Set an hourly rate. (3) Calculate the subtotal (hours × rate). (4) Add a scope buffer (10–20%).
(5) Round to a clean figure. (6) Present the price before starting.
A5. Politely explain that the change is outside the agreed scope and quote for the extra work. If the client agrees, do the work; if
not, the original agreement stands. The principle: charge fairly for out-of-scope work — saying no politely is a professional
skill.
A6. A handover note is a one-page document that tells the client what was delivered, in what format, and what to do with each
file. It matters because it is the last impression — and it prevents the most common post-delivery question: "Which file do I
use?"
