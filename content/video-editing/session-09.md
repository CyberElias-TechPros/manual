# Capstone: Edit a Complete Video Project

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Polish and Deliver

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (capstone — drawing on all eight sessions)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–8 — learners must have their complete skill set: workflow, software, cut craft, audio, colour, text, motion, export and professional practice. They must have their organised project folder and all previous mini-projects.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 8 you learned to work like a professional with clients, revisions and files. Today is the capstone: you receive the Mama T's Kitchen footage pack and a written brief, and you edit a complete 60–90 second brand video — from setup and assembly through cut craft, audio, colour, text, motion and export — then present and defend your edit in a viva. This is where everything comes together.

**Preparation before class:** The Capstone Pack — Mama T's Kitchen (kitchen prep, food plates, staff at work, customers, exterior, and a short owner interview) copied to the shared class folder and verified; the written brief printed for each learner; a projector; a quiet space for the viva presentations; the capstone assessment rubric printed for the instructor; a certificate ceremony planned for the end of the session. Instructor note on tone: This is the culmination of the course — the session where learners prove they can do the whole job. The tone should be celebratory but rigorous. The capstone is not a test to fear; it is a chance to show what you can do. Remind learners of the journey: "From Zero to Expert, Together." The viva is marked on clarity and accuracy, not on speaking style or accent.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Set up a complete project — aspect ratio, resolution and frame rate chosen and stated before editing, with a clean folder
structure and backup.
2. Build a coherent edit from the supplied footage pack — clear beginning, middle and end; no accidental gaps; no unused
clips left in the timeline.
3. Demonstrate deliberate cutting — at least three named cut types used on purpose, including one J-cut or L-cut around
dialogue.
4. Deliver balanced audio — voice clearly above music; music ducked under speech; sensible loudness.
5. Apply corrected colour and readable text — exposure and white balance corrected; a title, at least one lower third, and
captions — correctly spelled, timed and inside safe areas.
6. Apply restrained motion — at least two intentional motion techniques and no gratuitous effects.
7. Export two correctly named files matching the brief — a 16:9 file (1920×1080) and a 9:16 file (1080×1920), both with
captions.
8. Write a short handover note — what was delivered, in what format, and what the client should do with each file.
9. Present and defend the edit in a viva — explaining the key decisions clearly and accurately under questioning.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a 60-second brand video — a polished, professional edit of a food business (the
instructor's own capstone example, or a sample). Play it once without commentary.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. That is what you are going to make today. A complete, professional brand video — from raw footage to
finished, exported, defended.
Look around the room. Three weeks ago, some of you had never opened an editing program. Today you are going to do the
whole job: setup, assembly, cut craft, audio, colour, text, motion, export and a viva. Every skill you have learned comes
together in this one video.
This is the capstone — the thing that proves you can do the job. It is not a test to fear. It is a chance to show what you can
do. And at the end, we celebrate — because you have earned it.
Remember our philosophy: From Zero to Expert, Together. Today you take the last step."
Expected learner response: Learners will feel a mix of excitement and nervousness. Some will say "I can do this," others "I am
not ready." Accept both. Say: "You are ready — you have been building to this for eight sessions. Trust the process."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"The brief is on your desk. The footage is in the shared folder. You have 105 minutes. Work through the checklist — setup
first, then the edit, then the export, then the viva. I am here to guide. Let us begin."
Why this opener works: It shows the finished result (making the goal concrete), it acknowledges the journey (building
confidence), and it frames the capstone as a celebration of learning — not a test to fear.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the ten stages of the post-production workflow?"
Expected answer: Ingest, organise, log, backup, assembly, rough cut, fine cut, sound, colour, titles/export/delivery/archive.
Common gap: learners remember the first four and the last two but forget the middle. Clarify: "The middle stages are where
the craft lives."
Question 2: "What are the five core editing actions?"
Expected answer: Place, split, trim, reorder, delete.
Common gap: learners confuse split and trim. Clarify: "Trim shortens the ends; split cuts in the middle."
Question 3: "What is the rule about audio and picture?"
Expected answer: "Audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad sound."
Common gap: learners still focus on picture first. Clarify: "The voice carries the message — protect it."
Question 4: "What is the professional habit for exporting?"
Expected answer: Export to specification, then check the file.
Common gap: learners export without checking. Clarify: "The habit is the check — not just the export."
Question 5: "What is the versioning convention?"
Expected answer: v1, v2, Approved — not final, final2, finalfinal.
Common gap: learners still use "final." Clarify: "The convention is the memory — use it every time."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have every skill. Today you use them all. The brief is your contract — read it, follow it, and check every requirement.
The viva is your chance to explain your decisions. Let us make a professional video."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing the workflow stages on the board
before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 The capstone brief — the contract
The idea. The capstone is a complete client job. The brief is the contract — read it, follow it, and check every requirement.
The Mama T's Kitchen brief (read this to the class):
Client: Mama T's Kitchen — a fictional small food business in Port Harcourt. Job: A 60–90 second brand video for
YouTube and TikTok. Purpose: Introduce the kitchen and drive orders — warm, appetising, trustworthy. Must include:
A hook in the first 3 seconds.
At least one line of the owner's interview with synced audio.
A title with the business name.
Lower-third or caption text.
A music bed mixed under any speech.
An end card with a call to action (the order number). Must respect:
Readable captions inside safe areas.
No misleading claims about the food.
Only approved free music. Deliverables:
Two exports — a 16:9 file (1920×1080) and a 9:16 file (1080×1920), both with captions, correctly named.
A short handover note.
A presentation and viva.
The ten requirements (the checklist):
1. Correct project setup — aspect ratio, resolution and frame rate chosen and stated before editing.
2. A coherent edit — clear beginning, middle and end; no accidental gaps; no unused clips.
3. Deliberate cutting — at least three named cut types, including one J-cut or L-cut around dialogue.
4. Balanced audio — voice clearly above music; music ducked under speech; sensible loudness.
5. Colour — exposure and white balance corrected; clips of the same scene matched; a light grade.
6. Text — a title, at least one lower third, and captions; correctly spelled, timed and inside safe areas.
7. Restrained motion — at least two intentional motion techniques and no gratuitous effects.
8. Two correctly exported files matching the brief, plus properly versioned file names.
9. A short handover note — what was delivered, in what format, and what the client should do with each file.
10. A presentation and viva — a short spoken defence of the edit.
Why the checklist matters. The capstone is marked against these ten requirements. Every one is a skill you have learned. Work
through the checklist in order — and tick each one only when it is genuinely done.
Real-life comparison. The brief is like a building plan. A builder does not start bricks before the plan is read. The plan protects
the builder and the client — it is the contract.
Common misconception. "I will figure it out as I go." No. The brief is the contract — read it, follow it, check every requirement.
That is the professional habit.
F.2 The capstone workflow — the whole job in order
The idea. The capstone is the whole workflow from Session 1 to Session 8, applied to one job. Follow the order — do not skip
stages.

Why the order matters. Each stage prepares the next. Setup before assembly. Assembly before cut craft. Cut craft before audio.
Audio before colour. Colour before text. Text before motion. Motion before export. Export before handover. Handover before
viva. Skipping a stage makes the next one harder.
Real-life comparison. The workflow is like building a house: foundation before walls, walls before roof, roof before paint, paint
before furniture. You do not put the furniture in before the walls are up.
Common misconception. "I can do the stages in any order." No. The order is the discipline. Follow it — and the capstone is
manageable.
F.3 The viva — presenting and defending your edit
The idea. The viva is a short spoken defence of your edit — where you explain your key decisions and answer questions. It is
the mark of a professional.
Plain-English definition. A viva (short for viva voce, Latin for "with live voice") is a spoken examination — in this case, a 3–5
minute presentation where you explain your edit and answer questions.
What the viva assesses:
Can you explain why you made your key decisions?
Can you name the cut types you used and why?
Can you explain your audio, colour and text choices?
Can you answer questions clearly and accurately?
What the viva does NOT assess:
Your speaking style or accent.
How confident you sound.
Whether your video is perfect.
How to prepare for the viva:
1. Watch your finished video once.
2. Note your key decisions: the hook, the cut types, the audio mix, the colour, the text, the motion.
3. Write one sentence of reason for each decision.
4. Practise saying them out loud — once is enough.
5. Prepare for likely questions: "Why did you cut here?" "Why this music?" "Why did you delete that clip?"
The viva questions (likely):
"What is your hook, and why does it work?"
"Name two cut types you used and why."
"How did you handle the audio?"
"What did you correct in the colour?"
"Why did you delete that clip?"
"What would you do differently next time?"
Real-life comparison. The viva is like a job interview for your own work. You are not being tested on your personality — you
are being asked to show that you understand what you did and why. A professional can always explain their decisions.
Common misconception. "The viva is about sounding confident." No. The viva is about being clear and accurate. A quiet
learner who explains their decisions clearly scores higher than a confident learner who cannot explain them.
F.4 What success looks like
The idea. The capstone is not about perfection — it is about demonstrating the complete skill set. Success is a clean, coherent,
correctly exported video that meets the brief — and a learner who can explain it.
What success looks like:
The video is complete — 60–90 seconds, with a clear beginning, middle and end.
The cuts are deliberate — at least three named cut types, including a J-cut or L-cut.
The audio is balanced — voice clear, music ducked, sensible loudness.
The colour is corrected — exposure and white balance fixed, clips matched.
The text is readable — title, lower third, captions, correctly spelled, timed, inside safe areas.
The motion is restrained — at least two techniques, no gratuitous effects.
The exports are correct — two files to specification, checked, correctly named.
The handover note is clear — what was delivered, in what format, what to do.
The viva is clear — the learner explains their decisions accurately.
What does NOT look like success:
A video with drowned dialogue, unreadable captions or a 720p export when 1080p was asked for.
A learner who cannot explain why they made their choices.
A video that does not meet the brief.
Real-life comparison. Success is like a well-cooked meal — not a Michelin star meal, but a meal that is clean, tasty, served on
time, and made with care. A client can use it. That is success.
Common misconception. "The capstone must be perfect." No. The capstone must be professional — clean, coherent, correctly
exported, and honestly made. Perfection is the goal of a career; professionalism is the goal of this course.
F.5 Summary of the lecture
The brief is the contract — read it, follow it, check every requirement.
The workflow is the order — setup to viva, no skipped stages.
The viva is a spoken defence — clarity and accuracy, not confidence.
Success is a clean, coherent, correctly exported video that meets the brief — and a learner who can explain it.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner begins the capstone — setup, ingest, organisation and
assembly.
Starting materials: The Capstone Pack — Mama T's Kitchen in the shared class folder; the written brief; Worksheet 9.1
(Capstone Requirements Checklist).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Read the brief — read the whole thing once. Do not start editing.
2. Set up the project folder — create CEA-Edit-YourName with the full folder structure.
3. Set up the CapCut project — 16:9, 1080p, 30 fps, named CEA-YourName_MamaT_Capstone .
4. Ingest and organise — copy the footage, rename, log, back up to USB.
5. Build the assembly — place the clips in story order: hook → beginning → middle → end. No music, text or effects.
6. Watch the assembly — does the story make sense? Tick requirements 1 and 2 on the checklist.
Expected result: A set-up project with an assembly in story order — the foundation for the rest of the capstone.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 1: "What are the ten requirements?" (If they cannot name them, they are not ready to start.)
After step 6: "Does the story make sense in this order?" (If not, what would you move?)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Footage will not copy → Check the USB drive is formatted; check the file count.
Project will not save → Check the name has no special characters.
Assembly has gaps → Check that all clips are placed; no empty space on the timeline.
Story does not make sense → Reorder the clips; the assembly is the time to fix the story.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Brief read in full
Project folder created with full structure
CapCut project set up (16:9, 1080p, 30 fps, named)
Footage ingested, renamed, logged, backed up
Assembly built in story order
Assembly watched — story makes sense
Requirements 1 and 2 ticked on the checklist

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Objective: Continue the capstone independently — the rough cut, audio pass, colour and text pass, and motion pass —
working through the checklist.
Starting materials: The learner's assembly from Section H; the Capstone Pack; Worksheet 9.1.
Procedure (learners work independently; instructor walks the room and guides):
1. Rough cut — deliberate cuts: at least three named cut types, including a J-cut or L-cut around the owner's interview.
Cover jumps with B-roll. Adjust pacing.
2. Audio pass — levels, ducking, noise reduction if needed, a music bed mixed under speech, loudness check.
3. Colour and text pass — correct exposure and white balance, match clips, add a title, a lower third for the owner, and
captions (proofread, timed, inside safe areas).
4. Motion pass — at least two motion techniques (slow zoom, speed ramp, motion title); delete any gratuitous effects.
5. Tick requirements 3–7 on the checklist — only when each is genuinely done.
Expected result: A complete edit — rough cut, audio, colour, text and motion — with requirements 1–7 ticked.
Points to pause on and predict:
After the rough cut: "Name your three cut types." (If they cannot, the cuts are accidental.)
After the audio pass: "Is the voice clearly above the music?" (If not, adjust.)
After the colour and text pass: "Are the captions inside safe areas?" (If not, move them.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
J-cut is out of sync → Nudge the audio until it feels natural.
Captions are misspelt → Proofread; auto-captions make mistakes.
Colour looks worse → Reset and adjust in smaller steps.
Too many effects → Apply the story test; delete the gratuitous ones.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Skipping setup — editing before the project is configured
Symptoms: The project has the wrong aspect ratio or frame rate; titles are misplaced. Likely cause: The project was not set up
before editing started. How to investigate: Check the project settings — do they match the brief? Corrective steps: If the
settings are wrong, create a new project with the correct settings and re-import. How to confirm resolved: The project settings
match the brief before editing. Prevention: Setup is step one — every time.
K.2 No hook — the video starts slowly
Symptoms: The first 3 seconds do not grab attention. Likely cause: The video starts with a slow intro or a logo. How to
investigate: Watch the first 3 seconds — would a stranger stop? Corrective steps: Start with the strongest moment — the
sizzling food, a bold question, a surprising image. How to confirm resolved: The first 3 seconds make a stranger want to stay.
Prevention: The hook is the most important 3 seconds — protect it.
K.3 Drowned dialogue — music louder than voice
Symptoms: The viewer cannot hear the owner's interview. Likely cause: The music is louder than the voice; no ducking. How
to investigate: Listen — is the voice clearly above the music? Corrective steps: Lower the music; duck it under the speech.
How to confirm resolved: The voice is clearly audible. Prevention: The voice is the boss — set levels and duck.
K.4 Unreadable captions — misspelt or outside safe areas
Symptoms: Captions are misspelt, mistimed or covered by the platform interface. Likely cause: Auto-captions were not
proofread; text was placed outside safe areas. How to investigate: Read every caption; check the safe-area guide. Corrective
steps: Proofread; move text inside safe areas. How to confirm resolved: Every caption is correct, timed and visible. Prevention:
Proofread every caption; check safe areas before exporting.
K.5 Gratuitous effects — motion that does not earn its place
Symptoms: The video has effects that distract from the story. Likely cause: Effects were added without applying the story test.
How to investigate: For each effect, ask "does this help the story?" Corrective steps: Delete every effect that does not earn its
place. How to confirm resolved: Every effect is intentional. Prevention: The story test — apply it to every effect.
K.6 Wrong export — one file for both platforms
Symptoms: The video has black bars on one platform; the quality is poor. Likely cause: One export was used for both platforms.
How to investigate: Check the exports — are there two files, one 16:9 and one 9:16? Corrective steps: Export separately for
each platform. How to confirm resolved: Each platform gets a file that matches its specification. Prevention: Export for the
platform — not for convenience.
K.7 No handover note — the client is confused
Symptoms: The client does not know which file to use. Likely cause: No handover note was provided. How to investigate: Ask:
"What did you tell the client about the files?" Corrective steps: Write a handover note — what was delivered, in what format,
what to do. How to confirm resolved: The client knows exactly what they have. Prevention: Write a handover note for every
delivery.
K.8 Cannot explain decisions — the viva falls flat
Symptoms: The learner cannot explain why they made their choices. Likely cause: The decisions were not deliberate — or the
learner did not prepare. How to investigate: Ask: "Why did you cut here?" "Why this music?" Corrective steps: Prepare the viva
— note the key decisions and reasons; practise once. How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain their decisions clearly.
Prevention: Make deliberate decisions throughout — then the viva is just explaining them.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the project settings, the export files, or the handover note). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
