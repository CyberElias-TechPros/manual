# Cuts, Pacing and Story

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Foundations and Story

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first real cut craft)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1 and 2 — learners must have their organised, backed-up Pack A and their first assembly (from Session 2). They must be able to place, split, trim, reorder and delete in CapCut. No prior cut-craft knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 2 you built your first assembly — clips in story order, no music or text. Today you turn that assembly into a rough cut with real craft: you learn the named cut types, the J-cut and L-cut, pacing and rhythm, story shape, and how B-roll covers a jump. The assembly is the raw material; today you shape it.

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and tested; Pack B (Product and B-roll) copied to the shared class folder and verified; each learner's first assembly available (from Session 2); a projector; printed cut-type reference cards (one set per learner); the instructor's own rough cut pre-built to show the finished result; a short "pacing test" clip prepared (a 60-second sequence the class will time). Instructor note on tone: This is the session where editing becomes a craft rather than a task. Learners will feel the difference between a cut that is accidental and a cut that is deliberate. Celebrate that feeling. The goal is not to memorise terminology — it is to make decisions and explain them. Use the phrase "a cut is a decision, not an accident" early and often.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Name and demonstrate the five main cut types — straight cut, jump cut, cut on action, match cut, and the J-cut/L-cut
— and explain when each is used.
2. Select cut points deliberately — choosing the exact frame to cut on, and justifying the choice in one sentence.
3. Explain the difference between a J-cut (audio leads picture) and an L-cut (picture leads audio), and use at least one of them
around dialogue.
4. Analyse and adjust pacing — identifying a sequence that drags or feels frantic, and explaining what to cut or hold to fix it.
5. Describe the short-video story shape — hook, beginning, middle, end, payoff — and build a short edit with that shape.
6. Use B-roll purposefully — covering a jump cut and adding meaning, not just filling space.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, play the same 20-second dialogue clip twice.
Version 1: The dialogue is cut with obvious, clumsy cuts — each sentence starts with a breath, ends with a pause, and the
cuts land mid-word. It feels uncomfortable.
Version 2: The same dialogue, cut cleanly — pauses removed, cuts landing on natural breaks, the conversation flowing.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Same two people, same words, same footage. The first version feels uncomfortable. The second feels like a
real conversation. The only difference is where the cuts landed.
A cut is a decision, not an accident. Today you learn to make that decision on purpose. By the end of this session, you will
be able to look at any dialogue and say: 'I cut here because ______.' That is the difference between a beginner and an
editor."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "the first one is annoying," "the second one is smooth," "how did you do that?"
Accept all answers. Say: "You already have the taste — you can feel the difference. Today we give that taste a method."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"We are going to turn your Session 2 assembly into a rough cut. Not by cutting randomly — by choosing every cut point on
purpose, and being able to explain why. That is craft."
Why this opener works: It isolates the cut as the variable (same footage, different cuts), it proves that learners already have the
taste to feel good cuts, and it frames the session as giving that taste a method.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five core editing actions, and which one removes a pause from the middle of a clip?"
Expected answer: Place, split, trim, reorder, delete. The split removes a pause from the middle (split on both sides of the
pause, then delete the middle part).
Common gap: learners confuse split and trim. Clarify: trim shortens the ends; split cuts in the middle.
Question 2: "What is the playhead, and why does it matter for cutting?"
Expected answer: The playhead is the vertical line on the timeline showing the exact moment you are working on. A split
happens at the playhead — so the playhead's position decides where the cut lands.
Common gap: learners split without looking at the playhead. Clarify: "The playhead is your scalpel — you would not cut
without looking."
Question 3: "Why did we build the assembly in Session 2 with no music, text or effects?"
Expected answer: Because the story order has to work on its own first. Music and text are decoration — they go on after the
structure is solid.
Common gap: learners want to add music now. Clarify: "Music comes in Session 4. Today is about the cut."
Question 4: "What is a J-cut?" (Give a hint: "It is named after the shape it makes on the timeline.")
Expected answer: A cut where the audio of the next clip starts before the picture — you hear the next speaker before you
see them. It makes dialogue feel natural.
Common gap: learners have never heard the term. Do not worry — this is the session that teaches it. Say: "By the end of
today, you will not only know what it is — you will have used it."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have the assembly and the five actions. Now we add the craft: the named cut types, the J-cut and L-cut, pacing, story
shape and B-roll. By the end of today, your assembly will be a rough cut — and you will be able to explain every cut you
made."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing split vs. trim on the projector before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 A cut is a decision
The idea. Every time two clips meet on the timeline, a cut happens. The cut is the most basic and most powerful tool in editing
— and the one beginners make carelessly.
Plain-English definition. A cut is the moment one clip ends and the next begins. A good cut is chosen; a bad cut is an accident.
Why it matters. The audience feels a bad cut even if they cannot name it — a conversation feels choppy, a story feels
confusing, a moment feels interrupted. A good cut is invisible: the audience simply experiences the story. The goal is not to
make cuts the viewer notices, but to make cuts the viewer feels.
The decision framework (teach this for every cut):
1. Where does the cut land? (On a natural break — a pause, a breath, a movement.)
2. Why there? (Because it feels natural, or because it hides a jump, or because it matches the action.)
3. What type of cut is it? (Straight, jump, cut on action, match, J or L.)
4. Does it earn its place? (If removing it makes no difference, it should not be there.)
Real-life comparison. A cut is like a sentence break in writing. A story with no sentence breaks is exhausting to read. A story
with random breaks in the middle of words is confusing. A story with well-placed breaks flows. The cut is the editor's
punctuation.
Common misconception. "More cuts = more professional." No. A fast-cut action video and a slow interview need completely
different cutting rhythms. The right number of cuts is the number the story needs — no more, no less.
F.2 The five cut types
The idea. Editors have named the common cuts so they can talk about them and choose them deliberately. You need to know
five.
The five cut types (teach with a diagram of each on the board):
1. Straight cut. The simplest cut: one clip ends, the next begins, with no transition. This is the workhorse of editing — 90% of
cuts in most videos are straight cuts. Use them everywhere; they are invisible when chosen well.
2. Jump cut. A cut that jumps forward in time within the same shot — the same person, the same angle, but a chunk of time
removed. Jump cuts feel abrupt by design. They are popular in vlogs and YouTube videos because they create energy and
honesty. Use them on purpose, not by accident.
3. Cut on action. A cut made in the middle of a movement — a door opening, a hand reaching, a person standing up. The
movement hides the cut, so the viewer's eye follows the action and does not notice the edit. This is one of the most useful cuts
in editing.
4. Match cut. A cut that matches a visual element between two shots — a shape, a colour, a movement. For example, a cut
from a round plate to a round clock: the shapes match, so the cut feels clever and satisfying. Match cuts are advanced; use
them sparingly.
5. J-cut and L-cut (the invisible workhorses). These are the cuts that make dialogue feel natural. They are named after the
shape they make on the timeline.
The J-cut and L-cut in detail:
J-cut: The audio of the next clip starts before the picture. You hear the next speaker's voice while still seeing the previous
shot, then the picture changes. The timeline shape looks like a J.
L-cut: The picture of the next clip starts before its audio. You see the next shot while still hearing the previous speaker's
voice. The timeline shape looks like an L.
Why they exist. In real conversation, we often hear a person before we see them, or we keep listening while looking at
something else. J-cuts and L-cuts mimic that natural flow. Without them, dialogue feels like a rigid back-and-forth. With them,
it feels like life.
How to do them in CapCut (desktop):
1. Split the audio from the video on the timeline (right-click the clip → "Separate audio" or use the audio track).
2. For a J-cut: drag the audio of the next clip to the left, so it starts before its video.
3. For an L-cut: drag the video of the next clip to the left, so it starts before its audio.
4. Watch and adjust until the conversation flows.
Real-life comparison. A J-cut is like hearing your friend's voice from the next room before you turn and see them — the
sound leads, the picture follows. An L-cut is like still hearing the TV while you look up at someone — the picture leads, the
sound continues. Both are how our ears and eyes actually work.
Common misconception. "J-cuts and L-cuts are advanced." No. They are simple once you separate the audio. The hard part is
not the technique — it is hearing when a conversation needs one. That is a skill you build by practising.
F.3 Pacing and rhythm — the speed of the story
The idea. Pacing is how fast or slow the video feels. It is controlled by how long each shot stays and how often you cut.
Plain-English definition. Pacing is the rhythm of the edit — the pattern of fast and slow that makes a video feel energetic,
calm, tense or boring.
Why it matters. A video that drags loses the audience. A video that is frantic exhausts them. Good pacing serves the story: fast
for excitement, slow for emotion, steady for information.
The three pacing problems (and their fixes):
Problem What it feels like How to fix it
Dragging The audience is bored; shots stay too long Cut more tightly; remove pauses and dead air; shorten
clips
Frantic The audience feels rushed; they cannot absorb
anything
Hold shots longer; remove unnecessary cuts; let moments
breathe
Uneven Some parts drag, others rush — the video feels
inconsistent
Watch the whole video; adjust section by section to a
steady rhythm
The pacing check method: Watch the whole video once without touching anything. Note where your attention wanders (that
part drags) and where you feel rushed (that part is frantic). Then fix those sections.
Real-life comparison. Pacing is like the tempo of a song. A fuji song has a steady, relaxing tempo. A hip-hop track is faster. A
ballad is slower. A good song changes tempo on purpose — and so does a good video.
Nigerian context. A church sermon edit and a football highlight need completely different pacing. The sermon needs space for
the message; the highlights need energy for the action. The editor serves the story, not a formula.
Common misconception. "Faster is always better for social media." No. Fast cuts can be exhausting; a well-held shot can be
powerful. Match the pacing to the story and the platform.
F.4 Story shape — the invisible structure
The idea. Every good video has a shape: a beginning that hooks, a middle that builds, and an end that pays off. This is true for a
15-second TikTok and a 2-hour film.
Plain-English definition. Story shape is the structure of the video — the order and purpose of its parts.
The short-video story shape (teach this for social media and promos):
Job How long
Hook Stop the viewer in the first 1–3 seconds 1–3 seconds
Beginning Set the context — what is this about? 5–10 seconds
Middle Build the story — the points, the action, the emotion The bulk of the video
End / Payoff Deliver the promise — the result, the call to action 5–10 seconds
The hook — the most important 3 seconds. The hook is the first thing the viewer sees and hears. It must make them want to
stay. Good hooks: a bold question, a surprising image, a strong statement, a problem the viewer has. Bad hooks: a slow logo, a
long intro, "hello everyone."
Why the shape matters. A video with no hook loses the audience immediately. A video with no payoff wastes the audience's
time. The shape is the promise you make and keep.
Real-life comparison. Story shape is like a journey: the hook is the reason to leave home, the middle is the road, the payoff is
the destination. A journey with no reason to leave and no destination is just walking.
Worked example — a 60-second restaurant promo:
Hook (0:0s): A close-up of sizzling food, steam rising — "The smell that brings the whole street."
Beginning (3:0s): The shop exterior and the owner's welcome — "Mama T's Kitchen, your neighbourhood kitchen."
Middle (10:0s): Dishes being prepared, plates served, customers smiling — the food and the people.
End (50:0s): The owner's final line, the order number, the end card — "Call 0803-XXX-XXXX to order."
Common misconception. "Story is only for films." No. A 15-second TikTok has a hook, a middle and a payoff. Structure is not
about length — it is about purpose.
F.5 B-roll — the editor's best friend
The idea. B-roll is supplementary footage that supports the main footage (the A-roll). It covers jumps, adds interest, and shows
what the voice describes.
Plain-English definition. B-roll is any footage that is not the main subject — cutaways of hands, places, objects, reactions,
details.
Why it exists. When you cut a pause out of a talking-head clip, the picture jumps — the person's head suddenly moves. That
jump is ugly. B-roll covers it: you place the B-roll on a track above the main clip, so the viewer sees the cutaway while the audio
continues smoothly.
What B-roll does:
Covers jump cuts — hides the visual jump when a pause is removed.
Adds meaning — shows what the voice describes ("the kitchen" while the owner talks about cooking).
Adds interest — a video of only a talking face gets boring; cutaways keep the eye engaged.
Controls pace — a B-roll shot can be held to slow the video down, or cut quickly to speed it up.
How to use B-roll (the rule): B-roll supports the A-roll; it never competes with it. If the B-roll is more interesting than the main
shot, the main shot should be replaced or shortened.
Real-life comparison. B-roll is like the illustrations in a textbook. The text (the talking head) explains; the pictures (the B-roll)
show. A textbook with only text is dry; a textbook with only pictures has no depth. Together they teach.
Common misconception. "B-roll is filler." No. B-roll with purpose covers jumps and adds meaning. B-roll without purpose —
random shots that show nothing — is filler. Every B-roll shot should earn its place.
F.6 When to cut and when to hold
The idea. Knowing when not to cut is as important as knowing when to cut.
The rules of thumb:
Cut on a natural break — a pause, a breath, the end of a thought, a completed movement.
Hold when the moment matters — a smile, a reaction, an emotion. Cutting too soon kills the feeling.
Cut to remove dead air — pauses that add nothing. The audience's time is precious.
Hold to let the audience absorb — after a key point or a beautiful shot, let it breathe.
The test: After making a cut, ask: "Does this cut earn its place?" If the answer is "I don't know," watch it again. If the cut does
nothing, remove it. If it does something good, keep it.
Real-life comparison. Cutting is like conversation: you wait for a natural pause to speak, you do not interrupt mid-sentence,
and you let a good story breathe before responding.
Common misconception. "If it feels awkward, cut it." Not always. Some of the most powerful moments in video are awkward
— a long silence, a hesitation. Cut on purpose, not on discomfort.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
A cut is a decision — where it lands, why there, what type it is, whether it earns its place.
The five cut types: straight cut, jump cut, cut on action, match cut, and the J-cut/L-cut.
J-cut: audio leads picture. L-cut: picture leads audio. Both make dialogue natural.
Pacing is the rhythm — fix dragging by cutting tighter, fix frantic by holding longer.
Story shape: hook → beginning → middle → payoff. The hook is the most important 3 seconds.
B-roll covers jumps, adds meaning and interest — but it supports, never competes.
Cut on natural breaks; hold when the moment matters.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Turn a raw assembly into a rough cut with deliberate, named cuts — narrating every decision so learners see the craft in
action.
Setup: CapCut open on the projector. A raw assembly (clips in story order, no cuts made). Pack B B-roll available. Cut-type
reference cards on the desk.
Step 1 — Watch and note (2 minutes)
Say: "First, I watch the whole thing without touching it. I note where it drags, where it jumps, where the story is unclear."
Play the assembly. Point out one drag and one jump.
Step 2 — Cut on natural breaks (3 minutes)
Say: "I remove the pause after the intro. I split on both sides of the pause and delete the middle. That is a straight cut —
invisible."
Perform the splits and delete. Point out the cut type: straight cut.
Ask: "Why not use a transition here?" → Because straight cuts are invisible; transitions draw attention.
Step 3 — Cover the jump with B-roll (3 minutes)
Say: "When I removed the pause, the picture jumps. That is ugly. I cover it with B-roll — a shot of the kitchen. The audio
continues smoothly over the B-roll."
Place a B-roll clip from Pack B on the track above the main clip, covering the jump.
Ask: "What does the B-roll do?" → It hides the jump and shows what the voice describes.
Step 4 — A J-cut for dialogue (4 minutes)
Say: "This conversation feels rigid — like a tennis match. I make a J-cut: the audio of the next speaker starts before the
picture. Now it feels like a real conversation."
Separate the audio, drag the next clip's audio to the left so it leads the picture.
Ask: "What shape does this make on the timeline?" → A J.
Step 5 — Fix the pacing (2 minutes)
Say: "The middle drags — this shot is too long. I trim it. The ending feels rushed — I hold the final shot a beat longer."
Trim the long shot; extend the final shot.
Ask: "What changed?" → The middle is tighter; the ending breathes.
Step 6 — Watch the rough cut (1 minute)
Play the full rough cut. It is still rough — no music, no text — but every cut is deliberate.
Say: "That is a rough cut. Every cut is a decision. In your practical, you will do the same."
Expected result: A rough cut with straight cuts, a B-roll cover, a J-cut, and adjusted pacing — the model learners will follow.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why is the straight cut invisible?" (Because the picture simply changes — no effect, no transition.)
"What does the B-roll do?" (Covers the jump and adds meaning.)
"What is the J-cut doing to the conversation?" (Making it feel natural — audio leads picture.)
"How do I know when to hold a shot?" (When the moment matters — a smile, a reaction, an emotion.)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Tunde is a church media volunteer in Port Harcourt. His church records every Sunday service — a two-hour sermon
— and posts a 10-minute highlights video on YouTube. Tunde's current method: he watches the whole service, finds the "best
bits," and cuts them together with straight cuts and a fade between each one. The result is stiff — the sermon feels choppy, the
fades look dated, and viewers stop watching after three minutes.
Relevant facts.
Tunde has all the footage — the full service on a memory card.
He knows CapCut but only uses straight cuts and fades.
The church wants more watch-through — people should stay for the whole highlights video.
Tunde has no B-roll of his own, but the church has photos of the congregation, the choir, and the building.
Decision point. What is Tunde doing wrong — and what should he change?
Possible approaches.
1. Keep the same method but make the fades shorter.
2. Add more transitions to make it look professional.
3. Learn the craft: deliberate cuts, J-cuts for the sermon, B-roll from the church's photos, and pacing that serves the message.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Tunde's problem is not the length of his fades — it is that every cut is the same. A
sermon has natural rhythms: a pause for emphasis, a shift in topic, a moment of emotion. Cutting on those natural breaks,
using J-cuts so the pastor's voice flows over cutaways, and placing B-roll (the choir, the congregation) to cover jumps — these
make the video feel alive, not choppy.
Reasoning / what Tunde would do:
Watch the service once and note the natural breaks and emotional moments.
Cut on those breaks — not on a timer.
Use J-cuts: the pastor's voice continues over a cutaway of the choir or the congregation.
Place B-roll on an upper track to cover jumps.
Hold the moments that matter — the congregation's response, the pastor's key point.
Let the pacing serve the message: slower for reflection, steady for teaching.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: shorter fades are still fades — the video still feels dated and stiff.
Approach 2: more transitions make it worse — transitions draw attention to themselves, not the message.
Approach 3: takes more thought and time, but the result is a video people actually watch — which is the whole point.
Professional connection. A church media volunteer who can turn a two-hour service into a 10-minute video that people watch
to the end is invaluable. The same skill applies to weddings, events and conferences. Tunde can also take this skill to small
businesses — a ₦20,000–₦40,000 job per event video is realistic for a volunteer who builds a reputation.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Cutting mid-word or mid-breath
Symptoms: The dialogue feels jagged; the conversation is uncomfortable to watch. Likely cause: The cut landed in the middle
of a word or breath — the playhead was not on a natural break. How to investigate: Zoom in on the timeline; check the exact
frame of the cut. Corrective steps: Move the playhead to the natural break (end of a word, end of a breath); split again. How to
confirm resolved: The cut lands on a clean break — the conversation flows. Prevention: Cut on natural breaks, not on a timer.
K.2 Using a transition between every clip
Symptoms: The video feels dated and amateur; the transitions draw attention to themselves. Likely cause: A fade or effect was
added between every clip instead of using a straight cut. How to investigate: Watch the video — do you notice the transitions
more than the story? Corrective steps: Replace transitions with straight cuts; save transitions for special moments. How to
confirm resolved: The cuts are invisible — the story flows. Prevention: Straight cuts are the default; transitions are the
exception.
K.3 B-roll that distracts instead of supports
Symptoms: The viewer watches the B-roll and loses the speaker; the B-roll competes with the main shot. Likely cause: The B-
roll is too interesting, too long, or poorly placed. How to investigate: Watch with fresh eyes — where does your attention go?
Corrective steps: Shorten the B-roll; choose less distracting shots; place it only where it supports the audio. How to confirm
resolved: The B-roll supports the A-roll; the voice stays the focus. Prevention: B-roll supports; it never competes.
K.4 No hook — the video starts slowly
Symptoms: Viewers click away in the first 3 seconds. Likely cause: The video starts with a logo, a slow intro, or a long setup.
How to investigate: Watch the first 3 seconds — would a stranger stop for this? Corrective steps: Start with the strongest
moment — a bold image, a question, a surprising fact. How to confirm resolved: The first 3 seconds make a stranger want to
stay. Prevention: The hook is the most important 3 seconds — protect it.
K.5 Ignoring the story shape
Symptoms: The video has no clear beginning, middle or end; it feels like a collection of clips. Likely cause: The clips were
placed without a plan for the story's shape. How to investigate: Can you name the hook, the beginning, the middle and the
payoff? Corrective steps: Reorder the clips to fit the shape; remove clips that do not serve a part. How to confirm resolved:
The video has a clear beginning, middle and end. Prevention: Plan the shape before you cut; every clip should serve a part.
K.6 J-cut or L-cut that is out of sync
Symptoms: The audio leads or lags awkwardly; the conversation feels unnatural. Likely cause: The audio was dragged too far or
not far enough. How to investigate: Watch the transition — does the audio lead or lag smoothly? Corrective steps: Nudge
the audio clip left or right until the transition feels natural. How to confirm resolved: The conversation flows; the J or L shape is
smooth. Prevention: Small adjustments — nudge, watch, nudge again.
K.7 Cutting too much — the video feels frantic
Symptoms: The viewer feels rushed; they cannot absorb anything. Likely cause: Too many cuts, too little holding. How to
investigate: Watch the video — do you feel breathless? Corrective steps: Hold shots longer; remove unnecessary cuts; let
moments breathe. How to confirm resolved: The pacing is steady — the viewer can absorb the story. Prevention: Cut on
purpose, not on impulse.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact frame of the cut, or the B-roll placement). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

A1. A cut is the moment one clip ends and the next begins. It is a decision because the editor chooses exactly where it lands (on
a natural break), why there, and what type it is — rather than letting it happen by accident.
A2. Straight cut, jump cut, cut on action, match cut, J-cut and L-cut. (Accept the five main ones; J-cut and L-cut count as the

A3. (c) Cut on action — the movement hides the cut.

next clip starts before its audio (picture leads audio). Both make dialogue feel natural.
A5. Trim the shots that drag — cut more tightly, remove dead air and shorten clips. The pacing principle: fix dragging by
cutting tighter; fix frantic by holding longer. The goal is a steady rhythm that serves the story.
A6. Hook → Beginning → Middle → End/Payoff.

"B-roll is filler." → Purposeful B-roll covers jumps and adds meaning.
