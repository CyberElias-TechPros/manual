# Effects, Transitions and Motion

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Craft

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first effects and motion work)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5 — learners must have their colour and text pass (from Session 5) with corrected colour, titles, lower thirds and captions. They must be able to do a full audio pass. No prior effects or motion knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 5 you made your video look right — corrected colour, a light grade, titles, lower thirds and captions. Today you make it move right: tasteful transitions, keyframes and a slow zoom, a speed ramp, and a basic motion title. The rule: the best transition is usually no transition. Every effect must pass the test — does this help the story, or only my ego?

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and tested; a footage pack with a mix of still images and video clips (for the slow zoom and speed ramp exercises); a projector; the instructor's own motion pass pre-built to show the finished result; printed "effect justification" cards (a table of effects and when they help or hurt). Instructor note on tone: This is the session where learners are most tempted to overdo it — and the session where discipline matters most. The key message: the best transition is usually no transition, and the best effect is the one the viewer does not notice. Celebrate restraint. A learner who uses two effects well is ahead of one who uses twenty badly.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why the best transition is usually no transition, and identify the tasteful set: straight cut, dissolve, and a sparing whip
or slide.
2. Apply a dissolve between two clips where it helps — and remove it where it hurts.
3. Animate with keyframes — including a slow zoom on a still image or video clip.
4. Create a speed ramp — speeding up and slowing down a clip for dramatic effect.
5. Build a basic motion title — text that moves with purpose.
6. Apply the "does this help the story?" test to every effect — and delete every effect that does not earn its place.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, play a 20-second video twice:
Version 1: The video uses a different transition between every clip — a whip, a spin, a glitch, a zoom, a flash. It feels
chaotic and exhausting.
Version 2: The same video uses straight cuts and one dissolve. It feels calm and professional.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Same video, same clips, same story. Version 1 uses a different transition between every clip. Version 2 uses
straight cuts and one dissolve.
Here is the rule that will save you from the most common beginner mistake: the best transition is usually no transition.
A straight cut is invisible — the viewer simply experiences the story. A transition draws attention to itself. If the viewer is
noticing your transitions, they are not feeling your story.
Today you learn the tasteful set — straight cut, dissolve, and a sparing whip or slide. You learn keyframes, a slow zoom, a
speed ramp and a basic motion title. And you learn the test every effect must pass: does this help the story, or only my
ego? By the end of this session, you will have applied at least two motion techniques — and deleted every effect that does
not earn its place."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "version 1 is too much," "version 2 is calm," "I want to use the cool ones." Accept
all answers. Say: "You already have the taste — you can feel the difference. Today we give that taste a method — and the
discipline to say no."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"Effects are like pepper in food. A little enhances the meal. Too much ruins it. Today you learn to use pepper — and to
know when the meal is better without it."
Why this opener works: It shows the contrast between over-use and restraint, it proves learners have the taste to feel the
difference, and it frames the session as disciplined choice, not button collection.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is the difference between colour correction and colour grading?"
Expected answer: Correction is fixing problems (exposure, white balance, contrast). Grading is styling (creating a mood).
Beginners master fixing first.
Common gap: learners still want to grade everything. Clarify: "Grade only when the story needs it — and only after
correction."
Question 2: "What are safe areas, and why do they matter?"
Expected answer: Safe areas are the parts of the screen not covered by the platform's interface. They matter because text
placed outside them is hidden by buttons and overlays.
Common gap: learners place text wherever it looks nice. Clarify: "Text in the editor is not text on the platform — check the
safe area."
Question 3: "What is a J-cut, and why does it make dialogue feel natural?"
Expected answer: A J-cut is when the audio of the next clip starts before its picture. It makes dialogue natural because it
mimics how we hear conversations.
Common gap: learners remember the shape but not the reason. Clarify: "The technique is simple; the reason is what makes
it professional."
Question 4: "What is the rule about audio and picture?"
Expected answer: "Audiences forgive a soft picture, but they will not watch bad sound." Audio matters more than picture
for watch-through.
Common gap: learners still focus on picture first. Clarify: "The voice carries the message — protect it."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your video now has good cuts, a clear story, balanced audio, corrected colour and readable text. Today we make it move
— with taste. The best transition is usually no transition. Every effect must earn its place. Learn the discipline, and your
video will feel professional."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing correction vs. grading on the
projector before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 The best transition is usually no transition
The idea. A transition is a visual effect between two clips — a fade, a wipe, a spin. The most powerful transition is the one the
viewer does not notice: the straight cut.
Plain-English definition. A transition is a visual effect that connects two clips. A straight cut is the absence of a transition —
one clip simply ends and the next begins.
Why the straight cut is king. A straight cut is invisible. The viewer's eye follows the story, not the edit. A transition — a fade, a
wipe, a spin — draws attention to itself. If the viewer is noticing the transition, they are not feeling the story.
The tasteful set (teach these three):
Transition What it does When to use it
Straight cut No effect — one clip ends, the
next begins
Default — 90% of cuts
Dissolve (crossfade) One clip fades into the next To show time passing, or a gentle change of scene
Whip / slide (sparring) A fast, energetic movement
between clips
For high-energy moments — sports, music, fashion — and
only a few times
The rule: use straight cuts by default. Use a dissolve when the story needs a gentle shift. Use a whip or slide sparingly — a few
times in a video, never between every clip.
Real-life comparison. A straight cut is like turning a page in a book — you do not notice the page turn; you simply continue
the story. A transition is like a page turn with a flashy animation — it is fun once, annoying every time.
Common misconception. "More transitions = more professional." No. A video with a different transition between every clip
looks amateur. A video with straight cuts and one well-placed dissolve looks professional.
F.2 Keyframes — the engine of motion
The idea. A keyframe is a marker that tells the software: "at this moment, the value is this." The software then interpolates
(creates the in-between frames) between keyframes.
Plain-English definition. A keyframe is a point on the timeline where you set a value — position, scale, opacity, rotation. The
software animates smoothly between keyframes.
Why keyframes matter. Keyframes are the mechanism behind almost all motion in editing: zooms, pans, fades, animations.
Once you understand keyframes, you can create any motion effect — and you understand how the software thinks.
The keyframe concept (teach this with a simple example):
At the start of a clip, set a keyframe with scale = 100% (normal size).
At the end of the clip, set a keyframe with scale = 120% (slightly larger).
The software animates between them: the clip slowly zooms in. That is a slow zoom — built from two keyframes.
The three properties you animate most:
Scale — size (zoom in = scale up, zoom out = scale down).
Position — location (move the clip up, down, left, right).
Opacity — transparency (fade in = opacity 0% to 100%, fade out = 100% to 0%).
How to use keyframes in CapCut:
1. Select the clip on the timeline.
2. Move the playhead to where the animation should start.
3. Set the first keyframe (e.g., scale = 100%).
4. Move the playhead to where the animation should end.
5. Set the second keyframe (e.g., scale = 120%).
6. The software animates between them.
Real-life comparison. Keyframes are like giving someone directions: "Start here (keyframe 1). End there (keyframe 2)." The
software drives the route between them. You decide the start and end; the software handles the journey.
Common misconception. "Keyframes are advanced." No. They are simple once you understand the concept: set a value, set
another value, let the software connect them. The hard part is not the technique — it is choosing when motion serves the story.
F.3 The slow zoom — the most useful motion technique
The idea. A slow zoom is a gradual zoom in or out on a still image or video clip. It is the most useful motion technique in
editing — and the easiest.
Plain-English definition. A slow zoom is a gradual change in scale over time — the clip slowly gets bigger (zoom in) or smaller
(zoom out).
Why it exists. A still image with no motion is dead. A slow zoom gives it life — the viewer's eye moves, the image feels
present. A slow zoom on a video clip adds emphasis — drawing the viewer's attention to the subject.
When to use it:
On a still image — to make it feel alive.
On a video clip — to add emphasis or emotion.
At the start of a video — to draw the viewer in.
At the end of a video — to create a sense of closure.
How to create a slow zoom (the keyframe method):
1. Select the clip on the timeline.
2. At the start, set a keyframe with scale = 100%.
3. At the end, set a keyframe with scale = 110–120% (a subtle zoom — not a dramatic one).
4. The software animates the slow zoom.
The rule: a slow zoom is slow — 10–20% over the length of the clip. A fast zoom looks like a mistake; a slow zoom feels
intentional.
Real-life comparison. A slow zoom is like leaning in to listen — you move closer to what matters. A fast zoom is like jumping
toward the speaker — it startles. Lean in; do not jump.
Common misconception. "A bigger zoom is more dramatic." No. A subtle zoom (10–20%) is elegant. A big zoom (50%+)
looks like a mistake. Less is more.
F.4 Speed ramps — controlling time
The idea. A speed ramp is a change in playback speed within a single clip — speeding up, then slowing down (or the reverse).
Plain-English definition. A speed ramp is a gradual change in the playback speed of a clip — for example, starting fast, then
slowing to normal speed at the key moment.
Why it exists. A speed ramp controls the viewer's experience of time. Speeding up creates energy; slowing down creates
emphasis. A well-placed speed ramp makes a moment feel dramatic.
When to use it:
To emphasise a key moment — slow down as the action peaks.
To compress time — speed up a long, uneventful section.
To create energy — speed up a sequence of quick cuts.
How to create a speed ramp in CapCut:
1. Select the clip on the timeline.
2. Use the speed tool to change the playback speed.
3. For a ramp, split the clip into sections and set different speeds for each — or use the curve speed tool if available.
4. The transition between speeds should be gradual (a ramp), not abrupt.
The rule: a speed ramp should feel intentional — the viewer should feel the change, not be confused by it. Use it sparingly —
a few times in a video, not in every clip.
Real-life comparison. A speed ramp is like a drummer's roll — the beat speeds up, then slows to a stop at the key moment. It
is dramatic because it is controlled. A constant fast tempo is just noise.
Common misconception. "Speed ramps are for action videos only." No. A slow-down on a speaker's key line can be powerful. A
speed-up on a preparation sequence can compress time elegantly. Match the ramp to the story.
F.5 Motion titles — text that moves with purpose
A motion title is text that animates — it slides, fades, or scales onto the screen. It is more engaging than static text — but only
when the motion serves the message.
Plain-English definition. A motion title is a title or text element that animates onto (or off) the screen — using keyframes for
position, opacity or scale.
Why it exists. Static text is functional. Motion text is engaging — it draws the eye and adds energy. But motion text that is too
flashy distracts from the message.
The rules of motion titles:
1. The motion serves the message. A gentle fade or slide is professional; a spinning, bouncing title is a distraction.
2. Keep it short. A motion title is 2–4 seconds — long enough to read, short enough to not annoy.
3. Time it to the story. The title appears when the story is ready for it — not before.
4. Use keyframes. Animate position, opacity or scale — not all three at once.
How to build a basic motion title in CapCut:
1. Add a text element to the timeline.
2. At the start, set a keyframe with opacity = 0% (invisible) and position = below the screen.
3. A second later, set a keyframe with opacity = 100% and position = centre.
4. The title fades in and slides up — a simple, professional motion title.
Real-life comparison. A motion title is like a shopkeeper opening the store — the sign appears when the store is ready, not
before, and it does not spin wildly to get attention. It appears, clearly, and lets the customer in.
Common misconception. "More motion = more professional." No. A gentle, well-timed motion title is professional. A chaotic
one is a distraction. The motion serves the message — never the reverse.
F.6 The "does this help the story?" test
The idea. Every effect, transition and motion technique must pass one test: does this help the story, or only my ego?
Plain-English definition. The story test is a question you ask before keeping any effect: does this effect help the viewer
understand or feel the story — or does it just show off the editor's tools?
How to apply the test:
1. Add the effect.
2. Watch the video.
3. Ask: "Does this help the story?"
4. If yes — keep it. If no — delete it.
5. If you are not sure — delete it. (When in doubt, cut it out.)
The three categories of effects:
Category What it is What to do
Helps the story A dissolve showing time passing; a slow zoom on a key moment; a speed ramp on
the action peak
Keep it
Neutral A straight cut; a gentle fade Keep it (it is
invisible)
Hurts the story A spin transition; a glitch effect; a chaotic motion title Delete it
Real-life comparison. The story test is like the market trader's question: "Does this spice help the food, or am I just showing
off my pepper?" A good cook adds spice that enhances the meal. A bad cook adds spice to show off — and the meal suffers.
Common misconception. "If I made the effect, I should use it." No. The effect is a tool, not a trophy. If it does not serve the
story, it is clutter. Delete it without mercy.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
The best transition is usually no transition — straight cuts are invisible.
The tasteful set: straight cut (default), dissolve (gentle shifts), whip/slide (sparring, high energy).
Keyframes are markers that set values; the software animates between them.
A slow zoom (10–20%) brings stills to life and adds emphasis.
A speed ramp controls time — speed up for energy, slow down for emphasis.
A motion title animates with purpose — the motion serves the message.
The story test: does this help the story, or only my ego? If unsure, delete it.

    B[Keyframe zoom] --> D

    D --> E[Engaging edit]

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Do a full motion pass — a dissolve, a slow zoom, a speed ramp and a motion title — narrating every decision and
applying the story test.
Setup: CapCut open on the projector. A rough cut ready. Still images and video clips available. Effect justification cards on the
desk.
Step 1 — The straight cut default (2 minutes)
Say: "Most of my cuts are straight cuts — invisible. I only add a transition when the story needs it. Watch: this cut is a
straight cut. The viewer does not notice it — they simply continue the story."
Point out a straight cut on the timeline.
Ask: "Why is the straight cut invisible?" → Because there is no effect — one clip simply ends and the next begins.
Step 2 — A dissolve for time passing (3 minutes)
Say: "Here the story jumps forward — from morning to evening. A dissolve shows time passing gently. I add a dissolve
between the two clips."
Add a dissolve. Show the result.
Ask: "Why a dissolve here?" → Because the story needs to show time passing — a dissolve is the gentle way to do it.
Step 3 — A slow zoom on a still image (3 minutes)
Say: "This still image is dead — no motion. I add a slow zoom: 100% to 115% over the clip. Now it feels alive."
Set the keyframes for the slow zoom. Show the result.
Ask: "Why 100% to 115%?" → Because a subtle zoom is elegant; a big zoom looks like a mistake.
Step 4 — A speed ramp on the action (4 minutes)
Say: "This is the key moment — the reveal. I speed up the preparation, then slow down at the reveal. The speed ramp
controls time."
Split the clip and set different speeds. Show the result.
Ask: "Why slow down at the reveal?" → Because slowing down creates emphasis — the viewer feels the moment.
Step 5 — A motion title (3 minutes)
Say: "I add a motion title — it fades in and slides up. Gentle, professional. The motion serves the message."
Add the motion title with keyframes. Show the result.
Ask: "Why not a spinning title?" → Because a spinning title distracts from the message.
Expected result: A video with straight cuts, one dissolve, a slow zoom, a speed ramp and a motion title — every effect justified
by the story.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why is the straight cut the default?" → Because it is invisible.
"When do you use a dissolve?" → When the story needs to show time passing or a gentle shift.
"What is a keyframe?" → A marker that sets a value; the software animates between keyframes.
"What is the story test?" → Does this help the story, or only my ego?

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner does a full motion pass on their edit — applying at least two
motion techniques and deleting every effect that does not earn its place.
Starting materials: The learner's edit open in CapCut; still images and video clips available; Worksheet 6.1 (Effect Justification
Log) and 6.2 (Motion Pass Checklist).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Review your edit: watch it once. Note where motion could help — a still image, a key moment, a title.
2. Add a dissolve where the story shows time passing or a gentle shift.
3. Add a slow zoom (100% to 115%) on one still image or video clip.
4. Add a speed ramp on one key moment — speed up, then slow down.
5. Add a motion title — fade in and slide up, gentle and professional.
6. Apply the story test: for each effect, ask "does this help the story?" — delete any that do not.
7. Log your effects: fill in Worksheet 6.1 — effect, where used, does it help the story?, reason.
8. Complete Worksheet 6.2: check every motion technique — keyframes used, slow zoom subtle, speed ramp intentional,
motion title professional.
Expected result: A video with at least two motion techniques, every effect justified, and completed worksheets.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 6: "Which effect did you delete — and why?" (If they deleted none, ask: "Are you sure every effect earns its
place?")
After step 7: "Can you explain each effect in one sentence?" (If not, the effect may be accidental.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Dissolve looks like a mistake → Check it is placed where the story shifts — not randomly.
Slow zoom too fast → Extend the keyframes further apart; the zoom should be gradual.
Speed ramp is abrupt → Use a curve or split the clip into more sections for a gradual ramp.
Motion title too flashy → Simplify: fade and slide only, no spinning or bouncing.
Too many effects → Apply the story test ruthlessly — delete until only the essential remain.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Edit reviewed; motion opportunities noted
Dissolve added where the story shifts
Slow zoom added (100% to 115%)
Speed ramp added on a key moment
Motion title added (fade and slide)
Story test applied — weak effects deleted
Effect Justification Log completed
Motion Pass Checklist completed

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Kelechi is a freelance editor in Port Harcourt. His first client — a gym owner — asks for a 30-second promo. Kelechi
is excited and uses every transition he knows: a whip, a spin, a glitch, a zoom, a flash — between every clip. He adds a spinning
title and a speed ramp on every clip. The client watches the video and says: "It is too much. I cannot even hear what you are
saying. Can you make it calm?"
Relevant facts.
Kelechi's footage is good — clear shots of the gym, people training, the owner speaking.
The transitions are so frequent that the viewer cannot focus.
The spinning title distracts from the gym's name.
The speed ramps on every clip make the video feel chaotic.
The client's main request — "show the gym and give the address" — is lost in the effects.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should Kelechi do differently?
Possible approaches.
1. Use even more effects — the client just does not understand good editing.
2. Remove all effects and make the video completely static.
3. Apply the story test: keep only the effects that help the story, delete the rest.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Kelechi's problem is not that he knows effects — it is that he used them all. The story
test would have saved him: a straight cut between most clips, one dissolve where the story shifts, a slow zoom on the gym's
logo, a calm title with the gym's name. The client wants to see the gym and hear the address — not watch a effects showcase.
Reasoning / what Kelechi would do:
Replace most transitions with straight cuts.
Keep one dissolve where the story shifts.
Add a slow zoom on the gym's logo.
Replace the spinning title with a calm fade-in.
Remove the speed ramps except one — on the most energetic moment.
The result: a video that shows the gym, gives the address, and feels professional.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: more effects make the problem worse — the client already said "too much."
Approach 2: removing all effects makes the video flat — motion, used well, is engaging.
Approach 3: the story test keeps the effects that help and removes the ones that hurt — the video feels professional and
the client's message is clear.
Professional connection. Kelechi's first client could lead to many more — a gym, a salon, a shop, a church. But only if the first
video is professional. An editor who applies the story test — who uses effects with discipline — builds a reputation. An editor
who overdoes it loses clients. The difference is not skill — it is judgement. And judgement is what this session teaches.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 A different transition between every clip
Symptoms: The video feels chaotic and exhausting; the viewer cannot focus on the story. Likely cause: A transition was added
between every clip instead of using straight cuts. How to investigate: Watch the video — are there more transitions than
straight cuts? Corrective steps: Replace most transitions with straight cuts; keep only the ones the story needs. How to
confirm resolved: The video feels calm; transitions are rare and purposeful. Prevention: Straight cuts are the default;
transitions are the exception.
K.2 Over-using keyframes — everything moves
Symptoms: The video feels busy; the viewer does not know where to look. Likely cause: Keyframes were added to every clip —
zooms, pans, fades everywhere. How to investigate: Watch the video — does everything move? Corrective steps: Remove
keyframes from clips that do not need motion; keep only the essential. How to confirm resolved: Motion is rare and
purposeful. Prevention: Motion serves the story — not every clip needs it.
K.3 A slow zoom that is too fast or too big
Symptoms: The zoom looks like a mistake — too sudden or too dramatic. Likely cause: The keyframes were too close together
(too fast) or the scale change was too large (too big). How to investigate: Watch the zoom — is it gradual and subtle?
Corrective steps: Move the keyframes further apart; reduce the scale change to 10–20%. How to confirm resolved: The
zoom is slow and subtle. Prevention: A slow zoom is 10–20% over the length of the clip — no more.
K.4 A speed ramp that is abrupt
Symptoms: The speed change feels jarring — the viewer is confused. Likely cause: The speed changed instantly instead of
ramping gradually. How to investigate: Watch the ramp — is the change gradual? Corrective steps: Use a curve or split the
clip into more sections for a gradual ramp. How to confirm resolved: The speed change is smooth and intentional.
Prevention: A ramp is gradual — the viewer should feel the change, not be shocked by it.
K.5 A motion title that distracts
Symptoms: The title spins, bounces or flashes — the viewer watches the animation, not the message. Likely cause: The title
animation is too complex or too flashy. How to investigate: Watch the title — does the animation serve the message?
Corrective steps: Simplify — fade in and slide up, nothing more. How to confirm resolved: The title is calm and readable.
Prevention: The motion serves the message — gentle and professional.
K.6 Keeping an effect because it was hard to make
Symptoms: The video has an effect that does not help the story — but the learner keeps it because they spent time on it. Likely
cause: The learner is attached to the effect, not the story. How to investigate: Ask: "Does this help the story, or only your ego?"
Corrective steps: Delete it. The time is spent; the story is what matters. How to confirm resolved: Every effect earns its place.
Prevention: The story test is strict — if it does not help, delete it.
K.7 No motion at all — the video is static
Symptoms: The video feels flat and dead — especially if it uses still images. Likely cause: No motion techniques were applied.
How to investigate: Watch the video — do still images feel dead? Corrective steps: Add a slow zoom to still images; add a
motion title; add a dissolve where the story shifts. How to confirm resolved: The video feels alive but not chaotic. Prevention:
Motion, used well, brings a video to life.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the timeline with the transitions, or the keyframe settings). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. The best transition is usually no transition — because a straight cut is invisible; the viewer simply experiences the story. A
transition draws attention to itself, which pulls the viewer out of the story.
A2. Straight cut (default), dissolve (gentle shifts), whip/slide (sparring, high energy).
A3. (b) A marker that sets a value at a moment; the software animates between keyframes.
A4. A slow zoom is a gradual change in scale over time — the clip slowly gets bigger or smaller. The rule: 10–20% over the

bouncing title distracts from the message; a calm title communicates it.

no — or if you are not sure — delete it.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner uses straight cuts by default and adds transitions sparingly.
Learner uses keyframes to create a slow zoom, speed ramp or motion title.
Learner applies the story test and deletes effects that do not earn their place.
Learner's slow zoom is subtle (10–20%).
Learner's speed ramp is gradual and intentional.
Learner's motion title is calm and professional.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"More transitions = more professional." → Straight cuts are invisible; transitions draw attention.
"Keyframes are advanced." → They are simple: set values, let the software animate.
"A bigger zoom is more dramatic." → A subtle zoom is elegant; a big zoom looks like a mistake.
"If I made the effect, I should use it." → The effect is a tool, not a trophy.
"Motion on every clip." → Motion serves the story — not every clip needs it.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Transition drill: give a 5-clip edit; the learner replaces most transitions with straight cuts and keeps only one dissolve —
and explains why.
2. Keyframe drill: the learner creates a slow zoom on a still image using two keyframes — and explains the concept.
3. Story test drill: give an edit with 10 effects; the learner deletes the ones that do not help the story and justifies each
deletion.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide an edit with the clips already placed; the learner adds a slow zoom and a motion title.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full motion pass plus independent exercise.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to build a 30-second edit using only motion techniques (no transitions) — slow zooms,
speed ramps and a motion title — and explain how motion alone can carry the video.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners applying motion techniques and applying the story test (Sections H and the
assignment). If the room is slow, shorten the case study (Section J) to a 3-minute mention and limit the independent exercise to
one motion technique. Protect the practical time — a learner who leaves without a motion pass and a justification log has not
built the motion skill that Sessions 7–9 depend on.
