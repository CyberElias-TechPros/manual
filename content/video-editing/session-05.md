# Colour, Text and Graphics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Craft

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first colour and text work)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–4 — learners must have their rough cut with balanced audio (from Session 4). They must be able to set up a project, use the five core actions and do a basic audio pass. No prior colour or design knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 you made your video sound right. Today you make it look right: colour correction (exposure, contrast, white balance), matching clips of the same scene, a light grade, and then text and graphics — titles, lower thirds and captions that are correctly spelled, timed and inside safe areas. The rule: correction is fixing, grading is styling — and beginners master fixing first.

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and tested; a footage pack with mixed-colour clips (some dark, some warm, some cool) prepared and verified; a projector; printed safe-area diagrams (16:9, 9:16, 1:1, 4:5); the instructor's own colour pass pre-built to show the finished result; optional: DaVinci Resolve free version installed on the instructor's computer for the 5-minute first look (not required for learners). Instructor note on tone: This session is where editing starts to look "professional" to the untrained eye — and learners will be tempted to overdo it. The key message: fix first, style second, and less is more. A natural, corrected video beats a heavily graded one every time. Keep the class disciplined about taste.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain the difference between colour correction (fixing) and colour grading (styling), and why beginners master fixing first.
2. Correct exposure and white balance on a clip — making a dark clip brighter and a warm/cool clip neutral.
3. Match two clips of the same scene so they look like they belong together.
4. Apply a light, tasteful grade that suits the story — without destroying the natural look.
5. Create readable titles and lower thirds that are correctly spelled, well placed and timed.
6. Add captions that are correctly spelled, timed to the speech, and inside safe areas on every platform.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show the same 10-second clip three times:
Version 1 (uncorrected): The clip is too dark and too orange — the speaker's face is murky and the colours look sickly.
Version 2 (corrected): The clip is bright, neutral and natural — the speaker's face is clear and the colours look real.
Version 3 (over-graded): The clip has a heavy teal-and-orange grade — it looks like a Hollywood trailer, not a real video.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Same clip, same footage. Version 1 is what the camera recorded — too dark, too orange. Version 2 is
corrected — bright, neutral, natural. Version 3 is over-graded — it looks like a movie trailer.
Here is the rule for today: correction is fixing, grading is styling — and beginners master fixing first. Version 2 is the
goal. Version 3 is what happens when you style before you learn to fix.
Today you learn to make your video look right: fix the exposure, fix the white balance, match your clips, and add a light
grade if the story needs it. Then you add text — titles, lower thirds and captions — that are correctly spelled, timed and
inside safe areas. By the end of this session, your video will look intentional."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "version 2 looks real," "version 3 is too much," "how do I fix the orange?" Accept
all answers. Say: "You already have the eye — you can see the difference. Today we give that eye a method."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"Colour is not magic — it is three adjustments: exposure, contrast and white balance. Text is not decoration — it is
communication with rules. Learn both, and your video will look professional."
Why this opener works: It shows the three states (uncorrected, corrected, over-graded), it proves learners have the eye to tell
them apart, and it frames the session as disciplined fixing, not wild styling.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is ducking, and why do we do it?"
Expected answer: Ducking is lowering the music volume when someone is speaking, so the voice is always clear. We do it so
the music supports the voice without fighting it.
Common gap: learners confuse ducking with noise reduction. Clarify: "Ducking is music under voice; noise reduction is
removing background hiss."
Question 2: "What are the four parts of the short-video story shape?"
Expected answer: Hook, beginning, middle, payoff.
Common gap: learners forget the payoff. Clarify: "The payoff is the promise kept — the result or call to action."
Question 3: "What is the difference between a J-cut and an L-cut?"
Expected answer: A J-cut is when the audio of the next clip starts before its picture. An L-cut is when the picture of the next
clip starts before its audio. Both make dialogue feel natural.
Common gap: learners remember the shapes but not which leads. Clarify: "J = audio leads. L = picture leads."
Question 4: "What is loudness, and how do you check it?"
Expected answer: Loudness is the overall volume of the finished video. You check it by meter (the voice should peak around
- 6 dB, nothing distorting) and by ear.
Common gap: learners think loudness is the same as volume. Clarify: "Volume is one clip; loudness is the whole mix."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your video now has good cuts, a clear story and balanced audio. Today we make it look right — and add the words that
make it communicate. Colour first, then text. Fix first, style second. Less is more."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing ducking on the projector before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Correction vs. grading — fixing vs. styling
The idea. Colour correction is fixing problems — making a dark clip brighter, a warm clip neutral. Colour grading is styling —
giving the video a mood or look. Beginners master fixing first.
Plain-English definitions:
Colour correction — adjusting the picture so it looks natural and consistent: bright enough, neutral colours, no unwanted
tint.
Colour grading — adjusting the picture to create a mood: warm and golden for nostalgia, cool and blue for tension, teal-
and-orange for a cinematic look.
Why the distinction matters. A beginner who grades before correcting is painting a wall that is still cracked. The grade cannot
fix a dark, orange clip — it can only make a dark, orange clip look dramatically dark and orange. Fix first, then style — and
only if the story needs it.
The rule (say it with the class):
"Correction is fixing, grading is styling — and beginners master fixing first."
Real-life comparison. Correction is like cleaning a room — removing the dust, straightening the chairs. Grating is like
decorating the room — choosing the curtains and the wall colour. You clean before you decorate, and a clean room with no
decoration is still better than a decorated room that is dirty.
Common misconception. "Grading makes it look professional." No. A natural, corrected video looks professional. A heavily
graded video looks amateur — unless the grade is done by an experienced colourist for a specific story purpose.
F.2 The three correction adjustments
The idea. Every colour correction is built from three adjustments. Master these and you can fix most clips.
The three adjustments (teach them in this order):
1. Exposure (brightness). How light or dark the whole clip is. If a clip is too dark — the speaker's face is murky, the details are
lost — raise the exposure. If it is too bright — the sky is white, the highlights are blown — lower it.
The rule: the subject's face should be clearly visible, with detail in the shadows and no pure white areas.
2. Contrast. The difference between the dark and light parts of the picture. Low contrast looks flat and washed out; high
contrast looks punchy but can lose detail.
The rule: increase contrast slightly to make the picture "pop" — but not so much that the shadows go pure black or the
highlights go pure white.
3. White balance (colour temperature). Whether the clip looks warm (orange) or cool (blue). A clip shot under indoor lights
often looks orange; a clip shot in the shade often looks blue.
The rule: white objects should look white — not orange, not blue. Adjust the temperature until the colours look natural.
How to apply them in CapCut:
1. Select the clip on the timeline.
2. Open the colour tools (usually "Adjust" or "Colour").
3. Find the exposure, contrast and temperature (or "tint") sliders.
4. Adjust while watching the preview — fix exposure first, then white balance, then contrast.
5. Compare with the uncorrected version to check you have not overdone it.
Real-life comparison. The three adjustments are like cooking a pot of soup: exposure is how much soup there is (too little =
hungry, too much = overflowing), contrast is how rich it is (watery vs. thick), and white balance is the seasoning (too salty =
orange, too bland = blue). Adjust each until it tastes right.
Common misconception. "I can fix any clip." No. If a clip is very dark, very noisy or very blurry, correction can improve it — but
the result is limited by what was captured. The best fix is a good recording.
F.3 Matching clips — making one scene look like one scene
The idea. When you cut between two clips of the same scene (for example, two camera angles of the same interview), the
colours may not match — one is warmer, one is darker. Matching means adjusting them so they look like they belong
together.
Plain-English definition. Matching (or "shot matching") is adjusting the colour of two or more clips of the same scene so the
cut between them is invisible.
Why it exists. When the colours jump at a cut, the viewer notices — the scene feels broken. When the colours match, the cut is
invisible and the scene feels whole.
The matching method:
1. Place the two clips on the timeline, one after the other.
2. Watch the cut — do the colours jump?
3. Select the second clip and adjust its exposure, contrast and temperature until it matches the first.
4. Watch the cut again — is it invisible?
5. If the difference is too large, consider using a different clip — some shots simply cannot be matched.
Real-life comparison. Matching is like buying two pots of paint for the same wall. If one pot is slightly darker, you adjust it
until the two walls look like one. If the difference is too great, no amount of adjusting will hide the join.
Common misconception. "I can match any two clips." No. If the lighting is completely different (one indoors, one outdoors),
matching is impossible — and the cut should be hidden with a B-roll cover instead.
F.4 A light grade — styling with restraint
The idea. A grade is a stylistic adjustment that gives the video a mood. A light grade is subtle — it enhances without shouting.
Plain-English definition. Grading is adjusting the colours to create a mood: warm for nostalgia, cool for calm, slightly
desaturated for seriousness.
When to grade:
When the story has a clear mood (a warm family video, a cool tech product).
When you have already corrected everything — grade on top of correction.
When the client asks for a specific look.
When not to grade:
When the clips are not yet corrected.
When the story is neutral (a tutorial, an interview).
When you are not sure — a natural corrected video is always safe.
How to apply a light grade in CapCut:
1. Correct all clips first (exposure, white balance, contrast).
2. Apply a subtle adjustment to the whole video — for example, a slight warmth (+5 to +10 temperature) or a slight
desaturation (-5 to -10 saturation).
3. Watch the whole video — does it feel natural? If it looks "filtered," you have overdone it.
Real-life comparison. A light grade is like a light spice in food — it enhances the flavour without overwhelming it. Heavy
grading is like dumping in a whole bottle of pepper — it drowns everything else.
Common misconception. "Grading is what professionals do, so I should grade everything." No. Professionals grade selectively
— when the story needs it. A beginner who grades everything learns bad habits.
F.5 Titles and lower thirds — the words that communicate
The idea. Titles and lower thirds are text overlays that communicate information: the name of the video, the name of a
speaker, a location, a price.
Plain-English definitions:
Title — the main text, usually at the start or end: the name of the video or business.
Lower third — text in the lower third of the screen, usually introducing a speaker: their name and role.
Why they exist. Titles tell the viewer what they are watching. Lower thirds tell them who is speaking. Both make the video feel
professional and help the viewer follow the story.
The five rules of good text (teach these exactly):
1. Spell correctly. A misspelt title destroys trust. Always proofread.
2. Use a readable font. Sans-serif fonts (clean, simple) are easiest to read. Avoid decorative fonts for important information.
3. Keep it short. A title is 2–5 words. A lower third is a name and a role — nothing more.
4. Place it inside safe areas. (See F.6.) Text must not be covered by the platform's interface.
5. Time it well. A title appears for 2–4 seconds. A lower third appears when the speaker starts and disappears when they
finish.
Real-life comparison. A title is like the signboard of a shop — it tells you what the shop sells. A lower third is like a name
badge — it tells you who is speaking. Both are simple, clear and necessary.
Common misconception. "More text = more professional." No. Too much text clutters the screen and competes with the video.
The best text is the minimum that communicates.
F.6 Safe areas — where text survives the platform
The idea. Safe areas are the parts of the screen that are not covered by the platform's interface — the play button, the
progress bar, the username, the caption.
Plain-English definition. The safe area is the central region of the screen where text will always be visible — not covered by
buttons, icons or overlays.
Why it exists. Every platform puts its interface on top of the video: the play button, the progress bar, the likes, the username. If
your text sits under those elements, it is unreadable.

The practical method: In CapCut, turn on the safe area guide (usually in the preview settings). It shows a grid — keep all text
inside the inner grid.
Real-life comparison. Safe areas are like the clear space around a road sign. A sign planted in the bushes is useless. A sign in
the clear space is seen by everyone. Text in the safe area is seen; text outside it is hidden.
Common misconception. "I will place text wherever it looks nice." No. Text that looks nice in the editor but sits under the
platform's play button is invisible to the viewer. Always check the safe area.
F.7 Captions — the words everyone needs
The idea. Captions are the text of what is being said, placed on the screen as the video plays. They are for everyone — not just
the deaf.
Plain-English definition. Captions are a text transcription of the audio, timed to appear as the words are spoken.
Why they exist:
Many people watch with the sound off — on buses, at work, in church.
Captions improve comprehension — for people who are hard of hearing, or who speak a different first language.
Captions improve watch-through — platforms report higher completion rates for captioned videos.
Captions are standard, not optional — this course teaches them as a core skill.
The five rules of good captions:
1. Spell correctly. A misspelt caption is worse than no caption.
2. Time them accurately. Each caption appears when the word is spoken and disappears after.
3. Keep them short. One line, or two short lines at most. Do not fill the screen with text.
4. Place them inside safe areas. (See F.6.)
5. Use a readable style. White text with a black outline (or shadow) is readable on any background.
How to add captions in CapCut:
1. Use the auto-captions feature (if available) to generate a first draft.
2. Proofread the draft carefully — auto-captions make mistakes, especially with accents and names.
3. Adjust the timing — nudge captions that appear too early or too late.
4. Style them — readable font, white text with outline, inside safe areas.
Real-life comparison. Captions are like subtitles in a Nollywood film — they let everyone follow the story, regardless of the
language or the noise in the room. A video without captions excludes everyone who cannot hear it clearly.
Common misconception. "Captions are only for deaf people." No. Captions are for the bus, the office, the church, the noisy
market — and for anyone who reads faster than they listen. Most viewers under 30 watch with captions at least sometimes.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Correction is fixing; grading is styling — master fixing first.
The three corrections: exposure (brightness), contrast, white balance (temperature).
Matching makes two clips of the same scene look like one scene.
A light grade styles with restraint — only after correction, only if the story needs it.
Titles and lower thirds communicate — spelled correctly, short, readable, timed well, inside safe areas.
Safe areas are where text survives the platform interface.
Captions are for everyone — spelled correctly, timed accurately, short, inside safe areas.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Do a full colour and text pass — correction, matching, a light grade, titles, lower thirds and captions — narrating every
decision.
Setup: CapCut open on the projector. A rough cut ready. Mixed-colour clips available. Safe-area diagrams on the board.
Step 1 — Correct a dark, orange clip (3 minutes)
Say: "This clip is too dark and too orange. I fix exposure first — raise it until the face is clear. Then white balance — adjust
the temperature until white looks white. Then a touch of contrast."
Apply the adjustments. Show the before and after.
Ask: "Why exposure first?" → Because brightness affects everything else.
Step 2 — Match two clips (2 minutes)
Say: "These two angles of the same interview do not match — one is warmer. I adjust the second until the cut is invisible."
Apply the matching adjustment.
Ask: "What if the difference is too great?" → Use a different clip or hide the cut with B-roll.
Step 3 — Apply a light grade (2 minutes)
Say: "This is a warm family video. I apply a slight warmth — just enough to feel cosy, not so much that it looks filtered."
Apply the grade. Show the result.
Ask: "How do I know if I overdid it?" → If it looks filtered, you overdid it.
Step 4 — Add a title and a lower third (4 minutes)
Say: "I add a title at the start — the business name, short and spelled correctly. Then a lower third when the speaker
appears — their name and role. Both inside the safe area."
Add the title and lower third. Show the safe-area guide.
Ask: "Why inside the safe area?" → Because the platform's interface covers the edges.
Step 5 — Add captions (4 minutes)
Say: "I generate captions with the auto feature, then proofread — the auto-captions made two mistakes with the name. I
fix the spelling and nudge the timing. White text with a black outline, inside the safe area."
Generate, proofread and style the captions.
Ask: "Why proofread?" → Because auto-captions make mistakes — and a misspelt caption destroys trust.
Expected result: A corrected, matched, lightly graded video with a title, a lower third and accurate captions — the model
learners will follow.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"Why fix before grade?" → Because grading cannot fix a broken clip.
"What are the three corrections?" → Exposure, contrast, white balance.
"Why proofread captions?" → Auto-captions make mistakes.
"Why safe areas?" → The platform's interface covers the edges.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding step by step, each learner does a full colour and text pass on their edit.
Starting materials: The learner's edit open in CapCut; mixed-colour clips available; safe-area diagrams; Worksheet 5.1 (Colour
Correction Log) and 5.2 (Text and Caption Check).
Procedure (instructor calls out each numbered step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Correct a dark clip: raise exposure until the face is clear; adjust white balance until colours are neutral; add a touch of
contrast.
2. Correct a warm or cool clip: adjust the temperature until white objects look white.
3. Match two clips: adjust the second clip until the cut between them is invisible.
4. Log your corrections: fill in Worksheet 5.1 — clip, problem, adjustment, result.
5. Apply a light grade (optional, if the story has a clear mood): a slight warmth or coolness — subtle.
6. Add a title: the business name, short, spelled correctly, inside safe areas, 2–4 seconds.
7. Add a lower third: the speaker's name and role, inside safe areas, timed to their appearance.
8. Add captions: generate with auto-captions, proofread for spelling, adjust timing, style with white text and outline.
9. Complete Worksheet 5.2: check every text element — spelled correctly, timed, inside safe areas, readable.
Expected result: A corrected, matched, lightly graded video with a title, a lower third and accurate captions — and completed
worksheets.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 4: "What did you adjust, and why?" (If they cannot say, the correction is accidental.)
After step 8: "Did the auto-captions make any mistakes?" (Almost always yes — this is why proofreading matters.)
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Clip still dark after raising exposure → Check the original — if it is very dark, the detail may not exist.
Colours look worse after white balance → Reset and adjust temperature in smaller steps.
Grade looks filtered → Reduce the adjustment; less is more.
Title covered by the platform interface → Move it inside the safe area.
Captions out of sync → Nudge them left or right until they match the speech.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Dark clip corrected (exposure, white balance, contrast)
Warm/cool clip corrected (temperature)
Two clips matched
Colour Correction Log completed
Light grade applied (if appropriate) — subtle
Title added (spelled correctly, inside safe areas, timed)
Lower third added (name, role, inside safe areas, timed)
Captions added (proofread, timed, styled, inside safe areas)
Text and Caption Check completed

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)

decisions and explaining them.
Scenario. You are editing a 30-second product video for a Port Harcourt phone-accessory shop. The footage includes: a clip
shot under orange shop lights (too warm), a clip shot in the shade outside (too cool), and a clip that is correctly exposed. The

Requirements:
1. Import the clips into a new CapCut project set to 9:16 (for Reels).
2. Correct the warm clip (temperature) and the cool clip (temperature).
3. Match the corrected clips so they look consistent.
4. Add a title with the shop name and a lower third introducing the owner.
5. Add captions for the owner's key sentence — proofread for spelling.
6. Complete a Colour Correction Log (Worksheet 5.1) for every adjustment.
7. Check every text element against safe areas for 9:16.
Time allowance: 15 minutes.
Success criteria:
Warm and cool clips corrected (temperature adjusted).
Clips matched for consistency.
Title and lower third added — spelled correctly, timed, inside safe areas.
Captions added — proofread, timed, inside safe areas.
Colour Correction Log completed.
Hints if stuck:
Hint 1: The warm clip needs a cooler temperature; the cool clip needs a warmer temperature — adjust until white objects
look white.
Hint 2: For Reels, keep text inside the central 70% — the top and bottom are covered by the interface.
Hint 3: Generate captions with auto-captions, then proofread — the shop name is a common mistake.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ifeoma runs a hair salon in Port Harcourt. She wants to post a 30-second video on Instagram Reels showing a new
hairstyle. She has three clips: one shot under her salon's orange lights, one shot outside in the shade, and one correctly exposed
clip of the finished hairstyle. She adds a title with the salon name — but she spells "Glamour" as "Glamor." She adds no lower
third introducing herself. She does not add captions. The video gets few views and no new bookings.
Relevant facts.
The orange-lights clip makes the hair colour look wrong.
The shade clip makes the video look cold and unflattering.
The title has a spelling mistake.
Viewers cannot hear the audio clearly over the music in the first 5 seconds.
The video is posted in 16:9, so it has black bars on Reels.
Decision point. What is wrong — and what should Ifeoma fix first?
Possible approaches.
1. Post more videos — the problem is consistency, not quality.
2. Fix everything at once: colour, text, captions, aspect ratio and audio.
3. Fix the biggest problems first: aspect ratio, colour correction, title spelling and captions — then improve audio.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ifeoma's video has several problems, but the biggest are: wrong aspect ratio (16:9 on
Reels means black bars), uncorrected colour (the hair looks wrong), a misspelt title (destroys trust) and no captions (excludes
viewers who watch without sound). Fixing these four transforms the video. Audio improvements matter too — but the visual
and text problems are losing viewers first.
Reasoning / what Ifeoma would do:
Set the project to 9:16 for Reels.
Correct the orange and cool clips so the hair colour looks true.
Fix the title spelling — "Glamour," not "Glamor."
Add a lower third introducing herself ("Ifeoma, Glamour Salon").
Add captions — proofread.
Lower the music so her voice is clear.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: more videos with the same problems get the same poor results — consistency without quality is just repeated
failure.
Approach 2: trying to fix everything at once is overwhelming — and the learner may fix nothing well.
Approach 3: fixing the biggest problems first gives the fastest improvement — and teaches the habit of prioritising.
Professional connection. A salon that gets one new booking per week from a 30-second video has earned back the cost of the
video many times over. A 30-second Reel edited with correct colour, a spelt title and captions is worth ₦15,000–₦30,000 as
a freelance job. The skill is the same skill Ifeoma needs for her own business — and it is the skill this course teaches.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Grading before correcting
Symptoms: The video looks dramatically stylised but the colours are wrong — dark clips are dramatically dark, orange clips are
dramatically orange. Likely cause: A grade was applied before the clips were corrected. How to investigate: Watch the video
— does it look natural, or does it look filtered? Corrective steps: Remove the grade; correct all clips (exposure, white balance,
contrast); then apply a light grade only if the story needs it. How to confirm resolved: The clips look natural; any grade is
subtle. Prevention: Correction first, grading second — always.
K.2 Over-grading — the "filtered" look
Symptoms: The video looks like a movie trailer, not a real video. The colours are unnatural. Likely cause: The grade adjustment
is too strong. How to investigate: Watch the video — does it look filtered? Corrective steps: Reduce the grade to a subtle
level, or remove it entirely. How to confirm resolved: The video looks natural and intentional. Prevention: Less is more — a
light grade enhances; a heavy grade destroys.
K.3 Text outside safe areas
Symptoms: The title or captions are partially covered by the platform's interface when posted. Likely cause: The text was placed
in the outer margins, where the platform puts its buttons and overlays. How to investigate: Turn on the safe-area guide and
check — is the text inside the inner grid? Corrective steps: Move the text inside the safe area. How to confirm resolved: The
text is fully visible on the platform. Prevention: Always check the safe area before exporting.
K.4 Misspelt titles or captions
Symptoms: A title says "Glamor" instead of "Glamour"; a caption misspells a name. Likely cause: The text was not proofread; or
auto-captions made a mistake that was not caught. How to investigate: Read every word of every text element out loud.
Corrective steps: Fix the spelling; for captions, proofread the auto-generated draft. How to confirm resolved: Every word is
spelled correctly. Prevention: Proofread every text element — a misspelt title destroys trust.
K.5 Captions out of sync
Symptoms: Captions appear before or after the words are spoken. Likely cause: The caption timing was not adjusted after
generation. How to investigate: Watch the video — do the captions match the speech? Corrective steps: Nudge the captions
left or right until they match. How to confirm resolved: Each caption appears when the word is spoken. Prevention: Adjust
timing after generating captions — auto-timing is close but not perfect.
K.6 Clips that do not match
Symptoms: The cut between two angles of the same interview is jarring — the colours jump. Likely cause: The two clips have
different colour temperatures or exposure. How to investigate: Watch the cut — do the colours jump? Corrective steps:
Adjust the second clip's exposure and temperature until it matches; if the difference is too great, use a different clip or hide the
cut with B-roll. How to confirm resolved: The cut is invisible. Prevention: Match clips during correction — do not leave it for
later.
K.7 Unreadable text (poor font or contrast)
Symptoms: The text is hard to read — a decorative font, or text that blends into the background. Likely cause: The font is too
decorative, or the text colour clashes with the video. How to investigate: Watch the video — can you read every word easily?
Corrective steps: Use a clean sans-serif font; add a black outline or shadow so the text reads on any background. How to
confirm resolved: Every word is readable at a glance. Prevention: Sans-serif font, white text with outline — readable on any
background.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the safe-area guide with the text inside it, or the corrected clip's settings). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. Colour correction is fixing problems — making a dark clip brighter, a warm clip neutral. Colour grading is styling — giving

A2. Exposure (brightness), contrast, and white balance (temperature/tint).
A3. (b) The part of the screen not covered by the platform's interface — where text will always be visible.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner corrects before grading — without being reminded.
Learner adjusts exposure, white balance and contrast deliberately.
Learner matches clips so cuts are invisible.
Learner applies a light grade — or correctly decides not to grade.
Learner places text inside safe areas and checks it.
Learner proofreads captions and fixes auto-caption mistakes.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Grading makes it professional." → A natural corrected video looks professional; heavy grading looks amateur.
"I can fix any clip." → Correction improves; it cannot create detail that was never captured.
"Text anywhere is fine." → Text outside safe areas is covered by the platform.
"Captions are optional." → Captions are standard — they improve reach, inclusion and watch-through.
"Auto-captions are perfect." → They make mistakes — always proofread.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Correction drill: give three clips (dark, warm, cool); the learner corrects each and explains the adjustments.
2. Safe-area drill: show a 9:16 frame; the learner places a title and captions inside the safe area and explains why.
3. Caption proofread drill: give a caption script with five deliberate errors; the learner finds and fixes them.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide clips that are only slightly warm or cool; the learner corrects with small adjustments.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full colour and text pass plus independent exercise.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to correct a clip, match it with a second clip, and design a light grade that suits a
specific mood (warm for a family video, cool for a tech product) — and explain the mood choice.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners doing a colour and text pass (Sections H and the assignment). If the room is slow,
shorten the case study (Section J) to a 3-minute mention and limit the independent exercise to the guided steps. Protect the
practical time — a learner who leaves without a corrected, captioned video has not built the look and text skill that Sessions
6–9 depend on.
