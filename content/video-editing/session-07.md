# Export Settings, Formats and Delivery

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Polish and Deliver

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first export and delivery work)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6 — learners must have their motion pass (from Session 6) with disciplined effects. They must be able to do a full edit: cuts, audio, colour, text and motion. No prior export or codec knowledge assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 6 you made your video move right with disciplined effects. Today you learn the technical session that separates a hobbyist from a professional: export settings, formats and delivery. You will learn codecs, containers, resolution, frame rate, bitrate and aspect ratios — and how to export the right file for the right platform without destroying quality. The rule: export to specification, then check the exported file.

**Preparation before class:** CapCut installed and tested; a projector; a pre-edited video ready to export; printed platform specification sheets (YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp); the instructor's own export spec sheet pre-built as a model; a USB drive for each learner to take their exports home. Instructor note on tone: This session is technical and some learners will find it dry. The key is to make it relevant: every setting you teach is the difference between a file that plays beautifully and one that looks terrible or will not upload. Use the phrase "export to specification, then check the file" as the session's mantra. Connect every setting to a real consequence.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in plain English what a codec, a container, resolution, frame rate, bitrate and aspect ratio are — and why each
matters for delivery.
2. Choose the correct export settings for a given platform — YouTube, Instagram, TikTok, WhatsApp — including codec,
resolution, frame rate and bitrate.
3. Export a video from CapCut to a specified platform without visible quality damage.
4. Check the exported file against the brief — resolution, aspect ratio, file size and playback quality.
5. Explain how compression can be invisible or ruin a video — and how to avoid the ruin.
6. Name the common export mistakes and how to fix them.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show the same 10-second video exported three ways:
Export 1 (correct): 1080p, H.264, reasonable bitrate — the video looks sharp and plays smoothly.
Export 2 (too low bitrate): 1080p but very low bitrate — the video looks blocky and smeared, especially in areas of detail.
Export 3 (wrong resolution): 480p — the video looks soft and blurry on the projector.
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. Same video, same edit, same footage. Export 1 looks sharp. Export 2 looks blocky — you can see little
squares in the detail. Export 3 looks soft and blurry.
The difference is not the edit — it is the export settings. A codec, a bitrate, a resolution — these are the decisions that
determine whether your video looks professional or amateur when the client watches it.
Here is the rule for today: export to specification, then check the file. Every platform has a specification — a set of
recommended settings. If you export to that specification, the video looks right. If you guess, you risk a file that looks terrible
or will not upload.
Today you learn the six export settings — codec, container, resolution, frame rate, bitrate and aspect ratio — and how to
choose them for YouTube, Instagram, TikTok and WhatsApp. By the end of this session, you will have exported a video to
specification and checked it against the brief."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "I didn't know there were settings," "I just clicked export," "why does the blocky
one look like that?" Accept all answers. Say: "Most beginners just click export — and most beginners deliver files that look
wrong. Today you learn to export like a professional."
Then set the frame: Say clearly:
"The export is the last step — and the one the client sees. A beautiful edit exported badly is a bad video. A good edit
exported to specification is a professional video. Today you learn to finish properly."
Why this opener works: It shows the consequences of bad export settings in seconds, it proves learners can see the difference,
and it frames the session as the professional finish — not a technical chore.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is the story test, and how do you apply it?"
Expected answer: The story test is the question "does this help the story, or only my ego?" You apply it by asking that
question before keeping any effect — and deleting any effect that does not help.
Common gap: learners apply the story test to effects but not to exports. Clarify: "The story test applies to exports too —
does this setting serve the viewer?"
Question 2: "What is a keyframe, and what does it do?"
Expected answer: A keyframe is a marker that sets a value at a moment; the software animates between keyframes. It is the
engine of motion.
Common gap: learners confuse keyframes with cuts. Clarify: "A cut is where one clip ends; a keyframe is where a value is
set."
Question 3: "What is the difference between correction and grading?"
Expected answer: Correction is fixing problems (exposure, white balance, contrast). Grading is styling (creating a mood). Fix
first, style second.
Common gap: learners still want to grade everything. Clarify: "Grade only when the story needs it — and only after
correction."
Question 4: "What are safe areas, and why do they matter?"
Expected answer: Safe areas are the parts of the screen not covered by the platform's interface. They matter because text
placed outside them is hidden.
Common gap: learners place text wherever it looks nice. Clarify: "Text in the editor is not text on the platform — check the
safe area."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Your video now has good cuts, a clear story, balanced audio, corrected colour, readable text and disciplined motion. Today
we finish it properly — with the right export settings for the right platform. Export to specification, then check the file. That
is the professional habit."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing the story test on the projector
before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 The six export settings — the vocabulary of delivery
The idea. Every export is defined by six settings. Learn what each one is in plain English, and you can choose correctly for any
platform.
The six settings (teach them with analogies):
1. Codec (compressor-decompressor). The codec is the method used to shrink the video file so it can be stored and streamed.
Think of it as a zip file for video — it packs the data efficiently.
H.264 (AVC): the universal standard. Plays everywhere. Use this for almost everything.
H.265 (HEVC): newer, smaller files, but not all devices play it. Use it when file size matters and you know the device supports
it.
ProRes: a heavy, high-quality codec used in professional post-production. Do not use it for delivery — the files are
enormous.
2. Container. The container is the file "box" that holds the video, audio and metadata. It is the file extension you see.
MP4: the universal container. Use this for almost everything.
MOV: an Apple container. Fine, but MP4 is more universal.
MKV: a flexible container, but not always supported by platforms.
3. Resolution. The width × height of the video in pixels. (You learned this in Session 1.)
1920×1080 (1080p): the standard for professional work.
1280×720 (720p): acceptable for quick social posts, not for client work.
1080×1920: the 9:16 version for vertical video.
4. Frame rate. How many frames play per second. (You learned this in Session 1.)
30 fps: the standard for online video.
25 fps: common in some broadcast traditions.
60 fps: for smooth slow motion.
5. Bitrate. How much data is used per second of video. This is the most important setting for quality.
Think of bitrate as the bandwidth of a pipe: a bigger pipe (higher bitrate) carries more data and looks better; a smaller
pipe (lower bitrate) carries less and looks worse.
Too low: the video looks blocky and smeared — especially in areas of detail (foliage, fast movement, text).
Too high: the file is enormous, with no visible quality gain.
The sweet spot: high enough to look clean, low enough to be practical.
6. Aspect ratio. The shape of the frame. (You learned this in Session 1.)
16:9 for YouTube and TV; 9:16 for TikTok, Reels and Shorts; 1:1 or 4:5 for Instagram feed.
Real-life comparison. The six settings are like posting a parcel: the codec is how well you pack it, the container is the box, the
resolution is the size, the frame rate is how smoothly it moves, the bitrate is how much protection you use, and the aspect ratio
is the shape of the box. A professional packs the parcel so it arrives perfectly — not so heavy it costs a fortune, not so light it
breaks.
Common misconception. "Higher settings are always better." No. A 4K video on a phone screen is wasted weight. A 10 Mbps
bitrate for a talking head is wasted space. Choose what the platform and the story need.

video on WhatsApp is compressed to nothing. Export for the platform — or export twice for two platforms.
F.3 Compression — invisible or ruin
The idea. Compression is the process of shrinking the video file. Done well, it is invisible. Done badly, it ruins the video.
Plain-English definition. Compression reduces the file size by removing data the viewer is unlikely to notice — or by reducing
the overall data (bitrate).
The two types of compression:
Lossless compression: no quality loss. The file is smaller but perfect. (Rarely used for delivery — the files are still large.)
Lossy compression: some quality loss. The file is much smaller, but the quality drops. (This is what H.264 and H.265 do.)
How to compress without ruin:
1. Use the right codec (H.264 for compatibility).
2. Use a high enough bitrate — the single most important factor.
3. Use the right resolution — do not export 4K for a phone screen.
4. Check the exported file — watch it for blocking, smearing and artifacts.
What ruin looks like:
Blocking: little squares in areas of detail (foliage, fast movement).
Smearing: fine details blur together.
Banding: visible stripes in gradients (skies, walls).
Audio distortion: crackling or clipping.
Real-life comparison. Compression is like photocopying a document. A good copy is clean and readable. A bad copy is blurry,
with smudges and missing text. The bitrate is the quality of the copy — too low and the text is unreadable.
Common misconception. "I can compress as much as I want — the platform will fix it." No. The platform re-encodes what you
give it. If you give it a low-bitrate file, the re-encoding makes it worse. Give the platform a good file.
F.4 Exporting from CapCut — the steps
The idea. Exporting is the final step — and the one that determines what the client sees. Do it deliberately.
The export steps in CapCut:
1. Click "Export" (usually top-right).
2. Set the resolution — 1080p for most work.
3. Set the frame rate — 30 fps.
4. Set the codec — H.264 (the default for most exports).
5. Set the bitrate — choose "Higher quality" or set a specific bitrate (8–12 Mbps for 1080p YouTube; 5–8 Mbps for
social).
6. Choose the format — MP4.
7. Name the file — using the naming convention (see Session 8).
8. Choose the destination — your 03_Exports folder.
9. Click Export and wait.
10. Check the exported file — watch it, check the resolution, check the file size.
The rule: export to specification, then check the file. Never export and deliver without checking.
Real-life comparison. Exporting is like baking a cake. The ingredients are the edit; the oven settings are the export settings; the
finished cake is the video. A good cake needs the right temperature and time — too hot and it burns, too cool and it is raw. The
export settings are the temperature and time.
Common misconception. "Export is automatic — I just click the button." No. Export is a decision. The button is easy; the
settings are the skill.
F.5 Checking the exported file — the professional habit
The idea. The export is not finished when the progress bar reaches 100%. It is finished when you have checked the file.
The export check (teach this checklist):
1. Does it play? Open the file in a media player — does it play smoothly?
2. Is the resolution right? Check the file properties — does it match the specification?
3. Is the aspect ratio right? Does it look correct — no stretching, no black bars?
4. Is the quality clean? Watch for blocking, smearing, banding and audio distortion.
5. Is the file size reasonable? A 60-second 1080p video should be roughly 50–150 MB. If it is 5 MB, the bitrate is too low. If it
is 2 GB, it is too high.
6. Does it match the brief? Check every requirement — length, format, captions, content.
The rule: if any check fails, do not deliver. Re-export with better settings.
Real-life comparison. Checking the export is like tasting the food before serving. A cook who does not taste is guessing. A
professional editor checks the file — every time.
Common misconception. "The export looked fine in the editor." No. The editor shows a preview — the export is the real thing.
Always check the exported file, not the preview.
F.6 Common export mistakes and how to fix them
The idea. Beginners make the same export mistakes. Learn them now and avoid them.
The seven common mistakes:
Mistake What happens How to fix
Bitrate too low Blocky, smeared video Raise the bitrate to the platform standard
Wrong resolution Soft or cropped video Set the resolution to the platform standard
Wrong aspect ratio Black bars or stretching Set the aspect ratio to the platform standard
Wrong codec File will not play Use H.264 for compatibility
Exporting 4K for a phone Huge file, no visible gain Export 1080p for phone delivery
Not checking the file Delivering a bad export Always check the exported file
One export for all platforms Wrong shape or quality on some Export separately for each platform
Real-life comparison. Export mistakes are like posting a letter with the wrong address — it does not matter how good the
letter is; it will not arrive. The settings are the address; the file is the letter.
Common misconception. "The platform will fix my mistakes." No. The platform re-encodes what you give it — and a bad file
gets worse, not better.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
The six settings: codec, container, resolution, frame rate, bitrate, aspect ratio.
Codec: H.264 for compatibility; H.265 for smaller files; ProRes for post-production only.
Container: MP4 for almost everything.
Resolution: 1080p for professional work; 1080×1920 for vertical.
Frame rate: 30 fps for online video.
Bitrate: the most important quality setting — high enough to look clean, low enough to be practical.
Aspect ratio: 16:9 for YouTube; 9:16 for TikTok/Reels; 1:1 or 4:5 for Instagram feed.
Export to specification, then check the file.

Say: "I check this file too: does it play? Is it 1080×1920? Is the quality clean?"
Open the file; check the properties; watch a section.
Ask: "What is the professional habit?" → Export to specification, then check the file — every time.
Expected result: Two exported files — one for YouTube, one for TikTok — both checked against their specifications.
Questions to ask during the demo:
"What are the six export settings?" → Codec, container, resolution, frame rate, bitrate, aspect ratio.
"Why H.264?" → Because it plays everywhere.
"What is the most important quality setting?" → Bitrate.
"When is the export finished?" → When you have checked the file.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Obi is a freelance editor in Port Harcourt. His first client — a real estate agent — asks for a 60-second property video
for YouTube. Obi edits the video beautifully, then exports it. He does not check the settings — he just clicks export with the
default settings. The default is 720p at a low bitrate. He uploads it to YouTube. The video looks soft and blocky — the property
details are lost. The client is disappointed and does not pay the full amount.
Relevant facts.
Obi's edit is excellent — good cuts, clear story, balanced audio.
The export is 720p at a low bitrate — far below YouTube's 1080p standard.
Obi did not check the exported file.
The client expected a professional 1080p video.
Decision point. What went wrong — and what should Obi have done?
Possible approaches.
1. Blame the platform — YouTube compressed it.
2. Tell the client that 720p is good enough.
3. Acknowledge the mistake, re-export to specification, and deliver a better file — and learn the lesson.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Obi's problem is not the platform — it is that he exported below the specification and
did not check the file. YouTube re-encoded his low-bitrate 720p file and made it worse. The fix: re-export at 1080p, H.264, 8–12
Mbps, check the file, and deliver. The lesson: export to specification, then check the file — every time.
Reasoning / what Obi would do:
Re-export at 1080p, H.264, MP4, 30 fps, 8–12 Mbps.
Check the file — resolution, quality, file size.
Deliver the better file and explain the mistake honestly.
Learn the habit: export to specification, check the file.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1: blaming the platform is dishonest — and the client will not believe it.
Approach 2: 720p is not good enough for a professional property video — the details matter.
Approach 3: acknowledging the mistake costs a little pride — but it builds trust and teaches the habit that prevents every
future mistake.
Professional connection. Obi's first client could lead to many more — real estate is a steady market for video. But only if the
first delivery is professional. An editor who exports to specification and checks the file delivers quality every time. An editor who
guesses delivers disappointment. The difference is one habit — and it is worth thousands of naira in client trust.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Bitrate too low — blocky video
Symptoms: The video looks blocky and smeared, especially in areas of detail. Likely cause: The bitrate was set too low for the
resolution. How to investigate: Check the export settings — what was the bitrate? Corrective steps: Re-export with a higher
bitrate (8–12 Mbps for 1080p YouTube; 5–8 Mbps for social). How to confirm resolved: The video looks clean — no
blocking or smearing. Prevention: Use the platform specification — never guess the bitrate.
K.2 Wrong resolution — soft or cropped video
Symptoms: The video looks soft (too low resolution) or cropped (too high resolution for the frame). Likely cause: The
resolution did not match the platform specification. How to investigate: Check the file properties — what is the resolution?
Corrective steps: Re-export with the correct resolution. How to confirm resolved: The resolution matches the specification.
Prevention: Set the resolution before exporting — check the platform sheet.
K.3 Wrong aspect ratio — black bars or stretching
Symptoms: The video has black bars (wrong ratio) or looks stretched (mismatched ratio). Likely cause: The aspect ratio did not
match the platform. How to investigate: Watch the video — are there black bars? Does it look stretched? Corrective steps:
Re-export with the correct aspect ratio. How to confirm resolved: The video fills the screen correctly. Prevention: Set the
aspect ratio before exporting — check the platform sheet.
K.4 Wrong codec — file will not play
Symptoms: The file will not play on some devices or platforms. Likely cause: The codec is not widely supported (e.g., H.265 on
an old device). How to investigate: Check the export settings — what codec was used? Corrective steps: Re-export with
H.264. How to confirm resolved: The file plays everywhere. Prevention: Use H.264 for compatibility — the universal standard.
K.5 Not checking the exported file
Symptoms: The delivered file has a problem that was not caught. Likely cause: The file was not checked after export. How to
investigate: Ask: "Did you watch the exported file?" Corrective steps: Check every export — plays, resolution, aspect ratio,
quality, size. How to confirm resolved: The file passes every check. Prevention: Export to specification, then check the file —
every time.
K.6 One export for all platforms
Symptoms: The video looks wrong on some platforms — black bars, poor quality. Likely cause: One file was used for all
platforms, but the platforms have different specifications. How to investigate: Check the platforms — do they have different
aspect ratios or resolutions? Corrective steps: Export separately for each platform. How to confirm resolved: Each platform
gets a file that matches its specification. Prevention: Export for the platform — not for convenience.
K.7 Exporting 4K for a phone screen
Symptoms: The file is enormous; the quality gain is invisible on a phone. Likely cause: The resolution is too high for the
delivery platform. How to investigate: Check the resolution — is it 4K for a phone video? Corrective steps: Re-export at
1080p. How to confirm resolved: The file is smaller and looks the same on a phone. Prevention: Choose the resolution for the
platform — not the maximum.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the export settings or the file properties). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner names the six export settings and explains each in plain English.
Learner chooses the correct settings for a given platform.
Learner exports to specification and checks the file.
Learner explains why bitrate matters most.
Learner exports separately for different platforms.
Learner's exported files play cleanly at the correct resolution and aspect ratio.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Higher settings are always better." → Choose what the platform and story need.
"One export works for every platform." → Every platform has its own specification.
"The platform will fix my mistakes." → The platform re-encodes what you give it — a bad file gets worse.
"Export is automatic." → Export is a decision; the settings are the skill.
"The export looked fine in the editor." → The editor shows a preview; check the exported file.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Settings matching: give four platforms and a list of settings; the learner matches each platform to its correct settings.
2. Export check drill: give a deliberately bad export; the learner identifies the problems and states the fix.
3. Specification sheet drill: the learner fills in the specification sheet for all four platforms from memory.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide the specification sheet pre-filled; the learner exports and checks.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — dual export plus specification sheet.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to export the same video for all four platforms and write a short delivery note explaining
which file goes where and why.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners exporting to specification and checking the file (Sections H and the assignment). If
the room is slow, shorten the case study (Section J) to a 3-minute mention and limit the independent exercise to one export plus
the specification sheet. Protect the practical time — a learner who leaves without a checked, correctly exported file has not
built the delivery skill that Sessions 8–9 depend on.
