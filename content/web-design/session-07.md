# Planning a Website

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Building a Website

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (mostly thinking and drawing — no coding this session)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6. The learner should be able to build and style a responsive HTML page. This session has no coding, but it prepares every page the learner will code in Session 8. Note: no laptop is strictly needed; paper, pen and a printed plan template are the main tools.

**Connection to previous work:** For six sessions learners have built small pages on request. This session introduces the professional habit that comes before code: planning. It mirrors how real designers work — understand the client, plan the pages, sketch the layout, gather content, then build.

**Preparation before class:** Print a wireframe template (one per learner) and a site-plan template (one per learner); provide plain A4 paper and pens or pencils (two per learner); print a one-page "Adaeze's Fashion brief" (see Section F.1); have the projector ready to show example wireframes and a colour-contrast demo; optionally prepare one or two real business cards or flyers as colour/font examples.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Read a project brief and extract its key requirements: purpose, audience, pages and content.
2. Define the purpose and goals of a website in one or two clear sentences.
3. Describe the target audience of a small Nigerian business and explain how that audience shapes design decisions.
4. Create a sitemap that shows every page of the website and how the pages relate.
5. Draw a wireframe (by hand) for a page, showing header, navigation, main content and footer as labelled boxes.
6. Plan page content — decide what text and images each page needs before coding.
7. Choose a simple colour palette and two fonts, and explain why contrast between text and background matters for
accessibility.
8. Set up a clean file and folder structure for a multi-page site and apply sensible file-naming conventions.
9. Explain why planning before coding saves time and prevents rework.

## C. Required Materials
<!-- kind:materials -->
Hardware
One computer per learner (academy desktops) — used only for looking up nothing this session; it is fine if a learner works
entirely on paper at their desk.
Instructor computer connected to a projector or large screen.
Software
Visual Studio Code and Google Chrome are not required this session, but the instructor may open a sample project folder
in VS Code at the end to show the planned file structure.
Printed materials
"Wireframe Template" (one per learner) — a blank page-sized rectangle with a faint grid.
"Site Plan Template" (one per learner) — a form with fields for purpose, audience, pages, content, colours, fonts and file
structure.
"Adaeze's Fashion — Project Brief" (one per learner).
"Colour Palette & Contrast Handout" (one per learner).
Tools
A4 paper (at least two sheets per learner), pens and pencils.
Optional: a ruler for drawing straight boxes; coloured pencils if available.
Optional: one smartphone to show how a real phone screen looks while sketching.
Internet
Not required. If available, the instructor may show an online colour-contrast checker at the very end, but do not depend on
it. A simple paper method (described in F.7) works without internet.
Alternative for learners who lack a facility
This session is naturally paper-based, so no special accommodation is needed. If a learner cannot draw, they may lay out
boxes with sticky notes or describe the wireframe verbally while the instructor or a partner sketches it.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a photograph of a building under construction (or describe one) — or, if none is
available, draw a quick sketch of a house with foundation lines and say it is a plan. Then show a crumpled, poorly built structure
beside a neat finished building.
What the instructor says:
"Imagine you are building a house for your family. Would you start laying blocks on any open ground and hope it turns out
well? No. First you sit with the builder and agree what the house is for, how many rooms you need, where the kitchen goes,
where the doors are. You draw a plan. Then you build.
A website is the same. For six sessions you have been learning the tools — the blocks and cement of the web. Today we stop
and draw the plan for a real business website: Adaeze's Fashion in Port Harcourt. We will decide what pages it needs,
what goes on each page, what it looks like, and how the files will be arranged. Tomorrow, and for the rest of the course, we
build from this plan."
Expected learner response: Learners may be impatient to code. Address this directly: "A one-day plan today saves a week of
rework later. Professional designers never skip this step."
Then demonstrate the hook: Show a before/after. On the projector, display two wireframes: one messy, with boxes randomly
placed and unlabelled; and one neat, labelled sketch of the same page (header bar at the top, hero band, three product boxes,
footer at the bottom). Say:
"See? This is not art. It is a simple map. You can draw it in ten minutes with a pen. And once you have it, building the page
in HTML becomes almost automatic."
Why this opener works: It reframes planning from "boring paperwork" to "the thing that makes building easy", which is exactly
the professional truth this session teaches.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as an oral round-robin. Put the six questions on the projector. This session has no coding, so this quick review keeps
earlier skills alive.
Question 1: "What are the three building blocks of every website?"
Expected answer: HTML (structure), CSS (appearance) and JavaScript (behaviour).
Common gap: forgetting JavaScript. Remind them it comes in Session 10.
Question 2: "Name three semantic HTML elements we use for page structure."
Expected answer: <header> , <nav> , <main> , <section> , <footer> .
Common gap: naming <div> as semantic. Clarify: <div> is a generic box; semantic tags describe meaning.
Question 3: "How do you link a CSS file from index.html ?"
Expected answer: <link rel="stylesheet" href="css/style.css"> in the <head> .
Common gap: wrong path. Point out we will plan that folder today.
Question 4: "What is a media query, and what two words can start one?"
Expected answer: CSS that applies at certain screen widths; min-width or max-width .
Common gap: confusing the two. Remind them: min-width = "this wide or wider".
Question 5: "What does the viewport meta tag do?"
Expected answer: makes the page use the device's real width so mobile layouts work correctly.
Common gap: "I forgot it." Good reminder to include it on every page we build.
Question 6: "What is a relative path? Give one example."
Expected answer: a path relative to the current file, e.g. about.html or images/logo.png .
Common gap: using full Windows paths like C:\Users\... . Remind them we never do this.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You now have the tools — HTML, CSS, responsive design. But a builder with all the tools and no plan builds a mess. Today
we become the designer and the architect. Client first, pages second, sketches third, content fourth, colours and fonts fifth,
file structure last."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend a few minutes re-capping the three languages before
continuing (but keep it brief — this session must protect drawing time).

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Reading the project brief — what does the client actually want?
The idea. Before you design anything, you must understand the client's goal. In professional work this comes from a project
brief — a short document that states what the client needs.
Plain-English definition. A project brief answers, in plain language, four questions: What is the website for? Who will use it?
What pages does it need? What content goes on those pages?
Why it exists. A website with no clear purpose drifts. If you do not know what the client is trying to achieve, you will build the
wrong thing — beautifully. The brief keeps everyone anchored.
The brief for our project (read aloud):
Client: Adaeze Okafor, owner of Adaeze's Fashion, Port Harcourt. Business: A small fashion business that designs and
sews custom clothing — Ankara dresses, lace gowns, aso ebi sets and office wear — and also runs short sewing classes.
Problem: Adaeze sells mostly through WhatsApp and Instagram. She cannot show her full collection neatly, and
customers keep asking the same questions about prices, location and delivery. Goal: A simple, professional website where
customers can see her work, learn about her, and contact her to place an order. Budget: Small. The website must be built
with free tools and free hosting. Devices: Most customers use phones; the website must work perfectly on a phone first.
The four questions, answered for Adaeze:
Question Answer
Purpose Showcase the collection and let customers make contact to order.
Audience Adults in Port Harcourt and Rivers State who buy or commission clothing; plus people interested in sewing
classes.
Pages needed Home, Shop (Products), About, Contact.
Content needed Photos of outfits, prices in naira, Adaeze's story, address, phone, social links, a contact form.
How a learner recognises a good purpose statement. A strong purpose statement is short and specific: "To showcase
Adaeze's clothing and let customers contact her to order." A weak one is vague: "To have a website." If the purpose could apply
to any business, it is too vague.
Misconception. "The client always knows what they want." Often they know the outcome they want (more orders) but not the
pages or features. Part of your job is to translate their goal into a plan and confirm it with them.
Limitation. A brief can change. Real projects often have the client add a page later. Good planning makes such changes cheap,
because the structure is clear and consistent.
F.2 Defining purpose and goals
The idea. Write down the website's purpose (why it exists) and its goals (what success looks like).
Plain-English definition. Purpose is the single reason the site exists. Goals are the measurable outcomes that tell you if it
worked.
Why it exists. Purpose keeps design decisions honest. Every element on the page should serve the purpose. If it does not, it is
clutter.
Adaeze's purpose and goals:
Purpose: To show Adaeze's clothing clearly and make it easy for a customer to start an order.
Goals:
1. A customer can see at least six outfits without scrolling forever.
2. A customer can find Adaeze's phone number and address in under ten seconds.
3. A customer can send a message through the contact form.
How purpose shapes design. Because the first goal is "see outfits clearly", the Shop page must use a tidy grid of photos with
prices (the Grid skill from Session 5). Because the second goal is "find contact details fast", the phone number and address must
appear in the footer of every page. Because most customers use phones, every page must be responsive.
Say this to the class:
"Every time you are unsure whether to add something to a page, ask: does this help the purpose? If not, leave it out. A
clean, focused website beats a busy one."
Misconception. "More features make a better website." Usually the opposite is true for a small business. A clear, fast, phone-
friendly site with the essentials beats a crowded one with a broken contact form.
F.3 Understanding the audience
The idea. Design for the people who will use the site, not for yourself.
Plain-English definition. The audience (or target users) are the people the website is meant to serve. Knowing them shapes the
language, colours, images and even the page structure.
Why it exists. The same business can have different audiences. A clothing website for teenagers would look and sound
different from one for adults buying aso ebi for a family event. Matching the audience makes the site feel right to them.
Adaeze's audience (described plainly):
Audience group What they want Design implication
Individual customers To see outfits and prices, and to contact
Adaeze
Clear photos, visible prices, prominent contact
button
Event/party buyers (aso ebi) To order matching outfits for a group A note about group orders, a contact form for
details
Sewing-class students To know about training and fees A Services/Training section on the site
Phone users (the majority) Speed and easy tapping Responsive layout, large tap targets, light
images
How to talk to an audience. Use simple, warm English — the language of the customers, not technical jargon. Adaeze's
customers do not want to read "leveraging synergies"; they want to know if the dress comes in their size and how much delivery
costs. Write for the reader.
Misconception. "Everyone is my audience." Attempting to please everyone usually pleases no one. Picking a primary audience
(here: phone-using clothing customers in Port Harcourt) gives every design decision a clear reference point.
F.4 Planning the pages — the sitemap
The idea. A sitemap is a simple diagram (like a family tree) showing every page on the website and how they relate.
Plain-English definition. A sitemap is the list and shape of your pages, usually drawn as boxes with lines. The Home page sits at
the top; other pages branch off it.
Why it exists. A sitemap lets you see the whole website at a glance before building it, so you do not discover halfway through
that you forgot a page or gave two pages the same job.
Adaeze's sitemap:
```text
                        HOME (index.html)
                         |
     ----------------------------------------------
     |             |              |                |
   About         Shop          Services         Contact
 (about.html) (shop.html)   (services.html)   (contact.html)
```
Every page is reachable from the Home page through the top navigation. Four to five pages is the right size for a small business
site — enough to cover the essentials without overwhelming the visitor.
Navigation rules that follow from the sitemap:
1. Keep the main menu to 5 items or fewer.
2. Use the same menu on every page (consistency builds trust).
3. Use clear labels: "Shop", not "Stuff"; "About", not "Info".
4. The logo always links back to Home.
5. Mark the current page in the menu (we will do this with CSS in Session 9).
How a learner recognises a good sitemap. It is shallow (not many levels deep) and every page has one clear job. If two pages
do the same thing, merge them. If a page has no home in the structure, question whether it is needed.
Misconception. "More pages look more professional." A four-page site with clear content beats a twelve-page site with empty
pages. Depth is not quality.
Limitation. A sitemap shows what pages exist, not what they look like. That is the job of the wireframe (next).
F.5 Wireframing — sketching the layout by hand
The idea. A wireframe is a rough sketch of a page that shows where each block of content goes, using only boxes and labels —
no colours, no fonts, no real text.
Plain-English definition. A wireframe is the "skeleton" of a page drawn on paper. It answers: what is at the top, what is in the
middle, what is at the bottom, and how wide is each part?
Why it exists. It is far faster to move a box on paper than to move it in code. Wireframes let you try five layouts in twenty
minutes and pick the best before writing a single line of HTML.
Why we draw on paper (not in software). Paper is fast, free and cannot distract you with colours and effects. You can scribble,
erase and try again. Professional designers still do this first.
The standard blocks of a wireframe (draw these as rectangles):
Header — top bar with logo (left) and navigation (right).
Hero — a big banner area with a headline and a call-to-action button.
Main content — the main sections (e.g. featured products, text).
Sidebar — optional narrower column beside the main content.
Footer — bottom bar with contact details and social links.
Conventions for drawing:
Draw a rectangle for a box; label it (e.g. "LOGO", "MENU").
Draw an X through a box to mean "an image goes here".
Draw wavy lines to mean "text goes here".
Write the page name at the top of the sheet.
Worked example — a wireframe of Adaeze's Home page (described in words, drawn on paper):
```text
+--------------------------------------------------+
|  LOGO (Adaeze's Fashion)            HOME SHOP ... |  <- header + nav bar
+--------------------------------------------------+
|                                                  |
|        "Custom Fashion for Every Occasion"        |
|        [ View Our Collection  (button) ]          |
|                                                  |  <- hero (full width)
+--------------------------------------------------+
|   [X]        [X]        [X]                       |
|  dress1     dress2     dress3                     |
|  ₦18,500    ₦32,000    ₦27,500                    |  <- featured products (3 boxes)
+--------------------------------------------------+
|  Our Story        |   Get in Touch                |
|  ~~~~~ text ~~~~  |   Phone: 0801 234 5678        |  <- two columns: story + contact
|  ~~~~~ text ~~~~  |   Address: Aba Road, PH       |
+--------------------------------------------------+
|  (c) 2026 Adaeze's Fashion   Instagram  Facebook  |  <- footer
+--------------------------------------------------+
```
Expected result of drawing it: On paper, you can now see the page from top to bottom. Anyone — even a non-designer —
can look at this and understand what the Home page will contain. That clarity is the point.
How a learner recognises a good wireframe. It has clear blocks, the labels make each block's purpose obvious, and the most
important content (here, the outfits and the contact details) is high on the page.
Misconception. "A wireframe must look nice." No — neatness is optional; clarity is essential. A messy sketch that
communicates the layout is more useful than a beautiful drawing that hides it.
Limitation. A wireframe is only one page. You need one wireframe per page (Home, Shop, About, Contact). They should share
the same header and footer, which is why the navigation feels consistent to the visitor.
F.6 Gathering content — text and images
The idea. Decide exactly what words and pictures each page will contain before you build it.
Plain-English definition. Content is everything the visitor reads and sees: headings, paragraphs, prices, photos, contact details.
Content is the reason the site exists.
Why it exists. Many beginners build an empty layout and then scramble for content, which leads to lorem-ipsum placeholders
and a half-finished site. Planning content first means the layout can be designed to fit the real material.
Content plan for Adaeze's Fashion:
Home page
Headline: "Custom Fashion for Every Occasion"
Subheadline: "Beautiful, affordable clothing, handmade in Port Harcourt."
Featured products: 3 photos with names and naira prices.
Call to action button: "View Our Collection" (links to Shop).
Shop page
Heading: "Our Collection"
Six to nine products, each with a photo, a name and a price (e.g. Ankara Dress — ₦18,500).
Note: "To order, contact us or send a message using the form." (No online cart in this course.)
About page
Heading: "Our Story"
Three to four short paragraphs about Adaeze, the business and its values.
One photo of Adaeze or the workshop.
The three values: Quality, Affordability, Style.
Services page (if included)
Heading: "Training Services"
Three cards: Beginner Sewing Class, Ankara Design Workshop, Business of Fashion.
Contact page
Heading: "Get in Touch"
A contact form: Name, Email, Message, and a Send button.
Address: Aba Road, Port Harcourt, Rivers State.
Phone: 0801 234 5678 (a made-up example number).
Social links: Instagram, Facebook.
Where to get images for practice. Use your own photographs, or free stock sites, or (for practice) simple coloured boxes. For a
real client, always ask the client for photos of their actual products — real photos sell far better than stock images.
Writing tips for small-business content:
Short paragraphs (2–3 sentences).
Friendly, plain English.
Put the most important information first.
Spell-check carefully; typos reduce trust.
Misconception. "I will add the text later." If you leave content for later, you will design a layout that does not fit the real content
and end up rebuilding. Plan the words now, even in rough form.
Limitation. Writing good content takes time and, honestly, it is often the hardest part of a website. In this course we write short,
honest content; a real client may hire a copywriter or write it themselves.
F.7 Choosing colours and fonts (and why contrast matters)
The idea. Pick a small, consistent colour palette and one or two fonts that match the business, and make sure text is easy to
read against its background.
Plain-English definition. A colour palette is the small set of colours used across the whole site (usually two or three plus
neutrals). Fonts (typefaces) are the letter styles used for headings and body text.
Why it exists. A consistent palette and font make a website feel professional and trustworthy. Random colours and many fonts
make it look amateurish. Consistency also signals reliability — important when customers are deciding whether to spend
money with you.

Why purple and gold? They feel elegant and creative, match a fashion brand, and — importantly — purple and gold have
strong contrast against white, so text stays readable.
Fonts: choose one font for headings and (for simplicity) the same or a complementary one for body text. Adaeze's choice:
Headings: Montserrat (a clean, bold, modern sans-serif).
Body: Open Sans or simply Arial (a plain, highly readable sans-serif).
Fallback fonts matter. You should always end a font-family list with a generic fallback, e.g. font-family: 'Montserrat', Arial,
sans-serif; . If the first font is not available, the browser tries the next. This keeps the site looking good even without an internet
connection.
Contrast and accessibility. Contrast is how different the text colour is from the background colour. Low contrast (light grey text
on white) is hard to read, especially in bright Nigerian sunlight or for older users. High contrast (near-black text on white) is easy
to read for almost everyone.
A simple way to check contrast without any tool:
1. Imagine the page printed in greyscale (black-and-white photocopy).
2. If you can still clearly read the text, the contrast is probably fine.
3. For buttons, the button text must stand out from the button colour too.
Why this matters — the accessibility idea. Some of Adaeze's customers may have weaker eyesight, may be using an old phone
with a dull screen, or may be reading in bright sun. Good contrast is not a small detail — it decides whether your customer can
use your site at all.
Worked example — good and bad contrast:
```text
/* BAD: light grey on white — hard to read */
.old-text {
  color: #cccccc;
  background-color: #ffffff;
}
```
```text
/* GOOD: near-black on white — easy to read */
.new-text {
  color: #222222;
  background-color: #ffffff;
}
```
```text
/* GOOD button: white text on deep purple */
.btn {
  color: #ffffff;
  background-color: #4b2e83;
  padding: 12px 24px;
  text-decoration: none;
}
```
Expected browser result: The .old-text paragraph is barely legible — you have to squint. The .new-text paragraph is crisp
and comfortable. The .btn is a clear purple button with white text that jumps out. This is exactly the difference good contrast
makes.
How a learner recognises a good palette. It has a small number of colours, they appear consistently (the same purple
everywhere), and every piece of text is easy to read against its background.
Misconception. "I need many colours to look creative." A small, well-chosen palette looks more professional than a rainbow.
Pick two brand colours plus black, white and a grey, and stick to them.
Limitation. We are planning colours now, but applying them happens in Session 9 (Styling the Website). Today you only decide
the palette and write down the hex codes.
F.8 File and folder structure and naming conventions
The idea. Plan where every file will live before you create it.
Plain-English definition. File structure is the way you organise your files into folders. Naming conventions are the rules for
naming those files.
Why it exists. A website with scattered, badly named files is a nightmare to update — you will lose track of which style file is
the real one, and links will break when you publish online. A clean structure makes a project easy to build, easy to maintain and
easier to hand over to a client.
Adaeze's planned file structure:
```text
adaeze-fashion/
├── index.html          (Home page — must be named index.html)
├── about.html          (About page)
├── shop.html           (Shop / Products page)
├── contact.html        (Contact page)
├── css/
│   └── style.css       (One shared stylesheet for the whole site)
├── images/
│   ├── logo.png
│   ├── hero.jpg
│   ├── dress1.jpg
│   ├── dress2.jpg
│   └── ...
└── js/
    └── script.js       (JavaScript — added in Session 10)
```
Naming rules (learn these by heart):
1. All lowercase — about.html , never About.html .
2. Hyphens instead of spaces — ankara-dress.jpg , never ankara dress.jpg or AnkaraDress.jpg .
3. Short but descriptive — dress1.jpg is fine; my-lovely-new-dress-photo-final-v2.jpg is not.
4. The Home page is always index.html — the web server looks for this name automatically when someone visits your site's
main address.
5. No special characters — avoid & , # , ? , spaces; these can break links.
Why lowercase and hyphens matter so much. Windows does not care about letter case ( About.html and about.html are
treated the same), but most web servers run on Linux, which does care. So a link to About.html works perfectly on your
computer and breaks the moment you publish it. Using all lowercase everywhere avoids this trap completely. Spaces in file
names become %20 in URLs and are error-prone; hyphens avoid the problem.
Worked example — a correct link versus a broken one:
```text
<!-- CORRECT: matches the real lowercase file name -->
<a href="about.html">About</a>
```
```text
<!-- BROKEN once published: capital A does not match about.html on Linux servers -->
<a href="About.html">About</a>
```
Expected result: The first link opens the About page everywhere, on your laptop and online. The second works on your
Windows laptop but gives a "404 Not Found" on the live website. That 404 is one of the most common beginner mistakes —
and it is entirely avoidable by following the naming rules.
How a learner recognises a clean project. All HTML files sit together in the root folder; CSS is in css/ ; images are in images/ ;
JavaScript is in js/ ; and every file name is lowercase with hyphens.
Misconception. "The folder name does not matter." It does. Keep the project folder simple too (e.g. adaeze-fashion ), because it
may later become part of your website's address.
Limitation. This flat structure is perfect for a 3–5 page site. Very large sites with hundreds of pages need deeper organisation, but
that is beyond this course.

Start with the brief: purpose, audience, pages, content.
Write a short, specific purpose statement and a few goals.

Draw a sitemap: Home at the top, four pages branching off.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Two freelance designers each take on a small business website for a bakery in Port Harcourt. Designer A starts writing
code immediately, excited to show off. Designer B spends half a day on planning: a short brief chat with the owner, a sitemap,
paper wireframes, a list of content, a colour palette and fonts, and a folder structure.
Relevant facts.
The bakery owner wants customers to see cakes and order by phone.
Both designers are equally skilled at HTML and CSS.
The owner changes their mind partway through ("actually, I want a page for our delivery areas").
What happens to Designer A. Because there is no structure, the new "delivery areas" request forces changes across every page.
The navigation must change everywhere, the folder grows messy, and the colour choices drift page by page. The project runs
long and looks inconsistent.
What happens to Designer B. The sitemap makes the new page easy to slot in: add one box to the map, one wireframe, one
HTML file. The consistent template and folder structure mean the change costs an hour, not a week. The final site looks unified
and professional.
Decision point. Why did planning give Designer B such an advantage?
Possible approaches (for any project).
1. Skip planning and code immediately.
2. Plan fully (brief, sitemap, wireframes, content, palette, structure) before coding.
3. Plan a little and adjust as you go.
Recommended solution. Approach 2 for a project of any real size. A short, focused planning session makes later changes cheap
and keeps the site consistent.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 gives a fast first page but a slow, painful project overall.
Approach 2 costs a few hours up front and saves days later.
Approach 3 is a reasonable middle path for very small projects, but even then a sitemap and wireframe are worth the time.
Professional connection. In real agencies, planning documents (brief, sitemap, wireframes) are deliverables the client
sometimes signs off before development begins. Being able to produce them is a genuine professional skill, and it is often what
separates a paid designer from an amateur.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Because this session is about planning, the "troubleshooting" here is about planning mistakes rather than code bugs.
K.1 Skipping the plan and jumping straight to code
Symptoms: The learner wants to open VS Code immediately and starts typing. Why it hurts: Layout, navigation and structure
will be inconsistent; changes become expensive. Fix: Insist on a sitemap and one wireframe before coding. Ten minutes of
drawing saves an hour of rework. Prevention: Treat planning as the first step of every project, not an optional extra.
K.2 Vague purpose ("to have a website")
Symptoms: The purpose statement could apply to any business. Why it hurts: Without a clear purpose, there is no way to
decide what belongs on the page. Fix: Rewrite it specifically: "To showcase Adaeze's clothing and let customers contact her to
order." Prevention: Always name the outcome (what the visitor should be able to do).
K.3 Too many pages or too few
Symptoms: A twelve-page site with empty pages, or a one-page site hiding everything. Why it hurts: Empty pages look
unfinished; cramming everything onto one page overwhelms visitors. Fix: Aim for 3–5 clear pages, each with one job.
Prevention: Let the sitemap reveal overlap; merge pages that do the same thing.
K.4 Over-detailed wireframe (colours, fonts, shading)
Symptoms: The learner spends a long time making the sketch "pretty". Why it hurts: It wastes time and distracts from the point
— the layout. Fix: Boxes, labels, X for images, wavy lines for text. Nothing more. Prevention: Remind learners that colours come
in Session 9.
K.5 Inconsistent page structure
Symptoms: Different pages have different headers or menus. Why it hurts: Visitors feel lost; the site feels untrustworthy. Fix:
Draw the same header and footer on every wireframe. Prevention: Plan a single shared template for header and footer that
every page reuses.
K.6 Bad file names
Symptoms: Files like About Page.html , dress photo 1.jpg , Shop.html . Why it hurts: Spaces and capitals break links once
published to a Linux server. Fix: Rename using lowercase and hyphens: about.html , dress-photo-1.jpg , shop.html . Prevention:
Agree the naming rules before creating any files.
K.7 Low-contrast colour choices
Symptoms: Light grey text on white; yellow text on white. Why it hurts: Text is hard or impossible to read, especially in bright
light or on older screens. Fix: Use near-black text on light backgrounds, and white text on dark buttons. Prevention: Use the
"greyscale photocopy" test described in F.7.

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Planning is a waste of time; let me just code." → Planning is what makes coding fast and consistent.
"A wireframe should look beautiful." → It only needs to be clear; details come later.
"More pages = more professional." → Fewer, focused pages beat many empty ones.
"Colours do not matter much." → Contrast decides whether people can read your site.
"File name capitalisation does not matter." → It matters the moment you publish to a Linux server.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Brief-to-sitemap drill: give a one-paragraph brief and ask for a four-page sitemap in five minutes.
2. Wireframe labelling: give a blank rectangle and a list of blocks (header, hero, products, footer) and ask the learner to place
and label them.
3. Naming fix-up: give a list of ten bad file names and ask the learner to correct each.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed wireframe (header and footer already drawn) and a partially filled site
plan; the learner completes them.
Level 2 (standard): the practical and independent exercises as written.
Level 3 (challenge): plan a more ambitious site (six or seven pages, adding a Gallery and a Testimonials page), and design a
mobile-specific wireframe (a phone-shaped sketch) alongside the desktop one.
Timing guidance: The drawing is the heart of this session. If the room is slow, reduce the lecture explanations (Section F) to the
essentials and protect at least 15 minutes for the guided wireframe. If the room is fast, let learners begin a second wireframe (the
Shop page) live in class, so they arrive at Session 8 with more of the plan done.
