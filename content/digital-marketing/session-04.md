# Content and Social Marketing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Channels

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (writing and planning, not software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–3. Learners should be able to describe the funnel, build a customer avatar, set up a Google Business Profile, and write a value proposition. Basic smartphone and social media use required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–3 we built the map (funnel, journey), the customer (avatar, messaging) and the home (Google Business Profile, website, brand). Now we bring that home to life. Content and social marketing is how you show up, get noticed and build trust with your audience — it is the engine that feeds every other channel. Without content, your social media is an empty shop; without a plan, you post randomly and get nothing. Today we learn to plan, create and repurpose content that works.

**Preparation before class:** Print the Content Pillar Worksheet and the One-Week Content Calendar template (Section H) per learner. Prepare a projector slide showing a real or fictional Instagram feed — one with random posting, one with a planned content pillar structure. Prepare a Canva account open for the live demo. Print the Repurposing Map template (Section I). Have the Session 2 avatar worksheet and Session 3 brand kit available for reference. Instructor note on tone: This session is creative and practical. Many learners think "content" means being a video star or a professional photographer. It does not. Content is simply useful or entertaining material that shows your work and builds trust. A kitchen can content its food. A barbershop can content before/after photos. A phone repair shop can content 30-second tips. The skill is not creativity — it is planning. A good plan beats raw talent every time. Keep the room energetic and hands-on.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what content marketing is and why it is the engine of social media, using the "empty shop" analogy.
2. Build a content pillar plan with three to five pillars for a small business, each pillar tied to a customer pain, goal or interest
from their avatar.
3. Create a one-week content calendar with format, topic, hook, call-to-action and funnel stage for each post — realistic
for one person with a phone.
4. Write a story-led post using the situation–struggle–solution–next step structure, with a scroll-stopping hook and a
clear call-to-action.
5. Explain organic reach and why it must be earned through saves, shares, comments and watch time rather than bought.
6. Repurpose one idea into at least three formats — for example, one menu photo becoming an Instagram post, a
WhatsApp broadcast and a video script.
7. Apply ethical content rules — honest claims, real photos, no copying, no fake engagement — in every piece of content
they create.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two Instagram feeds side by side.
Feed A (random posting): A business page with 20 posts — random photos of food, a random quote, a random selfie, a
random advert, no pattern, no captions with purpose, posted at random times. The profile has 300 followers and 2–3 likes per
post.
Feed B (planned content): A business page with 20 posts — organised into clear themes: "Menu Mondays" (food photos),
"Customer Love" (reviews), "Behind the Scenes" (kitchen tips), "Special Offers" (weekly deals). Consistent colours and fonts.
1,500 followers and 30–50 likes per post.
What the instructor says:
"Two food businesses in Port Harcourt. Both have phones. Both can take photos. But look at the difference. Feed A posts
whenever they feel like it — a random photo today, nothing for two weeks, a quote that has nothing to do with food. Feed
B posts with a plan — same themes every week, same style, each post has a purpose. Feed A has 300 followers and 2
likes. Feed B has 1,500 followers and 40 likes. Same effort. Different result. Why?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say "Feed B looks organised", "it has a pattern", "the posts look like they belong
together", "Feed A is just random". Accept all answers and draw out the key point:
"Feed B has a plan. It has content pillars — recurring themes that tell you what to post before you open the app. Feed A
has no plan — so they post whatever comes to mind, and the audience never knows what to expect. Today you learn to
build the plan. You learn content pillars, a weekly calendar, and how to turn one idea into many posts. Because the
difference between 2 likes and 40 likes is not talent — it is a plan."
Why this opener works: It shows, visually, the difference between random and planned content — before any theory. Learners
recognise the pattern because they see random posting every day on their own feeds.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)

Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.

Expected answer: Too tired to cook after work; worried about food hygiene.
Common gap: learners give generic answers. Prompt: "Be specific — what exactly frustrated him?"

Expected answer: Name, Address, Phone number — must be identical across all platforms for trust and Google ranking.
Common gap: learners remember the acronym but not the reason. Ask: "What happens if they do not match?"
Question 3: "In Session 1, we said channels work together. What is the journey from Instagram to a WhatsApp order for Mama

Expected answer: Instagram post → Google search → Google Business Profile → website → WhatsApp order.
Common gap: learners remember the channels but not the sequence. Have them say it as a story.
Bridge to today (say this):
"We have the customer, the home and the map. But a home with no furniture is still empty. Content is the furniture — the

what to post, when to post, and how to make one idea work five times. Because the businesses that win on social media
are not the ones with the best cameras — they are the ones with the best plans."

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What content marketing is — and why it is the engine
The idea. Content marketing is creating and sharing useful, entertaining or inspiring material — photos, videos, captions,
stories, posts — that attracts people, builds trust and moves them toward buying. Instead of saying "buy my food", you show
your food, tell the story behind it, share a tip, make people laugh — and they come to you.
Plain-English definition. Content marketing is showing your work and your personality in a way that makes people want to buy
— without directly asking them to buy every time.
Why it is the engine of social media: Social media platforms are like big markets. Anyone can set up a stall (create a page). But
the stalls that get customers are the ones with attractive displays, friendly staff and interesting things to look at. Content is your
display, your staff and your products. Without content, your page is an empty stall — people walk past. With good content,
people stop, look, trust and eventually buy.
What content does:
Attracts — a good photo or video stops the scroll.
Builds trust — consistent, honest content shows you are real and reliable.
Educates — you teach customers what you offer and why it matters.
Entertains — you give people a reason to follow you beyond buying.
Sells (indirectly) — people who consume your content regularly are ready to buy when the need arises.
Real-life comparison. Think of content marketing like cooking with the window open. If your food smells good, people walking
past stop and look. If they see you cooking with clean hands and fresh ingredients, they trust you. If they taste a sample and it is
good, they come inside and order. You did not chase them down the street — you made something good and let it speak.
Content marketing is the same: make something good, put it where people can see it, and let it pull them in.
Common misconception. "Content means I have to be a video star or a professional photographer." No. Some of the best-
performing content from small businesses is a simple photo of the actual product with an honest caption. A 15-second video of
jollof rice being stirred, with steam rising — that is content. A before/after photo of a haircut — that is content. A 30-second
tip on how to keep your phone battery healthy — that is content. The bar is lower than you think. The skill is not production
quality — it is consistency and relevance.
How a learner recognises good content. Ask three questions: Does this stop the scroll? Does it make me trust the business
more? Does it tell me what to do next? If yes to all three, it is good content. If no to any, it needs work.
F.2 Content pillars — the plan behind the posts
The idea. A content pillar is a recurring theme or topic that you post about regularly. Instead of asking "what should I post
today?" every morning, you decide in advance: "I post about these three to five things, and I rotate through them." Pillars give
your content a structure, make it easy to plan, and tell your audience what to expect from your page.
Why pillars exist: Without pillars, you post randomly — a food photo on Monday, a random quote on Wednesday, a selfie on
Friday. The audience never knows what to expect, so they do not follow. With pillars, they know: "This page posts recipes on
Mondays, behind-the-scenes on Wednesdays, and special offers on Fridays." They follow because they know what they are
getting.
How to choose your pillars: Your pillars should be a mix of:
What you sell (your products or services — food photos, haircuts, repairs).
What your audience cares about (their pains, goals and interests — not just your product).
What shows your personality (behind the scenes, your story, your values).

F.3 The one-week content calendar — planning before posting
The idea. A content calendar is a simple plan that says what you will post, on which day, in what format, with what hook and
what call-to-action. It turns "I should post something" into "On Monday at 12pm I post a menu photo with the hook 'Hungry?'
and the CTA 'Order on WhatsApp'."
Why a calendar exists: Most small businesses fail at social media not because they post bad content, but because they post
inconsistently. They post five times one day, then nothing for two weeks. The algorithm notices — and so do the followers. A
calendar makes consistency possible, even when you are busy. You can even plan a whole week of content in one sitting — this
is called batching.
The six parts of a calendar entry:
1. Day and time — when to post.
2. Format — photo, video, carousel, story, reel, text.
3. Topic — what the post is about (which pillar).
4. Hook — the first line or visual that stops the scroll.
5. Call-to-action (CTA) — what you want the viewer to do.
6. Funnel stage — awareness, consideration, conversion or retention.

How often should a small business post? Three to five times per week is the sweet spot. Daily is fine if you have the content.
Less than three and the algorithm forgets you. More than ten and you burn out. Consistency beats frequency — three posts
every week for a year beats ten posts in one week and nothing for a month.
Best times to post (general guidance for Nigeria): Lunch time (12pm–1pm) and evening (7pm–9pm) — when people are
on their phone and thinking about food. But the best time for your audience is the time they are online. Check your Instagram
Insights (free) to see when your followers are most active.
Common misconception. "I must post every day or the algorithm kills me." No. The algorithm rewards engagement — saves,
shares, comments, watch time — not raw frequency. One post that gets 50 saves is worth more than ten posts that get 2 likes.
Quality and consistency beat quantity.
F.4 Organic reach — why it must be earned, not bought
The idea. Organic reach is the number of people who see your post without you paying for it — because the platform showed
it to them in their feed or explore page. This is the opposite of paid reach (ads). Organic reach is free — but it must be earned
by creating content that people actually engage with.
Why organic reach matters: Most small businesses cannot afford to run ads every day. Organic reach is how you get seen
without spending money. But organic reach is not guaranteed — it depends on whether people engage with your content. The
algorithm (the platform's internal system) shows your post to more people if it sees people stopping, watching, saving, sharing
and commenting.
What the algorithm rewards (the four engagement signals):
1. Watch time — people watching your video to the end (or most of it). This is the strongest signal. If people watch your 15-
second reel for 12 seconds, the algorithm thinks "this is good" and shows it to more people.
2. Saves — people tapping "save" on your post. This means "I want to come back to this" — very strong signal. Tips, recipes
and how-to content get saved a lot.
3. Shares — people sending your post to a friend or sharing it to their story. This means "someone I know needs to see this"
— extremely strong signal. Funny, relatable or very useful content gets shared.
4. Comments — people writing a comment. More comments = more algorithm love. Questions in your caption ("What is
your favourite soup?") drive comments.
What the algorithm punishes:
Boring content that people scroll past.
Content that people hide or report.
Engagement pods (groups that artificially like and comment on each other's posts) — the algorithm can detect these.
Buying likes, followers or views — detected and penalised.
How to earn organic reach (practical rules):
Hook in the first 3 seconds — start your video or caption with something that stops the scroll.
Make content worth saving — tips, recipes, checklists, how-to guides.
Make content worth sharing — relatable humour, "tag a friend" posts, impressive transformations.
Ask questions — in your caption, in your video, in your story. Questions drive comments.
Post consistently — the algorithm rewards accounts that post regularly.
Respond to comments — conversation doubles engagement.
Worked example — how one post earned organic reach:
Mama Nkechi's Kitchen posts a 20-second reel: "Stop making dry jollof at parties — here is the one mistake everyone
makes." The video shows jollof being cooked, then a close-up of dry, crumbly jollof, then the fix (add more stock, lower the
heat).
Result: 500 views in 2 hours. 45 people saved it ("need this for my sister's wedding"). 20 people shared it to their story ("this
is why my jollof is always dry!"). 30 comments ("Oh no, I have been doing this wrong!"). The algorithm sees high watch
time, saves, shares and comments — and shows it to 5,000 more people. By the next day: 8,000 views, 3 DMs asking "do
you deliver to GRA?" — two new orders.
Total cost: zero naira. Total effort: 20 seconds of video and a hook that stops the scroll.
Say to the class:
"That is the power of organic reach. One good post, earned through a real hook and real value, can reach thousands of
people and bring real orders — for free. But notice: it was earned. It earned saves by being useful. It earned shares by
being relatable. It earned watch time by being interesting. You cannot buy that. You can only earn it by making content
people actually want."
Common misconception. "Organic reach is dead — you must pay for ads." No. Organic reach is harder than it was five years
ago, but it is very much alive — especially for small, local businesses with a clear niche and good content. The businesses that
give up on organic and go straight to ads are often the ones whose content was not good enough in the first place. Fix the
content first; consider ads later (Session 7).
F.5 Storytelling — the situation–struggle–solution–next step structure
The idea. People do not remember facts — they remember stories. A story-led post takes the viewer through a mini-narrative:
a situation they recognise, a struggle they feel, a solution they want, and a next step they can take. This structure turns a flat
product post into something people actually feel.
The four-part story structure:
1. Situation — set the scene. Something the viewer recognises. "It is 6pm. You have just finished work. You are tired."
2. Struggle — name the problem. The pain the viewer feels. "The last thing you want to do is cook. But you are hungry, and
you deserve a proper meal."
3. Solution — offer the answer. Your product or service. "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen delivers hot jollof and small chops to your
door in 45 minutes."
4. Next step — tell them what to do. A clear call-to-action. "Order on WhatsApp now — 0803-XXX-XXXX. ₦1,500 a plate."
Worked example — story-led post for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Situation: "Friday evening. You have survived a long week." Struggle: "All you want is good food and a couch. But the
thought of cooking makes you want to cry." Solution: "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen has you. Smoky jollof, crispy small chops,
delivered hot to your door." Next step: "Order on WhatsApp before 7pm — 0803-XXX-XXXX. ₦1,500. You have earned
this. ðŸ ²"
Why this works: It does not start with the product — it starts with the viewer's life. The viewer sees themselves in the situation.
They feel the struggle. They want the solution. And they know exactly what to do next. That is persuasion — not through
pressure, but through empathy.
Another worked example — story-led post for a barbershop:
Situation: "Job interview on Monday. You need to look sharp." Struggle: "Your last barber rushed the fade and it is still
growing out badly." Solution: "At Kelechi's Cuts, we take our time. Every fade is done right — walk out looking like the
best version of yourself." Next step: "Book today — 0803-XXX-XXXX. Walk-ins welcome, GRA."
How a learner writes a story-led post:
1. Pick one pain from your avatar.
2. Write the situation — when does this pain happen?
3. Write the struggle — how does it feel?
4. Write the solution — how does your business fix it?
5. Write the next step — what should the viewer do now?
6. Read it aloud. Does it feel like a real story or a brochure? If it feels like a brochure, add more feeling.
Common misconception. "Stories are only for long captions." No. A story can be four lines in a caption, a 15-second video, or a
carousel of four slides. The structure is the same: situation → struggle → solution → next step. Length does not matter —
structure does.
F.6 Hooks and calls-to-action — the two lines that decide everything
The idea. A hook is the first thing a viewer sees or reads — the line that decides whether they stop scrolling or keep going. A
call-to-action (CTA) is the line that tells them what to do next. These two lines — the first and the last — are the most
important words in your post.
Why the hook matters: On a platform where people scroll past hundreds of posts per session, you have roughly 1–3 seconds
to win attention. If your hook does not stop them, nothing else in your post matters — they will never read it.
Five hook formulas (with Nigerian examples):
1. The Question — ask something the viewer wants answered.
"Still paying ₦2,000 for food that arrives cold?"
"Tired of your phone dying by 2pm?"
2. The Bold Claim — state something strong and honest.
"This is the jollof rice your mother wishes she made."
"The only fade you will ever need."
3. The Number/List — promise a specific count.
"3 mistakes that ruin every party jollof."
"5 things to check before hiring a caterer."
4. The Warning — warn them about a common error.
"Stop making dry jollof — here is why it happens."
"Do not book a photographer until you ask these 3 questions."
5. The Story — open a mini-narrative.
"A customer came in for one dress — and left with five."
"I lost ₦18,000 on one cake order. Here is the mistake I made."
Why the CTA matters: Without a CTA, people who enjoyed your post often do nothing — because you never asked. A clear
CTA removes the decision: it tells them precisely what to do and where. "Order on WhatsApp" is a CTA. "Message us" is weak.
"DM 'JOLLOF' to order" is strong — specific and easy.
The CTA matching table:
Goal Strong CTA Weak CTA
Get orders "Order on WhatsApp — 0803-XXX-XXXX" "Message us"
Get calls "Call 0803-XXX-XXXX to book today" "Contact us"
Get DMs "DM 'JOLLOF' for the menu" "Check our DMs"
Get saves "Save this for your next party" "Nice, right?"
Get shares "Tag a friend who needs this" "Share if you like"
Get comments "Comment your favourite soup below" "What do you think?"
Common misconception. "A strong video sells itself." No. Even happy viewers need to be told what to do. Always end with a
CTA. Always.
F.7 Repurposing — one idea, five posts
The idea. Repurposing is taking one piece of content and adapting it into multiple formats and posts. Instead of creating five
separate ideas from scratch, you create one strong idea and turn it into five different posts. This saves time, keeps you
consistent, and maximises the value of every piece of content you create.
Why repurposing exists: Content is hard work. Shooting a video, writing a caption, designing a graphic — it takes time. If you
only use each idea once, you will run out of content within a month. Repurposing lets you create once and publish five times.
Ways to repurpose one idea:
Worked example — one menu photo of jollof rice becomes:
1. Instagram post — the photo with a caption: "Monday blues? Hot jollof fixes everything ðŸ ² ₦1,500. Order on
WhatsApp."
2. WhatsApp broadcast — the same photo sent to the customer list: "Good morning! Today's special: smoky jollof, ₦1,500.
Reply YES to order."
3. Reel/Video — a 15-second video of the same jollof being stirred, with steam rising, set to music, with the text overlay:
"POV: your Monday just got better."
4. Story — the photo as an Instagram story with a poll: "Jollof today? Yes / Definitely yes."
5. Carousel — a 3-slide post: slide 1 = the photo, slide 2 = "3 reasons our jollof is different", slide 3 = "Order on WhatsApp —
0803-XXX-XXXX."
Say to the class:
"One photo. Five posts. That is repurposing. You cooked the jollof once — you photographed it once — and you used it
five times across three platforms. That is how a one-person business stays consistent on social media without burning out.
You do not need to create new content every day. You need to repurpose what you already have."
Common misconception. "Repurposing means posting the same thing everywhere." No — it means adapting the same core
idea for each format and platform. The caption changes, the format changes, the CTA changes — but the core idea (this jollof
looks good, order it) stays the same.
F.8 Content ethics — honesty in every post
The idea. Every piece of content you create must be honest. This is not optional — it is the foundation of trust, and trust is the
foundation of your business.
The five content ethics rules:
1. Honest claims. If you say "45-minute delivery", it must be 45 minutes. If you say "made fresh every morning", it must be
true. No fake discounts ("was ₦3,000, now ₦1,500" when it was always ₦1,500). No invented urgency ("only 2 plates left"
when there are 200).
2. Real photos. Use photos of your actual products. Do not use stock images of food you did not make. Do not copy photos
from another business's Instagram. If your product is not photogenic yet, improve the product or the presentation — do
not fake it.
3. No copying. Do not copy another business's caption, idea or design and present it as your own. You can be inspired by
others — but create your own version. Plagiarism is intellectual dishonesty, and it is easily spotted.
4. No fake engagement. Do not buy likes, followers, comments or shares. Do not use engagement pods or bots. Do not ask
friends to like every post (a genuine share is fine — a coordinated like-fest is fake). Earned engagement is real; bought
engagement is fraud.
5. Give credit. If you use someone's photo, music or idea, credit them. Use free or licensed assets — Canva's free library,
royalty-free music, your own photos. Never use copyrighted material without permission.
Say to the class:
"These rules are not just academy policy — they are good business. A business that lies in its content gets caught. A
business that uses fake photos gets called out. A business that buys followers gets penalised by the platform. The
businesses that win long-term are the ones that build real trust through honest content. That is the only sustainable way
— and it is the only way we practice in this course."
F.9 Summary of the lecture
Content marketing is the engine of social media — showing your work so people want to buy.
Content pillars are your recurring themes — three to five, mixing product, value and personality.
A content calendar plans what to post, when, with what hook and CTA — for a whole week in 30 minutes.
Organic reach must be earned through watch time, saves, shares and comments — not bought.
Storytelling follows situation → struggle → solution → next step — persuasion through empathy.
Hooks and CTAs are the first and last lines — they decide whether people stop and act.
Repurposing turns one idea into five posts — the key to consistency without burnout.
Ethics — honest claims, real photos, no copying, no fake engagement, give credit — always.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze runs a small bakery in Port Harcourt — Adaeze's Bakes — specialising in custom cakes and small chops.
She has an Instagram page with 800 followers, posts beautiful photos of her cakes, but only when she remembers —
sometimes three times a week, sometimes once a month. Her captions are usually just "Order now" or "Available." She has no
content pillars, no calendar, and no repurposing system. She gets a few cake orders a month, mostly from people who already
know her. Meanwhile, a competitor — Sweet Treats PH — posts three times a week with a clear structure: "Cake of the Week"
every Monday, "Behind the Scenes" every Wednesday, "Customer Love" every Friday. Sweet Treats has 5,000 followers and gets
10–15 cake orders a month from Instagram alone.
Relevant facts.
Adaeze's cakes are excellent — as good as or better than Sweet Treats.
Her Instagram photos are professional and beautiful.
She has no content plan — she posts whenever she has a new cake photo.
Her captions have no hooks, no stories, no CTAs.
Sweet Treats uses a simple three-pillar system and repurposes every cake photo into three posts.
Both businesses are in the same area and target the same customers (mums planning parties, young people planning
events).
Decision point. Adaeze comes to you and says: "I post good photos. Why do they get more orders than me? Is it because they
have more followers?"
Possible approaches.
1. "Yes — you need more followers. Buy some or run ads to get them." — This is wrong. Followers do not buy cakes;
content buys cakes. Buying followers or running ads without a content plan will waste money.
2. "Your photos are the problem — hire a better photographer." — Her photos are already good. This solves a problem
she does not have.
3. "You need a content plan — pillars, calendar, hooks, CTAs and repurposing." — This is the right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Adaeze's problem is not her product or her photos — it is her content system. She needs
to:
1. Build three to four content pillars: "Cake of the Week" (the product), "Behind the Scenes" (trust and personality),
"Customer Love" (social proof), "The Tip" (baking advice — gives value and builds loyalty).
2. Plan a one-week calendar: Monday — Cake of the Week with a hook and CTA. Wednesday — Behind the Scenes (baking
process, clean kitchen, packaging). Friday — Customer Love (review, testimonial, customer photo). Sunday — The Tip (a
baking tip or a "save this" post).
3. Write hooks and CTAs for every post: Not "Order now" but "Planning a birthday party? This cake will steal the show ðŸŽ‚
Order 2 weeks ahead — 0803-XXX-XXXX."
4. Repurpose every cake photo: One photo becomes an Instagram post, a WhatsApp broadcast and a story. Three posts
from one photo.
5. Post consistently: Three times a week, every week — no more random posting.
Say to the class:
"Adaeze does not need more followers or better photos. She needs a system. The same effort she already puts in — taking
beautiful photos — will produce five times the results when it is organised into pillars, planned in a calendar, and
repurposed across formats. That is the difference between 800 followers and 5,000 followers — and between 3 cake orders
and 15 cake orders a month. The product was never the problem. The plan was."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (buy followers/ads): wastes money on an audience that has no relationship with the business — they will not
buy.
Approach 2 (better photographer): her photos are already good — this solves a non-problem.
Approach 3 (content system): free, uses the effort she already puts in, builds a real audience that actually buys.
Professional connection. This is the most common content marketing situation: a business with a good product and good
photos but no system. The consultant's job is not to tell them to work harder — it is to tell them to work smarter. A content
plan is the highest-leverage intervention you can make for a small business on social media. It costs nothing, uses what they
already have, and multiplies the results. That is the skill you are building today.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Posting randomly with no plan
Symptoms: Inconsistent posting — five posts one day, nothing for two weeks. No clear themes. Likely cause: No content
pillars or calendar. The owner posts "when they feel like it." How to investigate: Look at the Instagram feed — is there a
pattern? Are there recurring themes? Corrective steps: Build 3–5 pillars. Plan a one-week calendar. Batch the planning (30
minutes on Sunday for the whole week). How to confirm resolved: The feed shows a consistent pattern — same pillars
rotating, regular posting schedule. Prevention: Plan every week. A calendar is not optional — it is the difference between
random and consistent.
K.2 Only posting "buy my product" content
Symptoms: Every post is a product photo with "Order now" or "Available." Followers stop engaging. Likely cause: No pillar mix
— the owner thinks social media is just a catalogue. How to investigate: Count the posts. How many are direct sales pitches
vs. value/trust/personality? Corrective steps: Add at least two non-sales pillars: a tip, a behind-the-scenes, a customer story.
Aim for a 60/40 mix — 60% value/trust, 40% sales. How to confirm resolved: The feed shows variety — product posts mixed
with tips, stories and customer content. Prevention: Use the pillar mix rule: product, value and personality — always.
K.3 No hooks (greetings or descriptions instead of stoppers)
Symptoms: Posts start with "Hello everyone" or "Our new product." Low engagement. Likely cause: The owner does not know
hook formulas. How to investigate: Read the first line of the last five posts. Would a stranger stop? Corrective steps: Replace
every hook with a question, warning, number, bold claim or story. Use the five formulas from Section F.6. How to confirm
resolved: Every post's first line would make a stranger stop. Prevention: Write the hook first. If the hook does not stop you,
rewrite it before you finish the post.
K.4 No CTAs (or vague ones)
Symptoms: People like the post but do not order, message or call. Likely cause: The owner forgot to ask, or thinks "order now"
is pushy. How to investigate: Read the last line of the post. Is there one clear action? Corrective steps: Add a specific CTA that
matches the post's goal. "Order on WhatsApp — 0803-XXX-XXXX" for conversion. "Save this for later" for awareness. "Tag a
friend" for reach. How to confirm resolved: Every post ends with one clear, specific action. Prevention: Never publish a post
without a CTA. Write it before the caption, not after.
K.5 Copy-pasting the same caption on every platform
Symptoms: The same long caption appears on Instagram, Facebook and WhatsApp Status. It feels off on each. Likely cause:
The owner does not understand platform adaptation. How to investigate: Check the same post on different platforms — is
the caption identical? Corrective steps: Adapt for each platform. Instagram: 2–3 short paragraphs with line breaks and
hashtags. WhatsApp: 1–2 lines, no hashtags. Story: short text with a poll or question. How to confirm resolved: Each
platform's version feels native — the right length, tone and format. Prevention: Create one master post, then tailor a short
version per platform. Never copy-paste.
K.6 Using fake engagement or bought likes
Symptoms: 500 followers, 3 likes per post, suspicious comment patterns ("great post!" from accounts with no profile photo).
Likely cause: The owner bought followers or likes, or is in an engagement pod. How to investigate: Check the follower-to-
engagement ratio. Check the comments — are they generic? Corrective steps: Stop all bought engagement immediately.
Focus on earning real engagement through good hooks, useful content and responding to comments. How to confirm
resolved: Engagement rate is consistent with follower count (3–5% is healthy). Comments are specific and real. Prevention:
The ethics rule — no buying engagement, no pods, no bots. Earned growth is the only growth.
K.7 Inconsistent brand (different colours, fonts and tone on every post)
Symptoms: The feed looks like it belongs to five different businesses. Likely cause: No brand kit — each post was designed
separately. How to investigate: Look at five posts side by side. Do the colours, fonts and tone match? Corrective steps: Create
a Canva Brand Kit — two colours, two fonts, one tone. Use it for every post. How to confirm resolved: Five posts side by side
look like they belong to one business. Prevention: Set up the Brand Kit before creating any graphic. Save it. Use it every time.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can point to the
exact post with the weak hook). Teach learners to confirm before concluding — ask them to show you the post, not just say "it
is boring."

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Content means I need to be creative or funny." → No — content means useful and consistent. A clear tip is better than a
clever joke that gets no saves.
"I must post every day." → No — three to five times a week, consistently, is better than daily posting followed by silence.
"Followers are what matter." → No — engagement rate matters more. 500 followers who save and share beat 5,000 who
scroll past.
"I can copy a competitor's caption if I change a few words." → No — that is still copying. Be inspired, then create your
own version.
"Bought likes are a shortcut." → No — they are fraud that distorts data and risks penalties.
"A good product sells itself." → No — good content is how people discover the good product. The best food in the world
sells nothing if nobody knows it exists.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Pillar building drill: Give the learner a business and ask them to list five possible pillars, then narrow to the best three and
explain why.
2. Hook rewrite drill: Give five weak first lines and ask the learner to rewrite each using one of the five hook formulas.
3. Repurposing drill: Give one photo and ask the learner to write three captions — one for Instagram, one for WhatsApp, one
for a story — each adapted to its platform.
4. Calendar fill drill: Give a blank calendar with the pillars already chosen and ask the learner to fill in the week with topics,
hooks and CTAs.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the pillar names (The Menu, The Kitchen, The Customer) and ask the learner to fill in example
topics and hooks for each. Provide a calendar with days and formats pre-filled.

Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to plan two weeks of content and then write a short reflection: "If I could only do three
posts per week instead of seven, which three would I keep and why?" — prioritising by funnel stage and expected impact.
