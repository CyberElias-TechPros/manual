# Understanding the Customer and Positioning

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Foundations

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (thinking and writing, not software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Session 1 — Digital Marketing Foundations. Learners must be able to describe the four funnel stages and name at least four channels. No prior marketing, writing or design knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 we built the map — channels, funnel, customer journey — and we met Mama Nkechi's Kitchen. We mapped Chinedu's journey from Instagram to repeat WhatsApp orders. But we mapped it for one imagined person. Today we go deeper: we build a proper customer avatar (a detailed picture of one specific customer), learn why marketing to "everybody" fails, write a value proposition, and practise messaging that names a real pain and answers it.

**Preparation before class:** Print the Customer Avatar Worksheet (Section H) per learner and the "So What?" Challenge Cards (Section G) — one set per pair. Prepare a projector slide showing a weak and a strong value proposition side by side. Print the Paired Pitch Feedback Form (Section H). Have the Session 1 funnel and journey maps available for reference. Prepare one real learner example (with permission) or a fictional one for the live avatar-building demo. Instructor note on tone: This session is about empathy and precision. Many learners have never been asked to think this carefully about one specific customer. Resist the urge to rush — the avatar work feels slow at first, but it is the skill that separates thoughtful marketers from people who just post randomly. Keep the room supportive; learners will share real business ideas and personal details.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why marketing to "everybody" is marketing to nobody, using at least one concrete example from their own
experience as a customer.
2. Build a complete customer avatar for a named individual — including demographics (age, location, income), goals,
pains, objections and preferred platforms — with at least two specific details in each category.
3. Write a one-sentence value proposition that states who the business is for, what it gives them and why it is different,
using the formula: For [who], we [do what] so that [benefit], unlike [alternative].
4. List three common customer objections for a given business and write one honest counter-message for each.
5. Write three messaging lines aimed at a named pain, each following the formula: We understand [pain]. That is why we
- solution]. [Proof or differentiator].
6. Defend a positioning choice by explaining, in plain language, why the business should target one specific customer type
first rather than trying to serve everyone.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two slides. Slide 1: a vague advertisement.
"QUALITY FOOD! BEST SERVICE! AFFORDABLE PRICES! Call Mama Nkechi's Kitchen today!"
Slide 2: a specific, customer-focused message.
"Too tired to cook after work? Mama Nkechi's Kitchen delivers hot jollof and small chops to GRA in 45 minutes. Order on
WhatsApp by 6pm. ₦1,500 a plate."
What the instructor says:
"Two adverts for the same kitchen. Hands up — which one would make you stop and read? … The second one, almost
every time. Now — why? What is the difference?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say things like "it says who it is for", "it mentions a problem", "it gives a price", "it tells
you what to do", "the first one says nothing". Accept all answers and draw out the three key differences:
1. The first ad talks about the business ("quality food, best service"). The second talks about the customer's problem ("too
tired to cook").
2. The first ad uses vague words ("quality", "best", "affordable"). The second uses specifics ("hot jollof", "45 minutes",
"₦1,500").
3. The first ad gives no clear action. The second tells you exactly what to do ("Order on WhatsApp by 6pm").
Then reveal the lesson:
"The first advert is marketing to everybody — it says nothing to anyone. The second advert is marketing to one person —
a busy professional in GRA who is tired at 6pm and wants food fast. Today you learn to think like the second advert. You
learn to know your customer so well that your message feels like it was written just for them. Because the best marketing
does."
Why this opener works: It shows, in 30 seconds, the difference between vague marketing and customer-focused marketing —
before any theory. Learners recognise the pattern instantly because they see vague ads like the first one every day on social
media.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four funnel stages, in order?"
Expected answer: Awareness, Consideration, Conversion, Retention.
Common gap: learners mix up the order. Have them say it as a story: "First they discover you, then they check you out, then
they buy, then they come back."
Question 2: "In Session 1, we mapped Chinedu's journey for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen. Name three channels his journey passed
through."
Expected answer: Instagram, Google Search, Google Business Profile, website, WhatsApp — any three.
Common gap: learners remember only the first and last step. Prompt: "What did he check between seeing the post and
ordering?"
Question 3: "What is a channel, in one sentence?"
Expected answer: A place or method a business uses to reach customers (social media, Google, WhatsApp, email, etc.).
Common gap: Some learners still say "Instagram is a channel" as if that is the definition. Clarify: "Instagram is one channel.
A channel is any road between a business and a customer."
Bridge to today (say this):
"Last session we built the map. Today we put a person on that map. Because right now, our funnel and journey are generic
— they could be for any customer. But real customers are specific. A 28-year-old professional in GRA has different pains,
different budgets and different habits from a student in Chobi or a mother of three in Rumuokwuta. If you do not know
who you are talking to, your message will be vague — like that first advert. Today you learn to build a customer avatar:
one specific, detailed picture of your most important customer. And you learn to write messages that speak to their pain,
not to 'everybody'."

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why marketing to everybody is marketing to nobody
The idea. Every business owner says "my customer is everybody." A food kitchen says "everybody eats." A salon says
"everybody needs hair care." A phone repair shop says "everybody has a phone." This feels logical — but it is the single most
common reason small businesses fail at marketing.
Why it fails — the three problems:
1. Your message becomes vague. If you are talking to everybody, you cannot name a specific pain. You end up with "quality
food, best service" — words that mean nothing to anybody. A message for everyone is a message for no one.
2. Your channels become scattered. If your customer is "everybody," you will try to be on every channel, post every kind of
content, and chase every trend. You will spread yourself so thin that nothing works well.
3. Your money (when you have it) is wasted. If you ever run paid ads and target "everybody," you will pay to show your ad to
thousands of people who will never buy — and the algorithm will learn to show it to the wrong people.
The alternative — the focused approach:
"Pick one type of customer. Understand them better than anyone else. Build your message, your channels and your content
around them. Win them first. Then, later, expand to a second type. This is not limiting yourself — this is being clear. A
sharp message to the right person beats a loud message to the wrong person every time."
Real-life comparison. Think of a fisherman. If he throws one big net into the ocean and pulls, he might catch a few fish — but
also a lot of rubbish, and the fish he catches are random. If he instead studies where one specific type of fish swims, what it eats,
and when it is active, he can place a hook exactly where that fish will find it. Both methods catch fish — but the focused
fisherman catches more of the fish he actually wants, with less waste. Marketing is the same.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, two possible avatars:
Avatar A: Chinedu Avatar B: Ada
Who 28-year-old professional, works in GRA 19-year-old student, lives in Chobi
Income ₦200,000+/month salary ₦20,000/month allowance
Goal Hot, tasty food delivered fast after work Affordable food that fills me up
Pain Too tired to cook; worried about hygiene of street food Limited budget; needs value for money
Objection "Is it clean? Will it arrive on time?" "Can I afford it regularly?"
Platform Instagram, Google Maps, WhatsApp WhatsApp, Instagram, word of mouth
Price comfort ₦2,000–₦3,000 per order ₦800–₦1,200 per order
Say to the class:
"Look at these two customers. They are both real. They both buy food. But they are not the same. Chinedu wants speed
and quality — he will pay more. Ada wants affordability — she will not. If Mama Nkechi writes one message for both, it
will be too expensive for Ada and too cheap-seeming for Chinedu. She must pick one first. Today we help her pick — and
build the avatar to match."
Common misconception. "If I focus on one customer type, I will lose the others." No. A sharp message for one type attracts
others who are similar. A clear brand for students also appeals to young professionals on a budget. A clear brand for
professionals also appeals to anyone who values quality. Focus creates clarity, and clarity attracts — vagueness repels.
How a learner applies this today. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, choose one avatar to build today. In the assignment, you will
build a second one. By the capstone, you will have two or three avatars and know which one to target first and why.
F.2 Building a customer avatar
The idea. A customer avatar (also called a buyer persona) is a detailed, fictional-but-realistic description of your ideal
customer. It is not a real person — it is a type of person, built from observation, conversation and common sense. You give
them a name, a life, specific pains and specific goals — so that when you write a post or a message, you know exactly who you
are talking to.
Why it exists. Marketing without an avatar is like cooking for a guest you have never met — you guess what they like, and you
usually guess wrong. An avatar lets you prepare a meal (a message) that your guest will actually love.
The six parts of a complete avatar:
Part 1 — Demographics (the basics). Age, gender, location, income level, education, job, family situation. These are the
"surface" facts. They matter, but they are the least important part of the avatar — because two people with the same
demographics can have completely different needs. A 30-year-old and a 50-year-old can both live in GRA, but one wants fast
food after work and the other wants a catering service for parties.
Part 2 — Goals (what they want to achieve). What is this person trying to accomplish? For Chinedu: "Eat well without
cooking or wasting time." For Ada: "Eat enough food to stay full on a small budget." Goals are about the outcome the customer
wants — not about your product.
Part 3 — Pains (what is stopping them). What frustrates, worries or annoys them? This is the most important part of the
avatar — because marketing works best when it names a real pain and offers a solution. For Chinedu: "Too tired to cook after
work", "worried about food hygiene", "tired of the same old food." For Ada: "Food prices keep rising", "small portions at
restaurants", "nothing filling near campus."
Part 4 — Objections (why they might not buy). Even if they have the pain and want the goal, they will hesitate. What holds
them back? For Chinedu: "Is the kitchen clean?", "Will it arrive on time?", "Is it worth the price?" For Ada: "Can I afford this every
week?", "Is the portion big enough?" Objections are the questions you must answer in your marketing — before the customer
has to ask.
Part 5 — Platforms (where they spend time online). Which apps and websites does this person actually use? For Chinedu:
Instagram (scrolls at lunch), Google Maps (searches "food near me"), WhatsApp (messages businesses). For Ada: WhatsApp
(student groups), Instagram (follows food pages), word of mouth from friends. Platforms tell you where to reach them.
Part 6 — Messaging style (what tone and format they respond to). Does this person respond to formal or casual language?
Quick videos or long captions? Emojis or no emojis? Chinedu responds to fast, clear, professional messages. Ada responds to
fun, casual, relatable content with emojis and student-friendly language. The same business speaks differently to different
avatars.
Worked example — complete avatar for Chinedu:
Chinedu's details
Demographics Male, 28, lives in GRA Phase 2, works at a bank, earns ~₦200,000/month, single, cooks rarely
Goals Eat tasty, hot food quickly after work; feed well on weekends without stress
Pains Too exhausted to cook after long workdays; distrusts street food hygiene; bored of the same meals;
delivery apps are expensive
Objections "Is the kitchen clean and licensed?", "Will it arrive within 45 minutes?", "Is ₦2,000 per plate worth it
compared to a restaurant?"
Platforms Instagram (scrolls during lunch break), Google Maps (searches "jollof delivery GRA"), WhatsApp (orders
directly)
Messaging style Short, clear, professional; values speed and reliability; responds to specific times, prices and proof (reviews,
photos)
Say to the class:
"Notice what makes this avatar useful. It is not just 'male, 28, GRA'. It is the pains, objections and platforms that matter
most. Because now I can write a message: 'Too tired to cook after work? Mama Nkechi's delivers hot jollof to GRA in 45
minutes — order by 6pm on WhatsApp.' That message names his pain, answers his objection (speed), and tells him where
to order (WhatsApp). That is what an avatar buys you — a message that fits like a glove."
Common misconception. "An avatar is just a guess." No — it is an informed hypothesis. You build it from what you already
know: conversations with real customers, comments on your posts, questions people ask, observations at your shop. Over time,
you test and refine it. It is never perfect, but it is always better than "everybody."
How a learner builds an avatar. Start with one real customer you know well (or a realistic imagined one). Interview them if you
can — or watch how they behave. Fill in all six parts. Be specific: not "likes good food" but "wants hot jollof after work, worries
about hygiene, will pay ₦2,000 for speed." Specificity is the whole point.
F.3 Pain points — the engine of good marketing
The idea. A pain point is a specific problem, frustration or fear that your customer experiences — that your product or service
can solve. Marketing that names a pain point is marketing that resonates, because it shows the customer that you understand
them.
Why pain points matter. People do not buy products — they buy solutions to problems. Nobody buys a drill because they want
a drill; they buy it because they want a hole. Nobody buys jollof rice because they want rice; they buy it because they are tired,
hungry and do not want to cook. Your job is to name the problem you solve — clearly, specifically and empathetically.
The three types of pain points:
1. Functional pains — something is not working or is too slow/expensive/hard. "Delivery apps take 90 minutes and charge
₦1,500 delivery fee." "Cooking after work takes 2 hours."
2. Emotional pains — something makes them feel bad. "I feel guilty feeding my family food I am not sure is clean." "I feel
stressed every evening wondering what to cook."
3. Social pains — something affects how others see them. "I cannot invite friends over because I have no time to cook a
proper meal." "I feel embarrassed when colleagues ask me out for lunch and I cannot afford it."
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, pain points by avatar:

How to find your customer's pain points:
Ask them. "What frustrates you about ordering food?" "What is the last thing you wish you could solve?"
Listen in comments. Read the comments on your posts and competitors' posts. People volunteer their pains: "I wish
someone delivered to my area", "Why is everything so expensive now?"
Observe behaviour. If customers always ask the same question ("Do you deliver?"), that is a pain point. If they always
hesitate at the price, that is an objection to address.
Check reviews of competitors. Read 3-star reviews of similar businesses. The complaints in those reviews are your
opportunity.
Common misconception. "Pain points are negative — should I avoid talking about problems?" No — naming a pain point is
not being negative; it is being relevant. You name the problem so you can position your business as the solution. "Too tired to
cook?" is not an insult — it is an empathetic observation that makes the customer feel seen. The key is to always pair the pain
with the solution. Never leave the customer hanging on the problem.
F.4 Value propositions — what you give, to whom, why you are different
The idea. A value proposition is one clear sentence that says: who you serve, what you give them, and why you are different
from the alternatives. It is the single most important sentence in your marketing — because if a customer cannot understand
what you offer and why it matters in five seconds, they will scroll past.
Plain-English definition. A value proposition is your answer to the customer's silent question: "Why should I choose you?" —
answered before they have to ask.
The formula:
For [who — the avatar], we [do what — the service], so that [benefit — the outcome they want], unlike [alternative —
what they use today].
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, value proposition for Chinedu:
For busy professionals in GRA, we deliver home-cooked jollof and small chops to your door in 45 minutes, so that you can
eat well after work without cooking — unlike delivery apps that charge high fees and arrive cold.
Break it down:
For busy professionals in GRA — the avatar (who).
we deliver home-cooked jollof and small chops — the service (what).
so that you can eat well after work without cooking — the benefit (outcome).
unlike delivery apps that charge high fees and arrive cold — the differentiator (why you are different).
Why each part matters:
The "for" part makes it specific. "For everybody" is a value proposition for nobody.
The "we do" part must be concrete. "We provide quality food" is vague. "We deliver home-cooked jollof in 45 minutes" is
clear.
The "so that" part is the benefit, not the feature. A feature is "delivery in 45 minutes." A benefit is "you can eat well after
work without cooking." Customers buy benefits, not features.
The "unlike" part is your edge. Why are you better than the alternative the customer already uses? If you cannot answer
this, you do not have a positioning yet.
Weak vs. strong value propositions — compare:
Weak Strong
"Quality food at affordable prices." "For busy professionals in GRA, we deliver hot jollof in 45 minutes, so
you can eat well after work without cooking — unlike delivery apps that
charge ₦1,500 and arrive cold."
"We sell the best Ankara dresses in Port Harcourt." "For young women who want stylish Ankara without tailor stress, we
make ready-to-wear dresses that fit perfectly, so you look great at every
event — unlike ordering from a tailor and waiting 3 weeks."
"Your number one salon in Port Harcourt." "For working women in GRA who need a salon they trust, we do neat
braids that last 3 weeks, so you look professional every day — unlike
salons that rush and leave your hair damaged."
Say to the class:
"The weak ones are about the business. The strong ones are about the customer. The weak ones use empty words —
'quality', 'best', 'number one'. The strong ones are specific — they name the avatar, the service, the benefit and the
difference. When you write your value proposition, test it: could this sentence only be about your business, or could any
business say it? If any business could say it, it is not specific enough."
Common misconception. "A value proposition is a slogan." A slogan is short and catchy ("Just Do It"). A value proposition is a
clear, complete sentence that explains who you serve and why. You can derive a slogan from a value proposition, but they are
not the same. For Mama Nkechi, the value proposition is the full sentence; the slogan might be "Hot jollof, at your door, fast."
How a learner writes a value proposition. Use the formula. Fill in each part. Read it aloud. Ask: "Does this make sense to the
avatar? Would they say 'yes, that is for me'?" If not, refine it until it fits.
F.5 Objections — what holds customers back, and how to answer honestly
The idea. An objection is a reason a customer hesitates to buy. It is not a rejection — it is a question they have not yet had
answered. If you can name your customer's objections and answer them before they have to ask, you remove the friction from
buying.
The four most common objections for small businesses:
1. "Is it worth the price?" — The customer is not sure the value matches the cost. Answer honestly: Show what is included,
what it compares to, and what the outcome is. "₦2,000 for a full plate of jollof, small chops and a drink — that is less than
the cost of cooking at home, and you save two hours."
2. "Can I trust you?" — The customer is not sure you are real, reliable or honest. Answer honestly: Show proof — reviews,
photos, a real address, a real phone number, a real person. "We have been in GRA for two years — here are 30 real reviews
from customers in your area."
3. "What if it goes wrong?" — The customer fears a bad experience — late delivery, wrong order, poor quality. Answer
honestly: Show your guarantee and your process. "If your order arrives late or cold, we refund you or send a fresh one — no
arguments."
4. "Why you and not the alternative?" — The customer is comparing you to someone else. Answer honestly: Show your
difference — not by putting the competitor down, but by showing what you do well. "We cook fresh every morning — we
do not reheat yesterday's food. That is why our jollof tastes different."
The golden rule of answering objections: be honest. Never lie to overcome an objection. If you cannot answer it honestly, fix
the business instead of the message. A business that overcomes objections with truth earns trust. One that overcomes them
with lies earns refunds and bad reviews.

F.6 Messaging that connects — naming the pain, offering the solution
The idea. A messaging line is a short piece of copy — a sentence or two — that speaks directly to one specific pain and offers
your solution. It is the building block of every post, caption, WhatsApp message and advert. Good messaging is not clever
wordplay — it is clarity about the customer's problem and your answer to it.
The messaging formula:
We understand [pain]. That is why we [solution]. [Proof or differentiator].
Worked examples — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, messaging lines for Chinedu's pains:
1. Pain: "Too tired to cook after work."
"We know you are tired after a long day at work. That is why Mama Nkechi's Kitchen delivers hot jollof and small
chops to your door in GRA — order by 6pm on WhatsApp and dinner is sorted."
2. Pain: "Worried about food hygiene."
"We understand you want to know your food is safe. Our kitchen is licensed and inspected monthly — and we post
fresh photos of our cooking every morning on Instagram."
3. Pain: "Delivery apps are expensive."
"Why pay ₦1,500 delivery fee and arrive at your door cold? Mama Nkechi's delivers to GRA for free when you order
two plates or more — hot, fresh, and no extra charges."
What makes these messages work:
They name the pain in the customer's own words ("tired after work", "worried about hygiene").
They offer a clear solution (delivery in 45 minutes, licensed kitchen, free delivery).
They include proof or a differentiator (licence, photos, free delivery for two plates).
They are specific — no vague words like "quality" or "best".
They are honest — every claim is verifiable.
How a learner writes messaging lines:
1. Pick one pain from the avatar.
2. Write: "We understand [pain]."
3. Write: "That is why we [what you do differently]."
4. Write: "[One piece of proof or difference]."
5. Read it aloud. Would the avatar say "yes, that is for me"? If yes, it works.
Common misconception. "Good marketing uses fancy words and clever slogans." No — good marketing uses clear words that
the customer actually thinks in. If your customer says "I am tired of cooking", your message should say "tired of cooking" —
not "exhausted from culinary endeavours." Speak like a person, not a brochure.
F.7 Positioning — choosing who to serve first and why
The idea. Positioning is the decision to place your business in the customer's mind as the choice for a specific type of person
with a specific problem. It is not what you do to the product — it is what you do to the idea of the product in the customer's
head.
Why positioning matters. A food kitchen cannot be "the best food in Nigeria" — that is too big a claim and too broad a
market. But it can be "the fastest home-cooked meal delivery for busy professionals in GRA" — that is a clear position that a
specific group of customers will remember and choose.
The positioning decision for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Option A: Position as the affordable food kitchen for students (₦800–₦1,200, large portions, Chobi area). Option B:
Position as the fast, quality meal delivery for busy professionals (₦2,000–₦3,000, 45-minute delivery, GRA area). Option C:
Position as the party and event caterer (large orders, bookings, events across Port Harcourt).
The recommendation (say this to the class):
"Option B — the fast, quality meal delivery for busy professionals — is the strongest starting position for Mama Nkechi's
Kitchen. Why? Three reasons. First, the avatar (Chinedu) has money to spend — professionals earn more than students.
Second, the pain is strong and frequent — every working professional faces the 'what for dinner' problem, not just
students during exam season. Third, the differentiator is real — free delivery for two plates and a 45-minute promise is
something delivery apps cannot match. Start with Option B, win that market, and then expand to Option C (events) later
using the same reputation."
How a learner makes a positioning decision:
1. List your possible customer types (avatars).
2. For each, ask: How strong is their pain? How often do they buy? Can they afford your price? Do you have a real
differentiator for them?
3. Pick the avatar with the strongest combination of pain, frequency, budget and difference.
4. Write your value proposition and messaging for that avatar first.
5. Later, build a second avatar and expand — but never at the same time. One position, one message, one channel focus —
then grow.
Common misconception. "Positioning means I can never serve other customers." No — positioning is about focus, not
exclusion. Mama Nkechi can still sell to a student who walks in off the street. But her marketing — her posts, her ads, her
messaging — will target professionals first, because that is where the strongest opportunity is. Focus your marketing, not your
doors.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Marketing to everybody is marketing to nobody — focus on one avatar first.
A complete avatar has six parts: demographics, goals, pains, objections, platforms, messaging style.
Pain points are the engine of good marketing — name them specifically and offer the solution.
A value proposition states who you serve, what you give, and why you are different — in one clear sentence.
Objections are unasked questions — answer them honestly with proof, not tricks.
Messaging names the pain, offers the solution, and includes proof.
Positioning is choosing who to serve first — and why — based on pain, budget and differentiator.

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner builds a complete customer avatar for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen (or their
own approved business), writes a value proposition, and drafts three messaging lines for that avatar's pains.
Starting materials: The Customer Avatar Worksheet (printed), the Objection and Counter table, a pen.
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Choose your avatar for today. You may use Ada (the student) or create your own (e.g., "Mama Nkechi's party customer,
Mrs. Okonkwo" or a customer for your own business). Write their name and one-line description at the top of the
worksheet.
2. Fill in the Demographics row — age, location, income, job, family situation. Be specific. If you are building for your own
business, think of a real customer you know.
3. Fill in the Goals row — what is this person trying to achieve? Write at least two goals.
4. Fill in the Pains row — what frustrates or worries them? Write at least three pains. Be specific — not "likes good food" but
"tired of small restaurant portions."
5. Fill in the Objections row — what might stop them from buying? Write at least three objections.
6. Fill in the Platforms row — where does this person spend time online? Name at least two apps.
7. Fill in the Messaging style row — what tone and format do they respond to?
8. Pause and predict: Cover the worksheet with your hand. Can you describe this person in two sentences without looking? If
not, go back and add more specific details.
9. Write a value proposition for this avatar using the formula: For [who], we [do what] so that [benefit], unlike [alternative].
10. Write three messaging lines for three different pains from your avatar. Use the formula: We understand [pain]. That is why
we [solution]. [Proof].
11. Pause and predict: Read each messaging line aloud. Would your avatar say "yes, that is for me"? If not, refine it.
12. Swap worksheets with a partner. Read their avatar and messaging. Give one piece of feedback: "The strongest part is ______
because ______. One thing to make more specific is ______."
Expected result (model answer — instructor key):
A completed worksheet with all six avatar parts filled with specific details, a value proposition, and three messaging lines — all
tailored to the chosen avatar — plus partner feedback.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 5: "Are your avatar's objections the real reasons someone would hesitate — or are they your guesses? If you are
guessing, what could you do to find out?" (Answer: ask a real customer, read competitor reviews.)
After step 9: "Does your value proposition mention a specific alternative? If not, add 'unlike ______'."
After step 11: "Read your messaging line to your partner. Does it sound like it was written for a real person — or like a
poster on a wall?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Avatar demographics are vague ("lives in Port Harcourt") → "Which part? What is their monthly income? What do they do
every day?"
Pains are features, not pains ("the food is jollof") → "That is what you sell. What problem does the customer have that jollof
solves?"
Objections are missing → "If you were this person, what would make you hesitate? Price? Trust? Time?"
Value proposition is a slogan ("Hot jollof, fast!") → "That is a slogan. A value proposition answers: who, what, benefit, unlike
what."
Messaging lines are all the same → "You have three different pains — make each message name a different pain and offer
a different solution."
Learner uses vague words ("quality", "best", "affordable") → Ask "so what?" and have them replace each vague word with a
specific fact.
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
Avatar has a name and one-line description
All six parts completed with specific details
At least 3 pains and 3 objections listed
Value proposition uses the full formula (who / what / benefit / unlike)
Three messaging lines, each naming a different pain
Each messaging line includes a proof or differentiator
Partner feedback received and one improvement made

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi runs a small bakery in Port Harcourt — Ngozi's Cakes — specialising in custom birthday cakes, small chops
and pastries. She has an Instagram page with 1,200 followers, posts beautiful photos of her cakes, and gets a few orders a month
— mostly from people who already know her. She has no Google Business Profile, no website, no WhatsApp Business account,
and no clear value proposition. Her Instagram bio says: "Ngozi's Cakes ðŸŽ‚ Order your cakes and small chops." She wants more
orders, especially for birthday cakes, which are her most profitable product (₦8,000–₦25,000 per cake).
Relevant facts.
Ngozi's cakes are excellent — beautiful, tasty, and always delivered on time.
Her Instagram photos are professional-looking, but her captions are vague: "Order now", "Call us", "Quality cakes."
She does not appear when someone searches "birthday cakes Port Harcourt" on Google.
She takes orders on her personal Instagram DM and personal WhatsApp — she has no system, no catalogue, no order
form.
A competitor, Sweet Treats PH, has a Google Business Profile with 4.9 stars and 40 reviews, a simple website with a cake
catalogue and prices, and a WhatsApp Business account. Sweet Treats gets 10–15 cake orders a month from Google
searches alone.
Ngozi's average customer is a woman aged 25–40 who needs a cake for a child's birthday, a wedding, or a corporate
event. These customers typically search on Google, check reviews, compare prices, and order 1–2 weeks before the event.
Decision point. Ngozi has ₦10,000 to invest in marketing this month. She is considering three options:
1. Spend ₦10,000 on Instagram ads to promote her cake photos.
2. Spend ₦10,000 on a professional photoshoot to improve her Instagram content.
3. Spend ₦10,000 on printing flyers and distributing them near schools and churches.
What should she do?
Possible approaches.
1. Run Instagram ads. This would put her cake photos in front of more people — but if those people click and find a vague
page with no prices, no reviews and no clear way to order, they will leave. The ads would drive traffic to a funnel with a
broken bottom.
2. Professional photoshoot. Her photos are already good — this would improve them, but it does not solve the real problem.
The issue is not the quality of her photos; it is that people cannot find her and cannot trust her easily.
3. Print and distribute flyers. Flyers can work for local businesses — but they are one-time, hard to measure, and most end
up in the bin. ₦10,000 might buy 1,000 flyers and zero cake orders.
Recommended solution. None of the three. Ngozi's problem is not awareness or content quality — it is positioning and
presence. Her cakes are beautiful and her photos are good, but she is invisible on Google, her Instagram page does not sell, and
she has no way to build trust with strangers. The ₦10,000 is better used — or rather, mostly not used — to fix the foundation:
1. Set up a Google Business Profile (free). Add her bakery name, address, phone number, photos of her best cakes, opening
hours, and start asking real customers to leave honest reviews. This makes her visible when people search "birthday cakes
Port Harcourt" — the exact moment someone is planning an event and ready to spend ₦15,000.
2. Rewrite her Instagram page (free). New bio: "Custom birthday cakes & small chops in Port Harcourt ðŸŽ‚ Order 2 weeks
ahead. Free delivery within PH. WhatsApp us for prices." Add prices to her posts, add a WhatsApp button, and post once a
week with a clear call-to-action and a specific pain point.
3. Switch to WhatsApp Business (free). Set up a catalogue with her cake types and price ranges, create labels for "birthday
cakes", "wedding cakes", "small chops", and send a follow-up after each order: "How was the cake? Leave us a review on
Google — it helps other mums find us."
4. Write a clear value proposition (free): "For mums planning birthday parties in Port Harcourt, we make custom cakes that
look stunning and taste better — delivered free to your event, ordered 2 weeks ahead, unlike bakeries that need 3 weeks
and charge delivery fees."
5. Only then, consider paid ads. Once the Google listing is live, the Instagram page sells, the WhatsApp catalogue is ready and
the value proposition is clear — a small paid campaign (₦10,000 over two weeks) would work because the foundation is
solid. People who click the ad will find a real business they can trust and order from.
Trade-offs.
Option 1 (ads now): fast reach, but wasted money — the funnel is broken at the bottom. People click, find no prices or
reviews, and leave.
Option 2 (photoshoot): better photos, but the problem is not photos — it is discoverability and trust. Good photos help,
but they do not fix a missing Google listing.
Option 3 (flyers): cheap and local, but unmeasurable and unlikely to reach the specific customer type (mums planning
parties) at the right moment (2 weeks before the event).
Recommended (fix the foundation): free or nearly free, builds permanent assets (Google listing, WhatsApp catalogue, clear
positioning), and makes any future spending work much better.
Professional connection. This is the same pattern we saw in Session 1 with Adaeze's Ankara shop — and it is the pattern you
will see again and again as a digital marketing professional: the foundation first, the spending second. A consultant who
immediately recommends ads without checking the avatar, value proposition, Google listing and ordering process is wasting
the client's money. The work you did today — building an avatar, writing a value proposition, naming pains and objections —
is exactly what you would do in your first week with a real client. This is not theory; this is the job.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Marketing to "everybody"
Symptoms: Avatar is "everyone who eats food" or "all phone users in Nigeria." Messages are generic. Likely cause: Fear of
excluding potential customers. How to investigate: Ask: "If you showed your post to 10 different people, would all 10 feel it was
written for them?" Corrective steps: Narrow to one specific avatar. Give them a name, an age, an income, a specific pain. Write
the message for that one person. How to confirm resolved: Avatar has a name and specific details; message would not make
sense for a different avatar. Prevention: At the start of every exercise, ask: "Who is this for? Name one person."
K.2 Vague words ("quality", "best", "affordable")
Symptoms: Messages use words that any business could say. No specifics. Likely cause: The learner has not asked "so what?"
enough times. How to investigate: Ask: "So what? What specifically makes it quality? Best compared to whom? Affordable —
how much exactly?" Corrective steps: Replace every vague word with a specific fact — a price, a time, a number, a place, a
name. How to confirm resolved: Every claim can be verified or measured. Prevention: The "So What?" drill from the opening
activity — use it whenever a vague word appears.
K.3 Avatar without pains and objections
Symptoms: Avatar has demographics but no real pains or objections. Messages are features, not benefits. Likely cause: The
learner described the customer's surface, not their struggles. How to investigate: Ask: "What frustrates this person? What
makes them hesitate?" Corrective steps: Interview a real customer or read competitor reviews. Find the complaints and fears
— those are the pains and objections. How to confirm resolved: Avatar has at least 3 specific pains and 3 specific objections.
Prevention: Always ask "what is stopping them?" after filling in demographics.
K.4 Value proposition is a slogan
Symptoms: "Hot jollof, fast!" or "Quality cakes, delivered." — catchy but incomplete. Likely cause: The learner confused a
slogan with a value proposition. How to investigate: Ask: "Who is this for? What benefit does it give? Why is it different from
the alternative?" Corrective steps: Use the full formula: For [who], we [do what] so that [benefit], unlike [alternative]. How to
confirm resolved: The value proposition answers who, what, benefit and unlike — in one sentence. Prevention: Teach the
difference: a slogan is derived FROM a value proposition, not instead of it.
K.5 Messaging that describes the product instead of naming the pain
Symptoms: "We sell jollof rice and small chops" — but no pain, no solution, no proof. Likely cause: The learner is thinking
about the business, not the customer. How to investigate: Ask: "What problem does the customer have that this solves?"
Corrective steps: Rewrite using the formula: We understand [pain]. That is why we [solution]. [Proof]. How to confirm
resolved: The message names a pain the customer actually feels. Prevention: Always start with the pain, not the product.
K.6 Answering objections with tricks instead of honesty
Symptoms: "Trust us, we are the best!" or "Everyone loves us!" — no proof, no specifics. Likely cause: The learner does not
have the proof yet and is covering up. How to investigate: Ask: "What proof do you have for that claim? Can the customer
verify it?" Corrective steps: If the proof exists (reviews, licence, warranty, photos), show it. If it does not exist, fix the business
first — then market it. How to confirm resolved: Every objection is answered with verifiable proof. Prevention: The golden
rule: never lie to overcome an objection. If you cannot answer honestly, fix the business.
K.7 Copying a competitor's positioning
Symptoms: "We want to be like Sweet Treats PH" — the learner models their avatar and messaging on a competitor. Likely
cause: The learner thinks the competitor has it figured out and copying is the fast route. How to investigate: Ask: "What is
YOUR differentiator? Why should a customer choose you over them?" Corrective steps: Find what makes the business unique
— speed, price, quality, service, location — and position around that. How to confirm resolved: The positioning is based on
the business's own strengths, not a competitor's. Prevention: Positioning is about being the best choice for a specific customer
— not about being a copy of someone else.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can point to the
exact word in their message that is vague). Teach learners to confirm before concluding — ask them to show you the sentence,
not just say "it is generic."

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. In your own words, why is marketing to "everybody" a mistake? Give one example.
Q2. Multiple choice. Which of these is the strongest value proposition? a) "Quality food at affordable prices." b) "For busy
professionals in GRA, we deliver hot jollof in 45 minutes, so you can eat well after work without cooking — unlike delivery apps
that charge high fees." c) "Best jollof in Port Harcourt!" d) "We sell food. Call us."
Q3. Match the avatar part. Match each piece of information to the correct avatar part (demographics, goals, pains, objections,
platforms). a) "Instagram, Google Maps, WhatsApp" b) "Too tired to cook after work" c) "Male, 28, lives in GRA, earns
₦200,000/month" d) "Is the kitchen clean? Will it arrive on time?" e) "Eat tasty food quickly without cooking"
Q4. Explain. Your friend says "I want to sell my cakes to everyone in Port Harcourt." Write two or three sentences explaining why
this is a problem and what she should do instead.
Q5. Practical. Write one messaging line for a phone repair shop whose avatar is a young professional who needs a broken
screen fixed before an important meeting. Name the pain and include proof.
Q6. Ethics. A customer leaves a 2-star review saying your food arrived cold. Your business partner says "just delete it or write a
fake 5-star review to balance it out." What do you do, and why?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. Marketing to everybody is a mistake because the message becomes vague — if you are talking to everyone, you cannot
name a specific pain, so you end up with empty words like "quality" and "best" that mean nothing to anyone. Example: "We sell
quality food" could be said by any kitchen in Nigeria. A focused message like "Too tired to cook after work? Hot jollof delivered
to GRA in 45 minutes" speaks to one specific person and makes them feel understood.
A2. (b). It has a specific avatar (busy professionals in GRA), a concrete service (deliver hot jollof in 45 minutes), a clear benefit
(eat well after work without cooking), and an honest differentiator (unlike delivery apps that charge high fees). The other
options are vague or empty.
A3. a) platforms; b) pains; c) demographics; d) objections; e) goals.
A4. Selling to everyone means her message will be vague and her marketing will be scattered. Instead, she should pick one
specific avatar — for example, "mums planning children's birthday parties in Port Harcourt" — build a detailed avatar for that
person, and write her value proposition and messaging to speak directly to that person's pains (need a beautiful cake on time,
within budget, with delivery). Once she wins that market, she can expand to a second avatar.
A5. (Accept any answer that names a specific pain, offers a solution, and includes proof.) Example: "Screen broke and you have
a meeting in 2 hours? We fix cracked screens in 2 hours — walk in, wait with a free coffee, walk out with a working phone. 30-
day warranty included." Pain: broken screen before a meeting. Proof: 30-day warranty.
A6. I would not delete the review and I would not write a fake review. Deleting a review (if possible) hides a real problem from
future customers, and writing a fake review is dishonest and breaks the academy's ethics rules. Instead, I would reply
professionally: "Thank you for letting us know. We are sorry your food arrived cold — this is not our standard. We would like to
make it right. Please message us on WhatsApp." Then I would fix the actual problem (packaging, delivery time) so it does not
happen again. A business that responds well to bad reviews often earns more trust than one with only perfect fakes.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner builds an avatar with specific details in all six parts — not just demographics.
Learner asks "so what?" spontaneously when they see or write a vague claim.
Learner's value proposition uses the full formula and would not make sense for a different business.
Learner names real pains and objections — and answers objections with verifiable proof.
Learner's messaging lines each name a different pain and use the customer's own words.
Learner's positioning statement explains the "why" with reference to pain, budget and differentiator.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"My customer is everybody." → No — pick one avatar first. You can expand later.
"Quality and best are good words." → They are empty without specifics. Ask "so what?" until they become concrete.
"An avatar is just demographics." → Demographics are the surface; pains, objections and platforms are what make it useful.
"A value proposition is a slogan." → A slogan is derived from a value proposition; it is not the same thing.
"I can overcome objections by insisting I am the best." → Only proof overcomes objections. If you do not have the proof,
fix the business.
"I should copy my competitor's positioning." → Find your own differentiator — what makes you the best choice for a
specific customer.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Avatar completion drill: Give the learner a half-built avatar (demographics only) and ask them to add goals, pains,
objections and platforms.
2. "So What?" drill: Give five vague claims and ask the learner to ask "so what?" three times for each and then rewrite them
specifically.
3. Value proposition repair drill: Give three broken value propositions (missing who, missing unlike, missing benefit) and ask
the learner to fix each one.
4. Pain-finding drill: Give the learner a real business (or Mama Nkechi's) and ask them to interview a friend or family member
who is a potential customer — then write down three pains and three objections from the conversation.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a partially completed avatar (demographics and goals already filled in) and ask the learner to
add pains, objections, platforms and messaging style. Provide the value proposition formula with an example filled in.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full avatar build, value proposition, messaging lines, objections and
positioning for Mama Nkechi's or the learner's own business.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to build two avatars for the same business (e.g., Chinedu and Ada for Mama Nkechi's
Kitchen) and then write a value proposition and messaging for each — explaining how and why the messages differ. Then
ask them to recommend which avatar to target first and justify the choice using pain strength, budget and differentiator.
Timing guidance. The priority is the avatar build (Section H) and the value proposition / messaging practice. If the room is slow,
shorten the lecture (combine F.3 and F.4) and reduce the case study (Section J) to a 5-minute discussion. Protect the practical
time — a learner who leaves without a completed avatar and value proposition has no positioning for the rest of the course. If
the room is fast, let learners present their avatars to the class and give peer feedback.
Capstone link. The avatar built today becomes the capstone's audience and positioning section. The value proposition and
messaging lines become the capstone's messaging document. The positioning statement becomes the capstone's channel
reasoning. The objections and counters become the capstone's trust and conversion strategy. Keep the worksheet safe — it is
the foundation of the capstone's most important section.
