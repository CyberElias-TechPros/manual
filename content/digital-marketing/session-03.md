# Branding and Online Presence

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Foundations

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (setup and writing, not coding)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1 and 2. Learners should be able to name the funnel stages, explain why channels work together, and describe a customer avatar. A Gmail account is strongly recommended for this session (Google Business Profile requires one) — the instructor helps learners create one in class.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 we built the map (channels, funnel, journey). In Session 2 we built the customer (avatar, value proposition, messaging). Now we build the home the customer arrives at: a Google Business Profile, a simple one-page website or landing page, consistent social profiles, and the trust signals that make a stranger pay. Without this home, the journey map from Session 1 breaks — the customer clicks and finds nothing.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers with internet ready (learners need Gmail for Google Business Profile). Prepare a projector slide showing a complete Google Business Profile (a fictional one for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen). Print the Online Presence Audit Worksheet (Section H) per learner. Prepare a list of five real local Port Harcourt businesses (a salon, a kitchen, a barbershop, a shop, a school) for the audit exercise — choose businesses with a range of online quality (some strong, some weak). Have Canva open on the instructor computer for the brand kit demo. Ensure learners have their Gmail accounts ready or plan 10 minutes in class for account creation. Instructor note on tone: Many learners feel anxious about "building a website" — they imagine coding, expense and technical difficulty. Your first job is to remove that fear. A one-page website can be free and built in minutes with Google Sites or a free website builder. A Google Business Profile is a form you fill in. Brand consistency is just "use the same colours and fonts everywhere." This session is about confidence: you can build a professional online presence with a phone and free tools. Say that early and often.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why a trustworthy online presence is the foundation of digital marketing, using the customer journey from Session
1 to show what happens when the "home" is missing or broken.
2. Set up or complete a Google Business Profile for a fictional or real business, including business name, category, address,
phone number, opening hours, photos and description — with consistent name, address and phone (NAP) across all
platforms.
3. Describe the six essential elements of a simple one-page website or landing page (headline, what you sell, where you are,
prices or menu, how to order, trust signals) and build one using a free tool.
4. Apply basic brand consistency — business name, logo, two colours, two fonts and one tone of voice — across a Google
Business Profile, a social profile and a Canva graphic, using only free Canva assets.
5. List five trust signals that make a stranger buy online and explain why each one works.
6. Complete an online presence audit of three real local businesses, scoring each on a checklist of presence elements and
identifying the strongest and weakest.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two Google Search results side by side. Left: a search for a business with a complete
Google Business Profile — photo, 4.7 stars, 30 reviews, "Open now", address, phone number, a "Order" button. Right: a search
for a business with no profile — just a name and an address, no photos, no reviews, no hours.
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses in Port Harcourt. Someone searches for each one on Google. Hands up — which business would you call?
… The one with the profile, almost every time. Why?"
Expected learner response: Learners will say "it has reviews", "it looks real", "you can see photos", "it has the address and
phone", "the other one looks fake or closed". Accept all answers and draw out the key point:
"Exactly. When a customer searches for you on Google and finds a complete profile — photos, reviews, hours, a way to
order — they trust you. When they find nothing, or a bare page with no details, they assume you are not serious, or worse,
not real. That is the power of your online presence. Today we build that presence — a Google Business Profile, a simple
website, and the trust signals that turn a searcher into a customer."
Then reveal the stakes (say this):
"Here is the number that should wake you up: Google says that 46% of all searches on Google are looking for local
information — 'food near me', 'salon in GRA', 'phone repair Trans-Amadi'. And 76% of people who search for a local
business on their phone visit that business within 24 hours. That means your Google Business Profile is not a nice-to-have
— it is the front door of your business. Today we open that door."
Why this opener works: It uses a visual comparison (strong vs. weak presence) that learners can instantly judge, and it backs it
up with a startling local statistic. It frames the session's importance before any teaching begins.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "In Session 1, we mapped Chinedu's journey for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen. What were the two weak links we
identified — the places where the journey could break?"
Expected answer: No Google Business Profile, no website, an Instagram page with no plan.
Common gap: learners remember the channels but not the specific gaps. Prompt: "What could Chinedu NOT find when he
searched?"
Question 2: "In Session 2, we built a customer avatar for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen. What were Chinedu's top two pains?"
Expected answer: Too tired to cook after work; worried about food hygiene. (Or for Ada: small portions, budget concerns.)
Common gap: learners give generic answers. Prompt: "Be specific — what exactly frustrated him?"
Question 3: "What is a value proposition? Give me the formula."
Expected answer: For [who], we [do what] so that [benefit], unlike [alternative].
Common gap: learners give a slogan instead. Remind them: "A value proposition is a complete sentence, not a
catchphrase."
Bridge to today (say this):
"We know the customer. We know their pains. Now we build the place they arrive at when they decide to check you out. In
Session 1, we called this the 'consideration' stage — the moment the customer is deciding whether to trust you. Your
online presence — your Google Business Profile, your website, your social profiles — is where that decision happens. If
your presence is strong, they trust you and move to conversion. If it is weak, they leave and find a competitor. Today we
build a presence they can trust."

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why your online presence is the foundation of everything
The idea. Every channel we have learned about — social media, search, WhatsApp, email, content — eventually leads the
customer to one of two places: your Google Business Profile or your website. These two places are your online home — the
controlled, trustworthy spaces where a stranger decides whether to become a customer. If your home is missing, broken or
untrustworthy, every other marketing effort leaks.
The leaky bucket analogy (say this):
"Imagine your marketing is water poured into a bucket. Social media, ads, word of mouth — they all pour water in. But if
the bucket has holes — no Google listing, no website, no reviews, inconsistent information — the water leaks out. You
can pour in as much as you like; the bucket never fills. Fixing the holes — building a complete, trustworthy online presence
— is the highest-leverage thing you can do. It makes every other channel work better."
What "online presence" means:
Google Business Profile — your listing on Google Search and Maps. The first thing many customers see.
Website or landing page — your own controlled space on the internet with your full offer.
Social profiles — your Instagram, Facebook, TikTok pages. The places you post and interact.
Consistency — the same name, address, phone number, colours, fonts and tone across all of them.
Why consistency matters (the NAP rule):
"NAP stands for Name, Address, Phone number. These three must be identical everywhere — on your Google Business
Profile, your website, your Instagram bio, your WhatsApp Business profile, your flyers. If your Google listing says 'Mama
Nkechi's Kitchen, 12 Aggrey Road' and your Instagram bio says 'Mamas Kitchen, Aggrey Rd', Google gets confused,
customers get confused, and trust drops. Google uses consistency as a signal: if your information matches everywhere,
Google trusts you more and ranks you higher. Inconsistency is one of the most common and most damaging mistakes small
businesses make."
Real-life comparison. Think of your online presence like the front of a physical shop. If the signboard says one thing, the door
has another name, and the person inside gives a different address — you would walk away. Your online presence is your digital
signboard, door and shop assistant. It must tell one clear, consistent story.
Common misconception. "I need an expensive website." No. A simple one-page website — or even just a well-built Google
Business Profile with a link to WhatsApp — is enough to start. Expensive websites come later, when the business can afford
them and needs them. The goal today is a complete, trustworthy, consistent presence — not a fancy one.
F.2 Google Business Profile — your front door on Google
The idea. A Google Business Profile (GBP) is a free listing that appears when someone searches for your business or your
category on Google Search and Google Maps. It shows your business name, address, phone number, photos, opening hours,
reviews, and a way to contact you or order. For a local business — a kitchen, a salon, a shop, a school — this is often the single
most important digital marketing asset you can have.
Why it is so important:
It puts you in front of people searching for exactly what you sell, in your area, at the moment they are looking.
It is free.
It is often the first thing a customer sees — before your website, before your Instagram.
It carries social proof: reviews, photos, Q&A.
It has a "message" button and a "call" button, so customers can reach you instantly.
The nine parts of a complete Google Business Profile:
1. Business name — your real, exact business name. Not "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen ðŸŒŸ Best Food in PH" — just "Mama
Nkechi's Kitchen". Google may suspend profiles with keyword-stuffed names.
2. Category — what you are. Choose the most specific accurate category: "Restaurant", "Food Delivery Service", "Caterer".
You can add secondary categories. Categories affect what searches you appear in.
3. Address — your physical address. If you deliver from home and do not want your home address public, you can hide the
address and set a service area (the areas you deliver to). This is common for home kitchens.
4. Phone number — a number customers can call. Use a number that is answered — a missed call is a lost customer.
5. Opening hours — when you are open. Set these accurately and update them for holidays. "Open now" / "Closed" signals
strongly affect whether customers choose you.
6. Photos — at least 10 photos: your food or products, your shop front, your team, your packaging. Businesses with photos
get more calls and direction requests. Use real photos, not stock.
7. Description — a short paragraph (up to 750 characters) saying what you do, who you serve and what makes you different.
Include your value proposition naturally.
8. Products or services — list your menu or services with prices. This helps customers decide before they even message you.
9. Reviews — ask real customers to leave honest reviews. Reviews are the #1 trust signal on a Google Business Profile. Never
buy or fake reviews.

Say to the class:
"Look at this profile. It is complete, specific and consistent. A customer searching 'food delivery GRA' sees: a name, a
category, a phone number, hours, 12 photos, a description with prices, and 15 reviews. They do not need to wonder
anything — they can decide in 30 seconds. That is a profile that converts."
How to set it up (the process):
1. Go to business.google.com and sign in with your Gmail.
2. Enter your business name. If it already exists, claim it (do not create a duplicate).
3. Choose your category.
4. Add your address or service area.
5. Add your phone number and website (or WhatsApp link).
6. Add your opening hours.
7. Add photos and description.
8. Add products/services with prices.
9. Verify the business (Google sends a postcard or calls — this is required for the profile to appear fully).
10. Start asking real customers for reviews.
Common mistakes and how to avoid them:
Keyword-stuffing the name ("Mama Nkechi's Kitchen Best Jollof Food Delivery") → Use your real name only.
No photos → Add at least 10 real photos.
Inconsistent NAP → Make sure your name, address and phone match your website and social profiles exactly.
Never responding to reviews → Reply to every review, good and bad, within 24 hours.
Forgetting to update hours → Set holiday hours and keep them current.
F.3 The one-page website — your home on the internet
The idea. A website is a page (or a few pages) on the internet that you own and control. Unlike social media — where the
platform can change the rules, limit your reach or suspend your account — your website is yours. For a small business, a one-
page website (also called a landing page) is often enough: a single page that tells the customer everything they need to know
and gives them a clear way to order.
Why a website matters even if you have a Google Business Profile:
Your website shows your full offer — your complete menu, your price list, your story.
It gives you a link to put in your Instagram bio, your WhatsApp messages, your email signature.
It builds credibility — a business with a website looks more established than one without.
It can rank on Google for searches like "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen menu" — capturing people who search for you by name.
It lets you collect customer emails or WhatsApp numbers (with permission) for future marketing.
The six essential elements of a one-page landing page:
1. Headline — one clear sentence saying what you do and who it is for. "Home-cooked jollof and small chops, delivered hot
to your door in Port Harcourt."
2. What you sell — your menu, products or services with prices. Use photos. Make it scannable — customers should find
what they want in 10 seconds.
3. Where you are — your location or delivery areas. "Serving GRA, Trans-Amadi, Rumuokwuta and surrounding areas."
4. How to order — a clear call-to-action: a WhatsApp button, a phone number, an order form. Make it impossible to miss.
5. Trust signals — reviews, photos, a licence number, "serving since 2024", a guarantee. Anything that makes a stranger feel
safe.
6. Contact and hours — your phone number, WhatsApp, opening hours, social media links.

How to build it for free (three options):
1. Google Sites (sites.google.com) — completely free, very simple, integrates with Gmail. Best for beginners. Drag and drop,
choose a template, publish. No coding.
2. WordPress.com free — slightly more flexible, free tier available. Good if you want a blog later.
3. Canva Websites (canva.com) — free, design-first, publishes as a Canva site. Good if you want it to look beautiful quickly.
Say to the class:
"You do not need to know how to code. You do not need to pay a developer. You need a free tool, 30 minutes, and the six
elements we just learned. In your practical today, you will build the structure for a one-page site. If you have time, you will
publish it. If not, the structure is the skill — you can finish it at home."
Common misconception. "A website needs many pages." No. A one-page site with the six elements is enough for most small
businesses. You can add more pages later — a menu page, an about page, a blog — but start with one clear page that answers
the customer's questions and tells them how to order.
F.4 Brand consistency — looking like one professional business everywhere
The idea. Brand consistency means your business looks and sounds the same everywhere — your Google Business Profile,
your website, your Instagram, your WhatsApp, your flyers. The same name, the same colours, the same fonts, the same tone of
voice. When a customer sees your Instagram post, then visits your Google listing, then opens your website — they should feel
like they are meeting the same business, not three different ones.
Why it matters: Consistency builds recognition and trust. When your visuals and voice are consistent, customers recognise you
faster and trust you more. When they are inconsistent — different names, different colours, different tones — customers
assume you are disorganised, or worse, a scam.
The five brand assets to keep consistent:
1. Business name — spelled identically everywhere. Not "Mama Nkechi's" on Instagram and "Mama Nkechis Kitchen" on
Google.
2. Logo — one logo (even a simple text logo) used everywhere. Create it once in Canva, save it, reuse it.
3. Two colours — pick two brand colours (e.g., warm orange and deep brown for a food kitchen) and use only those in your
graphics. Consistency is easier with two than with ten.
4. Two fonts — pick two fonts (one for headings, one for body text) and use them everywhere. Canva makes this easy with
brand kits.
5. Tone of voice — how you sound in writing. Friendly and professional? Casual and funny? Warm and motherly? Pick one
tone and use it in your Google description, your Instagram captions, your WhatsApp messages.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen brand kit:
Asset Choice
Name Mama Nkechi's Kitchen (spelled exactly the same everywhere)
Logo Simple text logo: "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen" in a warm font, with a small pot icon
Colours Warm orange (#E67E22) and deep brown (#5D4037)
Fonts "Playfair Display" for headings, "Open Sans" for body
Tone Warm, motherly, honest — "we cook like we are feeding our own family"
How to apply it: Create a free Canva account, set up a Brand Kit (Canva's free tier allows brand colours and fonts), and use it for
every graphic you make — your Google Business Profile cover photo, your Instagram posts, your WhatsApp catalogue images,
your flyer. When everything comes from the same kit, consistency is automatic.
Common misconception. "Branding is just a logo." No — branding is the whole experience: the name, the colours, the fonts,
the tone, the photos, the way you answer the phone. A logo is one piece. Brand consistency means every piece matches.
How a learner applies this today: Pick two colours and two fonts for your business (or Mama Nkechi's Kitchen). Create a
simple logo in Canva. Write your Google Business Profile description in your chosen tone. Make one Instagram post graphic
using your colours and fonts. You now have the start of a consistent brand.
F.5 Trust signals — what makes a stranger buy online
The idea. A trust signal is anything that reduces a stranger's fear of being scammed, ripped off or disappointed. When a
customer finds you online, they are thinking: "Is this real? Will they deliver? Is the food safe? What if it goes wrong?" Trust
signals answer those questions before they have to ask.
The five most important trust signals for a small business:
1. Real reviews with responses. Google reviews, Facebook reviews, testimonials on your website. Not just 5-star — a mix of
ratings with genuine, specific comments. And crucially: the business responds to reviews, especially negative ones. A
business that responds well to a bad review is more trustworthy than one with only perfect fakes.
2. Real photos of real work. Photos of your actual food, your actual shop, your actual team — not stock images. Customers
can spot stock photos, and stock photos reduce trust. Show the real thing, even if it is imperfect. A real photo of a steaming
pot of jollof is worth more than a stock image of a perfect plated dish.
3. Clear contact information. A real phone number that is answered, a real address, a real WhatsApp. A business that hides its
contact details looks like a scam. Make it easy to reach you.
4. Credentials and proof. A licence number, a certification, "serving since 2024", a guarantee, a refund policy. Anything that
shows you are established and accountable. "Licensed by the Rivers State Food Safety Agency" is a powerful trust signal for
a food business.
5. Consistent presence. The same name, address and phone number everywhere. A Google listing that matches your website
that matches your Instagram bio. Consistency tells the customer: "This is a real business that pays attention to details."
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, trust signals:
Trust signal What Mama Nkechi does
Real reviews 15 Google reviews, avg 4.3 stars, with specific comments ("jollof was hot and tasty, delivered in 40
minutes")
Real photos 12 photos of actual food, kitchen, packaging and delivery
Clear contact Phone number on Google, website and Instagram bio; WhatsApp answered within minutes
Credentials "Licensed by Rivers State Food Safety Agency" in the Google description and on the website
Consistent presence Same name, address, phone on Google, website, Instagram and WhatsApp Business
Say to the class:
"Notice that none of these trust signals cost money. They cost effort — taking real photos, asking customers for honest
reviews, keeping your information consistent, getting a licence. But they are worth more than any ad. A customer who sees
real reviews, real photos, real contact details and consistent information will order. A customer who sees none of those will
leave."
Common misconception. "I need perfect 5-star reviews to be trusted." No — perfect 5-star reviews with no specifics can
actually reduce trust (they look fake). A 4.3-star average with genuine, specific comments — some 4-star, one 3-star with a
professional response — is more believable and more trustworthy than an unbroken line of generic 5-star reviews.
How a learner builds trust signals: Start today. Take 10 real photos of your business. Ask three real customers for honest Google

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Emeka runs a barbershop in Port Harcourt — Emeka's Cuts — in a busy area near a market. He has been cutting
hair for 10 years. His shop is always busy with walk-ins, but he has no online presence at all: no Google listing, no Instagram, no
WhatsApp Business. He keeps his regular customers' phone numbers in his personal phone and calls them occasionally. A
young barber two streets away — Kelechi's Cuts — opened just one year ago. Kelechi has a Google Business Profile with 4.8
stars and 25 reviews, an Instagram page with before/after photos posted every week, and a WhatsApp Business account where
customers can see his price list and book ahead. Kelechi's shop is often full — with customers who found him on Google or
Instagram.
Relevant facts.
Emeka is the better barber — more experience, more skill, more loyal customers.
Kelechi is the better marketer — complete Google listing, consistent Instagram, easy WhatsApp booking.
Emeka's customers are mostly walk-ins and regulars who have been coming for years.
Kelechi's customers are mostly new — young men who searched "barber near me" or saw his Instagram photos.
A customer searching "barbershop near me" on Google sees Kelechi's profile — with photos, reviews, prices and a "Call"
button — and does not know Emeka exists.
Emeka loses potential new customers every day — not because his work is worse, but because he is invisible online.
Decision point. Emeka comes to you for advice. He says: "I have been here 10 years. I do not need Instagram. My customers
know me." What do you tell him?
Possible approaches.
1. "You are right — ignore online marketing. Your work speaks for itself." — This feels respectful, but it is wrong. Emeka
is losing new customers to Kelechi every day. His existing customers will age and move away; without new ones, the
business shrinks.
2. "Spend money on Instagram ads to compete with Kelechi." — Ads could work, but Emeka has no Google listing, no
Instagram page, no WhatsApp Business. The ads would drive traffic to nothing. Foundation first, ads later.
3. "Build your online presence — Google Business Profile, Instagram, WhatsApp Business — for free, and show up
where your customers are searching." — This is the right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Emeka does not need to become a social media star. He needs to do four things — all
free:
1. Set up a Google Business Profile (30 minutes). Add his shop name, address, phone number, hours, and 10 photos of his
work. Ask his regular customers to leave honest Google reviews. This alone will put him in front of people searching "barber
near me" — the exact customers he is losing to Kelechi.
2. Post on Instagram once a week (15 minutes). Before/after photos of his cuts. That is it. He does not need captions or
hashtags to start — just proof of his skill. His customers will share.
3. Switch to WhatsApp Business (10 minutes). Create a catalogue with his services and prices (fade ₦1,500, beard trim
₦1,000, etc.), create labels for regulars, and send a monthly message: "It has been a while — book your next cut this week
and get a free shaping." This builds retention.
4. Match his NAP — make sure his name, address and phone number are identical on Google, Instagram and WhatsApp.
Say to the class:
"Emeka does not need to beat Kelechi at Instagram. He needs to stop being invisible. A complete Google Business Profile
with 10 photos and 10 reviews would put him in the search results next to Kelechi — and his 10 years of experience would
show in the quality of his work photos. The customer would choose based on what they see. Right now, they cannot choose
Emeka because they cannot find him. That is the problem — and it is solvable in one afternoon with free tools."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (ignore online): comfortable, but the business slowly shrinks as existing customers age and move.
Approach 2 (ads without foundation): wasted money — ads drive traffic to a business with no online home.
Approach 3 (build the presence): free, permanent, makes every other marketing effort work better. Takes one afternoon.
Professional connection. This is the most common situation a digital marketing professional faces: a skilled business owner
who is invisible online. The fix is almost never "spend more money on ads." It is "complete the foundation first." The Google
Business Profile is the single highest-leverage action for any local business — free, fast, and immediately visible to customers
searching for exactly what you sell. Every learner in this room should be able to set one up by the end of today.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Inconsistent NAP (name, address, phone)
Symptoms: Google listing says one thing, Instagram says another, website says a third. Customers get confused; Google
ranking suffers. Likely cause: The business changed its phone number or moved and did not update everywhere. How to
investigate: Search the business on Google, Instagram and their website. Compare the name, address and phone number
character by character. Corrective steps: Choose the correct information and update every platform to match exactly. How to
confirm resolved: All three sources show identical name, address and phone number. Prevention: Write your NAP once on a
master sheet and copy it everywhere. Update all platforms simultaneously when anything changes.
K.2 Keyword-stuffed business name
Symptoms: "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen Best Jollof Food Delivery Port Harcourt" — Google may suspend the profile. Likely cause:
Trying to rank for keywords by stuffing them into the name. How to investigate: Compare the Google business name to the
real registered/commonly known business name. Corrective steps: Change the Google name to the real business name only.
Put keywords in the description and products instead. How to confirm resolved: The Google name matches the name on the
shop sign, flyers and website. Prevention: Use your real name. Google's guidelines are clear — and enforcement is real.
K.3 No photos or only stock photos
Symptoms: Profile has no photos, or uses generic stock images that do not show the real business. Likely cause: The owner
thinks photos are not important, or does not have good photos. How to investigate: Count the photos. Are they real or stock?
Corrective steps: Take 10 real photos with a smartphone — products, shop front, team, packaging. Upload them. How to
confirm resolved: The profile shows real photos of the actual business. Prevention: Take photos regularly — every time you
make something new, photograph it. Build a library.
K.4 Not responding to reviews
Symptoms: Reviews are left unanswered — especially negative ones. Likely cause: The owner does not know they should
respond, or is afraid of negative reviews. How to investigate: Check the Google listing — does the business have a response
under each review? Corrective steps: Reply to every review within 24 hours. Thank positive reviewers. Respond to negative ones
professionally: "Thank you for letting us know. We are sorry — this is not our standard. Please message us so we can make it
right." How to confirm resolved: Every review has a response. Prevention: Set a daily reminder to check and respond to
reviews. It takes 5 minutes.
K.5 No call-to-action on the website
Symptoms: Website shows information but no clear "Order" or "Book" button. Likely cause: The owner built a brochure, not a
sales page. How to investigate: Open the website — can a customer place an order or make contact in one click? Corrective
steps: Add a prominent WhatsApp button, phone number or order button. Make it impossible to miss. How to confirm
resolved: A stranger can order from the site in under one minute. Prevention: Every website needs one clear primary action.
For most small businesses, that is "Order on WhatsApp."
K.6 Different tone and design on every platform
Symptoms: Instagram is casual and colourful, the website is formal and plain, WhatsApp is all emojis. Likely cause: No brand
kit — each platform was designed separately without a shared style. How to investigate: Compare the colours, fonts and tone
across platforms. Do they feel like the same business? Corrective steps: Create a Canva Brand Kit with two colours, two fonts
and one tone. Apply it everywhere. How to confirm resolved: The platforms look and sound like one business. Prevention: Set
up a Canva Brand Kit before creating any graphic. Use it for everything.
K.7 Claiming a business that is not yours
Symptoms: Learner tries to set up a Google Business Profile for a real business without the owner's permission. Likely cause:
Not understanding that Google Business Profile represents the business, not the individual. How to investigate: Ask: "Does the
business owner know you are doing this? Do you have their permission?" Corrective steps: Only create or edit profiles for your
own business or a fictional one. For real businesses, get written permission from the owner. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can confirm the owner's permission. Prevention: State the rule clearly at the start of the session: your own business or a
fictional one only.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact platform where the NAP does not match). Teach learners to confirm before concluding — ask them to show you the
mismatch, not just say "it is inconsistent."

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes

Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner sets up a Google Business Profile with all nine parts — not just name and address.
Learner checks NAP consistency across platforms without being reminded.
Learner's landing page structure includes all six elements with specific, realistic content.
Learner creates a Canva Brand Kit and applies it to a graphic — colours and fonts match.
Learner's audit scores are evidence-based, not just opinions.
Learner's recommendations are prioritized and realistic — one clear action per business.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"I need an expensive website." → No — a one-page free site is enough to start.
"My Google name should include keywords." → No — use your real name only; keyword stuffing risks suspension.
"Stock photos are fine." → No — real photos build trust, stock images destroy it.
"I do not need to respond to reviews." → Responding — especially to negative reviews — is one of the most powerful
trust signals.
"I can set up a Google profile for any business." → Only your own or a fictional one; real businesses need the owner's
permission and verification.
"A website needs many pages." → One clear page with six elements is enough for most small businesses.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. GBP completion drill: Give the learner a half-completed Google Business Profile (name and category only) and ask them to
fill in the remaining seven parts.
2. Landing page repair drill: Give a weak landing page (missing headline, no order button, no trust signals) and ask the learner
to identify and fix the three biggest problems.
3. Brand consistency check: Show the learner a business's Instagram page, Google listing and website — all with different
colours and fonts. Ask them to identify the inconsistencies and suggest a simple brand kit (two colours, two fonts, one
tone).
4. Trust signal hunt: Give the learner a strong and a weak Google Business Profile and ask them to circle all the trust signals in
the strong one and list what is missing in the weak one.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a Google Business Profile with most fields pre-filled; the learner adds photos, description and
products. Provide a landing page template with the six elements as section headers; the learner fills in the content.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full GBP setup, landing page structure, Canva Brand Kit, and audit of three
real businesses.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to build and publish a complete one-page website (not just the structure) and then
write a 200-word explanation of why they chose each of the six elements and how each one answers a customer objection
from Session 2. Then ask them to set up a complete Google Business Profile for the same fictional business and verify that
the NAP matches the website exactly.
Timing guidance. The priority is the guided practical (Section G/H) — learners setting up a Google Business Profile and
planning a landing page. If the room is slow, shorten the lecture (combine F.3 and F.4) and reduce the case study (Section J) to a
5-minute discussion. If learners struggle with Gmail creation, allocate 10 minutes at the start of the practical for account setup.
Protect the practical time — a learner who leaves without a started Google Business Profile has no online home.
