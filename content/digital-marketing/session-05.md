# SEO and Google Basics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Channels

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (concepts and free tools, no coding)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–4. Learners should be able to describe the funnel, build an avatar, set up a Google Business Profile, and plan content. A Gmail account is required (for Keyword Planner basics).

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 we learned to plan content that attracts and engages on social media. But social media is rented land — the algorithm decides who sees your post. Search is different: when someone types "caterer in Port Harcourt" into Google, they are raising their hand and saying "I need this now." Today we learn how search works, how to find the keywords your customers actually type, and how to make sure your business appears — for free — when they do.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers with internet ready. Prepare a projector slide showing a Google Search results page for a local query (e.g., "caterer in Port Harcourt") — with the map pack, organic results and ads visible. Print the Keyword Worksheet (Section H) per learner. Have Google Keyword Planner accessible (it requires a Google Ads account — help learners create one in class; no spend needed). Print the Local SEO Checklist. Prepare a demo of Google Business Profile insights. Instructor note on tone: SEO has a reputation for being technical and intimidating. Your first job is to strip that away. SEO at this level is three things: understanding what people type, putting those words where Google can see them, and being honest and useful. No coding, no algorithms to game, no magic. Just: know what people search for, answer their questions, and be easy to find. Keep it practical and jargon-free.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain in simple terms how search engines work — crawling, indexing and ranking — using the library analogy.
2. Distinguish the three types of search intent (informational, navigational, transactional) and match keywords to the correct
intent for a small business.
3. Find relevant keywords for a small business using free tools and observation, and list at least 10 keywords with notes on
volume and competition.
4. Use Google Keyword Planner basics to judge keyword search volume and competition, explaining what the numbers
mean for a small business.
5. List the local SEO actions that lift a Google Business Profile — categories, keywords in the description, photos, posts,
reviews, Q&A, and consistent NAP.
6. Explain honestly why "number one on Google" promises are usually false, and what realistic local SEO results look like over
time.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a Google search results page for "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt". Circle three areas:
the map pack (3 business listings with pins), the paid ads at the top, and the organic results below.
What the instructor says:
"Someone in Port Harcourt just typed 'jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt' into Google. Look at what they see. First, three
business listings with a map — names, stars, photos, phone numbers. Then paid adverts. Then regular results —
websites and listings further down. Now — hands up: if you were hungry right now and needed food delivered, which
result would you click? … The map pack, almost always. Those three listings at the top with the map, the stars and the
photos. That is where the money is. Today we learn how to get your business into that map pack — and how to rank
higher when people search for what you sell. This is SEO — Search Engine Optimisation — and it is the biggest free
source of customers you will ever have."
Then ask: "What do the three businesses in the map pack have in common?"
Expected answer: They all have photos, reviews, names, addresses. They look complete.
Draw out: They all have Google Business Profiles — complete ones. That is what got them there.
Why this opener works: It shows the destination (the map pack) before explaining the journey. Learners see exactly what they
are aiming for — and it is achievable, not abstract.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 How search engines work — crawling, indexing, ranking
The idea. When you type a question into Google, the answer appears in less than a second. How? Because Google has already
been everywhere on the internet, read almost everything, filed it away, and ranked it — so it can pull the best answer to your
question instantly. This happens in three steps: crawling, indexing and ranking.
Step 1 — Crawling. Google sends out automated programs called crawlers (or spiders) that travel across the internet,
following links from page to page, reading everything they find. Think of them as librarians walking through every book in a
giant library, scanning every page.
Step 2 — Indexing. Everything the crawlers find gets filed into a massive index — like a catalogue of the entire internet.
When a crawler reads your Google Business Profile or your website, it files the information: your name, your address, your
photos, your description, the words you used. Think of it as the librarian writing a summary card for every book and putting it in
the catalogue.
Step 3 — Ranking. When someone searches, Google looks through its index and ranks the results — putting the most
relevant, most trustworthy and most useful results at the top. It uses hundreds of signals to decide the order, but the most
important ones for a local business are: relevance (does your listing match what they searched?), distance (are you near them?)
and prominence (do other people trust you — reviews, links, mentions?).
The library analogy (say this):
"Imagine Google is the world's biggest library. Crawling is the librarians reading every book. Indexing is them filing every
card in the catalogue. Ranking is them recommending the best book when you ask a question. If your business is not in the
library — no Google Business Profile, no website — the librarian cannot recommend you. If you are in the library but your
card is blank or wrong — incomplete profile, no reviews, inconsistent information — the librarian is unsure about you
and recommends someone else. If your card is complete, accurate and full of good reviews — the librarian points people to
you first."
Why this matters for a small business: You cannot pay Google to be in the index — you must earn your place by being
complete, relevant and trustworthy. A Google Business Profile is how you get into the library. Completing it fully is how you get
a good card. Reviews and consistent information are how the librarian learns to trust you.
Common misconception. "SEO is about tricking Google." No — modern SEO is about helping Google understand and trust
you. Google's goal is to show the best answer to every search. Your goal is to be that best answer. When those goals align, you
win — and so does the customer.
F.2 Search intent — what the searcher actually wants
The idea. Not every search is the same. Someone typing "what is jollof rice" wants information. Someone typing "Mama
Nkechi's Kitchen" wants a specific business. Someone typing "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt" wants to buy. Understanding
search intent — what the searcher actually wants — is the foundation of choosing the right keywords.
The three types of search intent:
1. Informational intent — the searcher wants to learn something. "How to make jollof rice", "what is SEO", "best time to
post on Instagram". They are not ready to buy — they are researching. Content that answers these questions builds
awareness and trust.
2. Navigational intent — the searcher wants to find a specific website or business. "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen", "Instagram
login", "CEA Port Harcourt". They already know what they want — they are looking for it. Your job is to make sure they find
you, not a competitor with a similar name.
3. Transactional intent — the searcher wants to buy or take action. "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt", "caterer in GRA",
"phone repair near me". These are the golden searches — the person has money ready and is choosing who to give it to.
For a local business, transactional local searches are the most valuable keywords.
The local transactional sweet spot (say this):
"For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, the most valuable keywords are transactional local searches: 'jollof rice delivery Port
Harcourt', 'food near me', 'caterer GRA', 'small chops delivery'. Why? Because the person typing these is hungry, in Port
Harcourt, and ready to order. They are not researching — they are buying. Your Google Business Profile and your content
must answer their question: 'Where can I get jollof delivered near me?' — and your answer must be the best one in the
results."
How to match keywords to intent: For each keyword you find, ask: "Is the person typing this wanting to learn, to find, or to
buy?" If they want to buy — that is a high-value keyword for your Google Business Profile and your website. If they want to
learn — that is a good keyword for your content pillars (The Tip, The Kitchen). If they want to find — make sure your name is
consistent everywhere.
Worked example — keyword intent for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Keyword Intent type Value for Mama Nkechi
"jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt" Transactional Very high — ready to buy, local
"food near me" Transactional Very high — ready to buy, local
"caterer in GRA" Transactional High — planning an event, needs a caterer
"how to make smoky jollof" Informational Medium — builds awareness and trust
"Mama Nkechi's Kitchen" Navigational Medium — people looking for her specifically
Common misconception. "More keywords = better." No — the right keywords beat more keywords. Ten keywords that match
your customer's real intent and your business's real offer will outperform a hundred random keywords.
F.3 Finding keywords — what your customers actually type
The idea. Keywords are the words and phrases people type into Google. Your job is to find the keywords your customers
actually use — not the words you think they use, but the words they actually type. This is the foundation of SEO.
Four free ways to find keywords:
1. Google Autocomplete. Start typing a search and see what Google suggests. Type "jollof rice" and Google might suggest
"jollof rice near me", "jollof rice delivery", "jollof rice recipe". These suggestions are based on what real people are typing
— they are gold.
2. "People Also Ask" and Related Searches. Scroll down any Google results page and you will see "People also ask" questions
and "Related searches" at the bottom. These are real queries from real searchers — write them down.
3. Keyword Planner. Google's free tool (in Google Ads) that shows search volume and competition for keywords. We cover
this in Section F.4.
4. Ask your customers. The simplest method: when a customer calls or messages, ask "How did you find us?" or "What did
you search for?" Their exact words are your best keywords.
Worked example — finding keywords for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Google Autocomplete: "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt", "small chops near me", "food delivery GRA", "caterer Port
Harcourt", "party jollof rice"
People Also Ask: "How much does a plate of jollof cost in Port Harcourt?", "What is the best food delivery in GRA?", "How
do I order Mama Nkechi's Kitchen?"
Customer question: "Do you deliver to Trans-Amadi?" — keyword: "food delivery Trans-Amadi"
Result: a list of 10–15 real keywords that match what customers actually type.
The 10+ keyword goal: For your assignment, you will build a keyword list of at least 10 keywords with notes on volume,
competition and relevance. Do not panic if you cannot access Keyword Planner — you can estimate volume
(high/medium/low) based on how specific the keyword is. "Food near me" is very high volume. "Ofe nsala soup delivery Port
Harcourt" is lower volume but very specific — and specificity is good.
Common misconception. "I should target the biggest, most popular keyword." No — the biggest keyword ("food") is too
broad and too competitive. A small business wins by targeting specific local keywords that match what customers actually type.
"Jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt" is better than "food" — smaller but more relevant and more likely to convert.
F.4 Keyword Planner basics — volume and competition
The idea. Google Keyword Planner is a free tool (inside Google Ads) that shows you how many people search for a keyword
each month (search volume) and how many businesses are bidding on it (competition). It does not require spending money —
you can use it for research only.
How to access it:
1. Go to ads.google.com and sign in with your Gmail.
2. Create a Google Ads account (free — no billing info needed for research).
3. Go to Tools → Keyword Planner.
4. Enter your keywords and see the results.
What the numbers mean:
Search volume — how many times per month people search for this keyword on average. In Nigeria, local keywords
typically range from 10 to 1,000 monthly searches. "Food near me" might be 1,000+. "Ofe nsala delivery GRA" might be 10.
Neither number is bad — they mean different things.
Competition — how many advertisers are bidding on this keyword (low/medium/high). For organic SEO, high
competition means many businesses are trying to rank for it — harder to break into. Low competition means less fight —
easier to rank.

Say to the class:
"Notice the pattern. The best keywords for a small local business are usually the specific, local, lower-competition ones.
'Small chops delivery GRA' might only get 50 searches a month — but those 50 people are in GRA, want small chops, and
are ready to order. That is 50 potential customers you can realistically win. The big keyword 'food near me' gets 1,000
searches — but you will never rank number one for it against big apps and chains. Win the small fights first."
Common misconception. "I need exact, precise volume numbers." No — Keyword Planner gives ranges and estimates, and for
a small local business, the exact number matters far less than the pattern: is it local? Does it match my offer? Is the competition
low enough for me to realistically rank? Focus on relevance and intent, not on chasing the biggest number.
F.5 Local SEO — lifting your Google Business Profile
The idea. Local SEO is the set of actions that help your business appear in local search results — the map pack and local
organic results. For a small business, local SEO is the highest-impact SEO you can do — and most of it is free. The Google
Business Profile is the centre of local SEO.
The eight local SEO actions that lift a Google Business Profile:
1. Choose the right categories. Your primary category should be the most accurate description of your business. For Mama
Nkechi: "Restaurant" — not "Food" or "Business". Add secondary categories: "Food Delivery Service", "Caterer". Categories
tell Google what searches to show you in.
2. Put keywords in your description. Write a natural description that includes your key services and location. "Home-cooked
Nigerian meals, soups and small chops for delivery in Port Harcourt, GRA and Trans-Amadi." Do not stuff keywords —
write for humans, include keywords naturally.
3. Add photos regularly. Businesses with 10+ photos get more calls and direction requests. Add photos of your products, your
shop front, your team, your packaging. Add new photos monthly — freshness is a signal.
4. Post weekly updates. Google Business Profile posts (like social media posts) show up on your listing. Post once a week —
a special offer, a new menu item, a behind-the-scenes photo. This keeps your listing active and gives customers a reason to
choose you.
5. Collect and respond to reviews. Reviews are the #1 trust signal and a major ranking factor. Ask every real customer to
leave an honest review. Respond to every review within 24 hours — thank positive reviewers, respond professionally to
negative ones.
6. Answer the Q&A. Google Business Profile has a Q&A section where customers can ask questions. Answer every question
promptly and accurately. You can also add your own frequently asked questions.
7. Keep NAP consistent. Your Name, Address and Phone number must be identical on your Google Business Profile, your
website, your Instagram, your WhatsApp and everywhere else. Inconsistency is one of the biggest local SEO killers.
8. Add products and services with prices. List your menu or services with prices. This helps customers decide before they
contact you — and it gives Google more content to match to searches.
The local SEO checklist (use this for your audit):
Action Where Done?
Correct primary category GBP settings
Keywords in description GBP description
10+ photos GBP photos
Weekly posts GBP posts
10+ reviews, all responded GBP reviews
Q&A answered GBP Q&A
NAP consistent everywhere All platforms
Products/services with prices GBP products
Say to the class:
"Eight actions. All free. Most take minutes, not hours. A business that does all eight will outrank a business that does three
— not because of tricks, but because Google sees the complete one as more relevant, more trustworthy and more useful to
the searcher. That is what local SEO is: being the most complete, most consistent, most trustworthy result for your local
searches."
Common misconception. "Local SEO is a one-time setup." No — it is ongoing. Photos, posts, reviews and Q&A need regular
attention. A profile set up once and forgotten will slowly lose rank to a competitor who is active. Think of your Google Business
Profile like a shop window — it needs regular tidying.
F.6 Honest expectations — why "number one" promises are usually false
The idea. You will see adverts online: "Get your business to number one on Google in 7 days!" or "Guaranteed first-page
ranking!" These promises are almost always false — or they refer to something other than what you think.
Why "number one" promises are usually false:
1. Google's algorithm has hundreds of signals. No one controls all of them. A consultant cannot guarantee a position that
depends on Google's secret formula, your competitors' actions and the searcher's location and behaviour.
2. Local results are personalised. Google shows different results to different people based on their location, search history
and preferences. There is no single "number one" — there is "number one for this person, in this place, at this time."
3. Promises often refer to paid ads. "Number one on Google" might mean "we will run a paid ad that appears at the top" —
which stops the moment you stop paying. That is not SEO; that is advertising (Session 7).
4. Fast promises signal manipulation. Anyone promising instant results is likely using tactics that violate Google's guidelines
— and Google penalises businesses that use them, sometimes removing them from results entirely.
What honest, realistic local SEO looks like:
Weeks 1–2: Set up a complete Google Business Profile. Add photos, description, products, categories. Ensure NAP
consistency.
Weeks 3–4: Start collecting reviews. Post weekly. Answer Q&A.
Months 2–3: Begin to appear in the map pack for your most specific local keywords (e.g., "small chops delivery GRA").
Months 3–6: Appear consistently in the map pack for broader local keywords (e.g., "food delivery Port Harcourt"). Get
calls and direction requests from your listing.
Months 6–12: Build a local reputation — more reviews, more consistent presence, more local mentions. Rank for a
growing set of keywords.
Say to the class:
"Local SEO is not a sprint; it is a marathon. But it is a marathon where every step is free and every step compounds. A
business that does the eight actions consistently for six months will be far ahead of a business that did nothing — and
will stay ahead, because consistency builds trust that is hard to displace. The businesses that win at local SEO are not the
ones that found a trick — they are the ones that showed up every week."
Common misconception. "SEO does not work anymore." SEO works — but it works slowly, honestly and cumulatively. The
businesses that say "SEO does not work" are usually the ones that tried it for two weeks, saw no instant results, and gave up. SEO
rewards patience and consistency — exactly the qualities that separate successful businesses from the rest.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Search works in three steps: crawling (reading the internet), indexing (filing it), ranking (recommending the best answer).
Search intent is what the searcher wants: information, navigation or a transaction. Transactional local searches are gold for
small businesses.
Keywords are what customers type. Find them through Google Autocomplete, People Also Ask, Keyword Planner and
asking customers.
Keyword Planner shows volume and competition. For small businesses, specific low-competition local keywords beat big
broad ones.
Local SEO is eight free actions centred on the Google Business Profile: categories, keywords in description, photos, posts,
reviews, Q&A, NAP consistency, products with prices.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Thinking SEO is about tricks
Symptoms: Learner asks about "hacks", "secrets" or "ways to fool Google". Likely cause: Misinformation from online sources
that promise quick results. How to investigate: Ask: "What have you heard about SEO?" Corrective steps: Re-explain that
modern SEO is about being complete, relevant and honest. Google rewards businesses that serve searchers well — not those
that manipulate. How to confirm resolved: Learner describes SEO as "being the best result for what people search" — not
"tricking Google". Prevention: Set the tone early: we practice honest, white-hat SEO only.
K.2 Targeting only big, broad keywords
Symptoms: All keywords are "food", "restaurant", "phone repair" — no location or specificity. Likely cause: The learner thinks
bigger keywords = more customers. How to investigate: Ask: "Will you realistically rank for 'food' against every restaurant in
Nigeria?" Corrective steps: Add location and specificity: "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt", "phone repair GRA". Small and
specific beats big and broad. How to confirm resolved: Every keyword includes a location or a specific service. Prevention:
The keyword worksheet requires a relevance note — if the keyword does not match a specific service, it does not belong on the
list.
K.3 Inconsistent NAP
Symptoms: Google listing says one phone number, Instagram says another, flyers say a third. Likely cause: The business
changed numbers or moved and did not update everywhere. How to investigate: Search the business on Google, Instagram
and their website — compare name, address and phone character by character. Corrective steps: Choose the correct
information and update every platform to match exactly. How to confirm resolved: All sources show identical name, address
and phone number. Prevention: Write NAP once on a master sheet and copy it everywhere. Update all platforms
simultaneously.
K.4 Keyword stuffing
Symptoms: Description reads: "Best jollof rice jollof delivery jollof food jollof restaurant jollof Port Harcourt jollof…" Likely
cause: The learner thinks repeating keywords boosts ranking. How to investigate: Read the description aloud. Does it sound
natural? Corrective steps: Rewrite naturally. Include keywords once or twice in a way that reads like a human wrote it. How to
confirm resolved: The description reads naturally and includes keywords without sounding forced. Prevention: Write for
humans first. If a sentence sounds weird when read aloud, rewrite it.
K.5 Expecting instant results
Symptoms: Learner says "I set up my profile two days ago and I am not in the map pack." Likely cause: Unrealistic expectations
about SEO timelines. How to investigate: Ask: "How long have you been working on this?" Corrective steps: Re-explain the
honest timeline: weeks to appear, months to rank consistently, months to build reputation. Local SEO compounds —
consistency over time is what wins. How to confirm resolved: Learner can describe realistic SEO expectations — weeks and
months, not days. Prevention: Share the honest timeline from Section F.6 at the start of the session.
K.6 No reviews or fake reviews
Symptoms: Profile has zero reviews — or a suspicious cluster of generic 5-star reviews posted on the same day. Likely cause:
The business never asked for reviews — or bought them. How to investigate: Check the reviews. Are they specific? Are they
spread over time? Do they sound real? Corrective steps: If no reviews: start asking real customers. If fake reviews: remove them
(if possible) and commit to earning real ones. How to confirm resolved: Reviews are genuine, specific and spread over time —
and the business responds to all of them. Prevention: The ethics rule — real reviews only. Never buy or fake reviews.
K.7 Setting up a profile and forgetting it
Symptoms: Profile was set up months ago but has no new photos, no posts, no reviews since. Likely cause: The owner did not
understand that local SEO is ongoing. How to investigate: Check the profile — when was the last post? How many photos?
Are there new reviews? Corrective steps: Set a weekly reminder: one post, one photo, respond to reviews. 15 minutes a week
keeps the profile alive. How to confirm resolved: The profile shows regular activity — new photos, posts, reviews over the past
month. Prevention: Treat the Google Business Profile like a shop window — it needs regular tidying.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact keyword that is too broad, or the exact platform where NAP does not match). Teach learners to confirm before
concluding.
