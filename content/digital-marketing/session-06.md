# Email and WhatsApp Marketing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Channels

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (writing and setup, not software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–5. Learners should be able to build an avatar, write a value proposition, plan content, and set up a Google Business Profile.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 4 and 5 we learned free channels — content, social media and search — where the platform controls the reach. Today we learn the channels where YOU own the relationship: email and WhatsApp. No algorithm decides who sees your message. No platform can take your list away. But these channels come with the strictest ethics rules: permission first, always. Today we build lists legitimately, send messages people want, and stay on the right side of anti-spam and privacy rules.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers or phones with internet ready. Prepare a projector slide showing a welcome email and a WhatsApp broadcast — one good, one bad. Print the Newsletter Structure Worksheet and the WhatsApp Opt-In Script per learner. Have a WhatsApp Business account ready for the demo. Prepare a fictional customer list (invented names and numbers only) for the broadcast exercise. Print the Ethics Check questions (Section M). Instructor note on tone: This session carries the heaviest ethics weight of the course. Learners must understand that email and WhatsApp marketing is powerful because it is permission-based — and that violating that permission is spam, is harmful, and can have real legal consequences under Nigeria's data protection rules. Set the tone early: we ask, we identify, we give opt-outs, we honour them. Always. The technical skills are simple; the discipline is the skill.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why email and WhatsApp are "owned" channels — and why that makes them more valuable than rented channels
like social media.
2. Build a permission-based list using at least three legitimate methods (sign-up link, giveaway, checkout question,
WhatsApp opt-in) — and explain why buying or scraping contacts is wrong.
3. Draft a simple newsletter with one message, one story, one call-to-action and one unsubscribe link — following the
structure taught in Section F.
4. Create a WhatsApp broadcast list and draft a permission-based WhatsApp message with an opt-out.
5. Explain consent, unsubscribe and anti-spam/NDPR-minded practice in their own words, scoring at least 70% on the
ethics check.
6. Read open and click rates from a sample email report and explain what the numbers mean.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two messages side by side.
Message 1 (bad — spam):
"CONGRATULATIONS! You have been selected for a special offer! Buy now at 50% discount! Limited time! Call 0803-
XXXXXXX. Send money to this account to claim."
Message 2 (good — permission-based):
"Hi Chinedu, it is Mama Nkechi here. You asked me to send you our new soup menu when it is ready. Here it is — 6 soups
starting from ₦2,000. Reply YES to order, or STOP if you no longer want these messages. Enjoy! ðŸ ²"
What the instructor says:
"Two messages. One is spam — nobody asked for it, nobody wants it, and it could be a scam. The other is useful — the
person asked for it, it is from someone they know, it offers something they wanted, and it gives them an easy way to say
no. Hands up — which message would you actually read? … The second one. Hands up — which message would get
you blocked, reported or ignored? … The first one."
"Today you learn to send messages like the second one. Messages people actually want. Messages that build relationships
instead of burning them. The skill is not just writing — it is discipline. Permission first. Always. Let us learn why."
Why this opener works: It creates an immediate emotional contrast — everyone has received spam, and everyone hates it.
The good message feels like a friend; the bad one feels like a scammer. That emotional anchor makes the ethics rules
memorable before they are even explained.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)

someone gave you their email or WhatsApp, you can reach them directly.
Common gap: learners say "because email is better". Clarify: "Better in what way? What is the structural difference?"
Question 2: "In Session 2, we built a customer avatar. What were Chinedu's top two pains for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen?"
Expected answer: Too tired to cook after work; worried about food hygiene.
Common gap: generic answers. Prompt for specifics.
Question 3: "In Session 3, we learned the NAP rule. Why does NAP consistency matter for email and WhatsApp too?"

Common gap: learners think NAP is only for Google. Clarify: NAP is about trust and recognition across ALL platforms.
Bridge to today (say this):
"We have content, search, and social media — all rented land where the platform decides who sees us. Today we build on
land we own: the inbox and the WhatsApp chat. But to build on that land, we need permission — and we need to earn
the right to stay there by sending messages people actually want. Let us learn how."

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why email and WhatsApp are owned channels
The idea. A channel is owned when you control the relationship with the customer — no middleman decides whether they see
your message. Email and WhatsApp are owned channels because once someone gives you their email address or phone
number (with permission), you can reach them directly — no algorithm, no platform, no gatekeeper.

Why owned channels matter:
You own the list. If Instagram shuts down tomorrow, your followers are gone. If you have 500 email addresses and 500
WhatsApp contacts who opted in, you still have 1,000 direct lines to customers.
No algorithm. On Instagram, 10% of your followers might see your post. On email or WhatsApp, every message goes to
every person who opted in.
Higher conversion. People who give you their email or WhatsApp have already raised their hand — they are warmer than
a social media follower.
It compounds. A list built over a year is an asset that keeps delivering value.
The Nigerian context (say this):
"In Nigeria, WhatsApp is not just a messaging app — it is a business tool. Many small businesses take orders, send
catalogues, and handle customer service entirely on WhatsApp. Email is more common in professional and corporate
settings. Together, they cover almost every customer type. A business with a WhatsApp customer list AND an email list has
two direct lines to its customers — and that is power."
Common misconception. "Email is dead — everyone uses WhatsApp." No. Email is alive and well for newsletters, formal
communication, and reaching professionals. WhatsApp is dominant for casual, immediate, personal communication in Nigeria.
They serve different purposes — smart businesses use both.
F.2 Building a permission-based list — legitimately
The idea. A permission-based list is a collection of email addresses or phone numbers from people who explicitly agreed to
hear from you. Permission is the foundation of ethical email and WhatsApp marketing. Without it, you are spamming.
Why buying or scraping contacts is wrong:
It is spam. People who did not ask for your messages see them as intrusive and annoying.
It is dishonest. You are pretending to have a relationship that does not exist.
It is illegal. Nigeria's data protection regulation (NDPR) and platform terms prohibit sending unsolicited bulk messages.
WhatsApp actively bans numbers that send spam.
It does not work. People who did not opt in will not buy from you — they will block, report and delete. You damage your
reputation and your sender credibility.
The four legitimate ways to build a list:
1. Sign-up link or form. Add a form to your website or Google Business Profile: "Get our weekly menu and special offers —
enter your email." People who fill it in are raising their hand.
2. Giveaway or lead magnet. Offer something free in exchange for contact details: "Get our free '10 Party Food Checklist' —
just enter your email or WhatsApp." The free item must be genuinely useful.
3. Checkout or order question. When a customer orders, ask: "Would you like to receive our weekly menu and special offers on
WhatsApp/email? Reply YES to join." This is permission asked at the perfect moment — they just bought from you.
4. WhatsApp opt-in. Add a WhatsApp button to your website and Google Business Profile. Send a first message that identifies
you and gives an opt-out: "Thanks for contacting Mama Nkechi's Kitchen. You will receive our weekly menu and offers here.
Reply STOP anytime to unsubscribe."
The permission rules (say these slowly):
Ask explicitly. "Do you want to receive our messages?" — not a pre-ticked box.
Say what they will get. "Weekly menu and special offers" — not "occasional updates".
Give an opt-out in every message. "Reply STOP to unsubscribe" — always.
Honour opt-outs immediately. When someone says STOP, remove them from the list that day. No exceptions.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, list building:
50 customers have ordered on WhatsApp. She sends each one: "Hi! Thank you for your order. Would you like our weekly
menu and special offers sent here? Reply YES to join."
35 say YES. She adds them to a WhatsApp broadcast list called "Customers - Weekly Menu".
She adds a note to her Google Business Profile: "Message us on WhatsApp for our weekly menu."
She adds a sign-up line to her website: "Get our weekly menu — WhatsApp us at 0803-XXX-XXXX."
Result: 35 permission-based WhatsApp contacts — and growing.
Common misconception. "I can buy a list of 10,000 Nigerian phone numbers for ₦5,000 and send them my advert." No — that
is spam. It is illegal, it gets your number banned, and it brings zero customers. A list of 100 people who actually asked to hear
from you is worth more than 10,000 who did not.
F.3 Newsletter structure — one message, one story, one CTA
The idea. A newsletter is a regular email (weekly or monthly) sent to your permission-based email list. The best newsletters are
not catalogues of everything you sell — they are focused, useful and human. One message, one story, one call-to-action, one
unsubscribe link.
Why newsletters work:
They keep you in your customer's mind between purchases.
They build a relationship — people buy from people they trust.
They drive repeat business — a weekly reminder with a special offer brings orders.
They are measurable — you can see opens, clicks and unsubscribes.
The six parts of a simple newsletter:
1. Subject line — the most important line. It decides whether the email is opened. Make it specific and intriguing: "This
week's soup: Ofe nsala ðŸ ²" not "Newsletter #12".
2. Preview text — the short snippet that appears next to the subject line. Use it to extend the hook: "Plus: 2 tips for perfect
party jollof".
3. Greeting — "Hi Chinedu," — personal, warm.
4. One story or message — the body. Tell one story, share one tip, or highlight one offer. Keep it short — 3–5 sentences.
Example: "This week we are cooking Ofe nsala — white soup with goat meat. It is rich, spicy and perfect for the rainy
weather. We only make 20 plates, so order early."
5. One call-to-action — what should they do? "Reply YES to order" or "Click here to see the full menu". One action, not
three.
6. Unsubscribe link — required by law and by ethics. "Reply STOP to unsubscribe" or an automatic unsubscribe link in email
marketing tools.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, weekly newsletter:
Subject: This week's soup: Ofe nsala ðŸ ² Preview: Plus: 2 tips for perfect party jollof
Hi Chinedu,
This week we are cooking Ofe nsala — white soup with goat meat, utazi and uziza. It is rich, spicy and perfect for this
rainy weather.
We only make 20 plates, so order early. ₦2,500 a plate, delivered hot to your door in GRA.
Reply YES to order yours this week.
Mama Nkechi ðŸ ²
P.S. Reply STOP anytime if you no longer want these messages.
Say to the class:
"One message. One story. One CTA. One unsubscribe. That is a newsletter. Notice it does not list everything on the menu
— it highlights ONE thing (the soup), tells a little story (rainy weather, only 20 plates), gives a price and a delivery
promise, and asks for one action. That is it. A newsletter that tries to say everything says nothing."
How often to send: Weekly or monthly — whatever you can sustain. A weekly newsletter you actually send beats a daily plan
you abandon. Consistency is the magic.
Common misconception. "A newsletter must be long and professional." No — short and human beats long and corporate.
Your customers are people, not machines. Write like you are messaging a friend who happens to be a customer.
F.4 WhatsApp Business — the tool, the catalogue, the broadcast
The idea. WhatsApp Business is a free app from WhatsApp designed for small businesses. It lets you create a business profile, a
catalogue of products, labels for customers, quick replies and broadcast lists. For many Nigerian small businesses, WhatsApp
Business is the entire sales system.
The four key features:
1. Business profile. Your name, description, address, hours, email and website. This is the first thing a customer sees when they
open your chat. Complete it fully — it builds trust.
2. Catalogue. A list of your products with photos, prices and descriptions. For Mama Nkechi: "Jollof Rice — ₦1,500 — Smoky
jollof with fried plantain", "Small Chops (20 pcs) — ₦1,200 — Spring rolls, samosa, peppered chicken". The catalogue lets
customers browse and order without asking.
3. Labels. Colour-coded tags to organise customers: "New customer", "Regular", "Catering enquiry", "Awaiting payment".
Labels help you send the right message to the right people.
4. Broadcast lists. Send one message to many people at once — but only to people who have your number saved AND have
opted in. This is the key difference from group messaging: broadcasts are one-to-many, private (each person sees it as an
individual message), and respect the opt-in.
The WhatsApp broadcast rules (say these slowly):
Only message people who opted in. They must have your number saved AND agreed to receive messages.
Identify yourself in the first message. "This is Mama Nkechi's Kitchen."
Give an opt-out in every broadcast. "Reply STOP to unsubscribe."
Honour opt-outs immediately. Remove them from the broadcast list.
Do not spam groups. Do not add people to WhatsApp groups without permission. Group-blasting is spam.
Respect WhatsApp's limits. WhatsApp limits broadcast lists to 256 contacts per list. Work within that.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, WhatsApp broadcast:
Subject (first line): Mama Nkechi's Kitchen ðŸ ²
Message: Good morning! This week's special: Ofe nsala — white soup with goat meat. ₦2,500 a plate. Only 20 plates
available.
Reply YES to order, or STOP if you no longer want these messages.
Mama Nkechi's Kitchen · 0803-XXX-XXXX
Common misconception. "I can add all my contacts to a broadcast list and send adverts." No — WhatsApp broadcasts only
work reliably for people who have your number saved. And sending adverts to people who did not opt in is spam — it gets
your number banned. The broadcast list must be built on permission, just like email.
F.5 Email marketing on a free tier — sending and measuring
The idea. To send newsletters to more than a handful of people, you need an email tool. Free tiers exist: Mailchimp (free for up
to 500 contacts), Brevo (free for up to 300 emails/day), or others. These tools handle the unsubscribe links, the formatting and
the delivery — and they give you reports.
The three key metrics:
1. Open rate — the percentage of people who opened your email. A healthy open rate for a small business is 20–40%. If it
is low, your subject lines are weak or your list is cold.
2. Click-through rate (CTR) — the percentage of people who clicked a link in your email. A healthy CTR is 2–5%. If it is low,
your content or your CTA is not compelling.
3. Unsubscribe rate — the percentage of people who opted out. A healthy unsubscribe rate is below 0.5% per email. If it is
high, you are sending too often, to the wrong people, or with the wrong content.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, email report after one newsletter:
Metric Number Rate What it means
Sent 35 100% 35 people on the list
Opened 14 40% Healthy — subject line worked
Clicked 3 8.6% of opens Good — the soup offer was compelling
Unsubscribed 0 0% Nobody wanted to leave — content is relevant
Say to the class:
"Forty percent open rate is good. Eight-point-six percent click rate is good. Zero unsubscribes is excellent. This newsletter
worked because it went to 35 people who asked for it, with a clear subject line, one useful story and one easy action. That is
email marketing. Not complicated — just disciplined."
Common misconception. "I need thousands of subscribers for email to work." No — 50 engaged subscribers who actually
open and click are worth more than 5,000 who ignore you. Start small. Build carefully. Grow slowly.
F.6 Consent, unsubscribe and anti-spam/NDPR-minded practice
The idea. This is the most important section of the session. The power of email and WhatsApp comes with responsibility.
Misusing these channels is spam — and spam has real consequences.
The five rules of permission-based messaging:
1. Consent first. Only message people who explicitly agreed. Consent can be:
Express — they filled a form, replied YES, or asked to join.
Inferred — they bought from you and you asked if they want updates (but you must still give an opt-out).
Never assume consent. If in doubt, ask.
2. Identify yourself. Every message must say who it is from. "This is Mama Nkechi's Kitchen." No fake names, no misleading
sender details.
3. Give an easy opt-out. Every message must include a way to unsubscribe. "Reply STOP" or an automatic link. No hoops, no
"reply to this number to unsubscribe" — just one clear action.
4. Honour opt-outs immediately. When someone says STOP, remove them from the list that day. Do not send "are you sure?"
messages. Do not delay. Immediate removal is the rule.
5. Respect data (NDPR-minded). Collect only what you need. Use it only for the agreed purpose. Keep it secure. Never sell it.
Delete it when no longer needed. Nigeria's Data Protection Regulation expects this — and platforms enforce it.
What spam looks like (and why it is harmful):
Messages to people who did not agree.
Misleading subject lines ("Your account is blocked!" when it is not).
No opt-out or a broken opt-out.
Fake sender identities.
Buying lists and blasting adverts.
The consequences of spam:
WhatsApp bans numbers that send spam. A banned number means losing your business line.
Email platforms block senders with high spam complaints — your legitimate emails stop reaching anyone.
Customers lose trust in your brand — spam damages reputation permanently.
Legal — Nigeria's data protection regulation and platform terms carry real penalties for unsolicited bulk messaging.
Say to the class:
"Spam is not just annoying — it is a trust destroyer. Every spam message you send tells a customer: 'I do not respect your
time or your choice.' And once that trust is gone, no amount of good content wins it back. Permission-based messaging is
not just the ethical choice — it is the effective choice. People who asked to hear from you will buy from you. People who
did not will never buy — and will make sure others do not either."
Common misconception. "But businesses spam WhatsApp groups all the time and they seem fine." Some do — until they do
not. WhatsApp's enforcement is increasing, and the businesses that build permission-based lists are the ones that survive long-
term. Short-term spam is a shortcut to a dead end.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Email and WhatsApp are owned channels — you control the relationship, no algorithm decides reach.
Build lists legitimately: sign-up forms, giveaways, checkout questions, WhatsApp opt-ins. Never buy or scrape contacts.
Newsletters have six parts: subject line, preview text, greeting, one story, one CTA, one unsubscribe link.
WhatsApp Business gives you a profile, catalogue, labels and broadcasts — all permission-based.
Measure open rate, click rate and unsubscribe rate — they tell you what is working.
Ethics first: consent, identification, opt-out, honouring opt-outs, NDPR-minded data handling.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze runs a small event planning business in Port Harcourt — Glam Events PH — specialising in birthday parties
and small weddings. She has 200 contacts on her personal WhatsApp — past customers, enquiries and people who asked for
quotes. She wants to send them a monthly message with party planning tips and special package offers. She also has 40 email
addresses from past customers. Her business partner says: "Just add all 200 WhatsApp contacts to a group and send the advert
— they all asked for quotes, so they are interested." Adaeze is unsure — she does not want to annoy people, but she also does
not want to miss the opportunity.
Relevant facts.
Of the 200 WhatsApp contacts, only 60 actually became customers. The other 140 asked for quotes but never booked.
None of the 200 contacts explicitly agreed to receive marketing messages.
The 40 email addresses are from past customers — they gave their email when booking, but were not asked if they want
updates.
Adaeze's business partner wants to create a WhatsApp group, add all 200 contacts and send monthly adverts.
A competitor, Sparkle Events, sends a monthly WhatsApp broadcast — but only to 80 people who replied YES when asked.
Decision point. What should Adaeze do?
Possible approaches.
1. Add all 200 contacts to a group and send adverts. This is spam — most did not agree, group-blasting is against
WhatsApp's rules, and it will damage her reputation and get her number banned.
2. Do nothing — it is too risky. This is safe but wasteful — she has 200 warm contacts and is not using them.
3. Ask permission first — send a polite opt-in message to all 200 contacts, and only add those who say YES. This is the
right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Adaeze should:
1. Send an opt-in message to all 200 WhatsApp contacts:
"Hi! This is Adaeze from Glam Events PH. I would like to send you monthly party planning tips and special package
offers on WhatsApp. Reply YES if you would like to join, or ignore this message if not. You can leave anytime by
replying STOP."
2. Only add the people who reply YES to her broadcast list. If 80 say yes, she has a permission-based list of 80.
3. Send a monthly newsletter to the 40 email addresses — but first, send one email asking: "Thank you for being a
customer. Would you like our monthly party tips and offers? Reply YES to confirm." Only keep those who confirm.
4. Never add anyone who did not explicitly say YES. No assumptions, no "they asked for a quote so they are interested".
5. Honour every STOP immediately. No exceptions.
Say to the class:
"Adaeze's business partner means well — but that approach would get her number banned and her reputation
damaged. The permission-first approach takes one extra message, but it builds a list of people who actually want to hear
from her. Those 80 people who say YES are worth more than 200 who did not. That is the power of permission — quality
over quantity, always."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (group spam): fast but destructive — banned number, damaged reputation, zero trust.
Approach 2 (do nothing): safe but wasteful — 200 warm contacts going unused.
Approach 3 (permission first): one extra step, builds a valuable owned asset, sustainable and legal.
Professional connection. This is the most common email/WhatsApp dilemma: a business has a pile of contacts and wants to
market to them. The answer is always the same — ask first. The businesses that build permission-based lists are the ones that
have direct customer relationships for years. The ones that spam are the ones that wonder why their number got banned and
their messages get ignored. Permission is not a barrier — it is the foundation.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Buying or scraping contact lists
Symptoms: Learner says "I can buy a list of Nigerian phone numbers for ₦5,000." Likely cause: Not understanding that
purchased lists are spam and illegal. How to investigate: Ask: "Did those people agree to hear from you?" Corrective steps:
Explain the four legitimate list-building methods. Emphasise that bought lists bring zero customers and real risk. How to
confirm resolved: Learner can explain why buying lists is wrong and name two legitimate alternatives. Prevention: The ethics
note at the start of the session — and the opening activity contrast.
K.2 No opt-out in messages
Symptoms: Newsletter or broadcast has no "reply STOP" or unsubscribe link. Likely cause: The learner forgot, or thinks it is not
necessary. How to investigate: Read the message — is there an opt-out? Corrective steps: Add an opt-out to every message.
It is the law of the course and the practice of professional marketing. How to confirm resolved: Every drafted message includes
a clear opt-out. Prevention: The six-part newsletter structure includes the unsubscribe as a required part.
K.3 Not honouring opt-outs
Symptoms: Someone replies STOP but stays on the list. Likely cause: The learner does not have a system for removing opt-
outs. How to investigate: Ask: "What do you do when someone replies STOP?" Corrective steps: Establish a rule: when
someone opts out, remove them immediately. On WhatsApp, delete them from the broadcast list. On email, use the platform's
automatic unsubscribe. How to confirm resolved: Learner can describe the exact steps to honour an opt-out. Prevention: The
permission checklist includes "how will you honour opt-outs?"
K.4 Group-blasting on WhatsApp
Symptoms: Adding people to WhatsApp groups to send adverts. Likely cause: Confusing groups with broadcast lists. How to
investigate: Ask: "Are you sending to a group or a broadcast list?" Corrective steps: Explain the difference: broadcasts are
private one-to-many messages to people who opted in. Groups are multi-person conversations — adding people to groups
without permission is spam. How to confirm resolved: Learner can explain the difference and why groups are not for adverts.
Prevention: The WhatsApp broadcast rules in Section F.4.
K.5 Sending too frequently
Symptoms: Daily broadcasts or newsletters — subscribers start ignoring or leaving. Likely cause: Enthusiasm without
discipline. How to investigate: Ask: "How often are you planning to send? Can you sustain that?" Corrective steps: Set a
realistic schedule — weekly or monthly — and stick to it. Consistency beats frequency. How to confirm resolved: Learner has
a sustainable schedule. Prevention: The newsletter section emphasises: weekly or monthly, whatever you can sustain.
K.6 Vague subject lines
Symptoms: "Newsletter", "Update", "Hello" — low open rates. Likely cause: The learner does not know what makes a good
subject line. How to investigate: Show the last subject line. Would you open it? Corrective steps: Make it specific and
intriguing: "This week's soup: Ofe nsala ðŸ ²" not "Newsletter #12". How to confirm resolved: Every subject line is specific and
would make the reader curious. Prevention: The newsletter structure worksheet requires a specific subject line.
K.7 Assuming consent from a business relationship
Symptoms: "They bought from me, so I can send them adverts." Likely cause: Confusing a transaction with consent. How to
investigate: Ask: "Did they explicitly agree to receive marketing messages?" Corrective steps: Explain that a purchase is not
consent. Ask explicitly: "Would you like our weekly menu?" Only those who say yes are on the list. How to confirm resolved:
Learner asks for permission even from existing customers. Prevention: The permission rules in Section F.2 and the permission
script exercise.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact message without an opt-out). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Newsletter repair drill: Give a broken newsletter (no subject, no CTA, no unsubscribe) and ask the learner to fix all three.
2. Permission script drill: Give a scenario (a salon, a shop, a school) and ask the learner to write a 3-sentence opt-in script.
3. Opt-out drill: Ask the learner to write the exact steps they would take when someone replies STOP on WhatsApp and on
email.
4. Channel sorting drill: Give 10 channel examples (Instagram, email, Google Maps, WhatsApp, paid ads, website, TikTok, SMS,
flyers, word of mouth) and ask the learner to classify each as owned, rented or paid.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the newsletter structure with the story already written — the learner adds the subject line,
CTA and unsubscribe. Provide a fill-in-the-blank permission script.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full newsletter, broadcast, permission script and list-building plan.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to design a 4-week email sequence for a new customer: a welcome email, a tip email,
an offer email and a follow-up — each with all six parts. Then explain how the sequence builds trust and drives a first
purchase.
Timing guidance. The priority is the guided practical (Section H) — learners drafting a newsletter and broadcast with the ethics

discussion. If learners struggle with the ethics rules, run an extra 5-minute scenario drill: "Is this spam?" with five quick examples.
Protect the practical time — a learner who leaves without a permission-based newsletter and broadcast has no owned-channel
plan.
