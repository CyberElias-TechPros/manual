# Paid Ads Basics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Convert and Measure

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (concepts and structure, not heavy software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–6. Learners should be able to build an avatar, write a value proposition, plan content, set up a Google Business Profile, and draft permission-based messages. No prior ads experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 4–6 we learned the free channels — content, search, email and WhatsApp. Today we learn the channel that can accelerate everything: paid advertising. We will compare Meta and Google ads, learn how to choose an objective, define an audience, set a naira budget, and understand ad structure. No ad spend is required — we learn the structure and the maths, and watch a small academy-funded demonstration.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers or phones with internet ready. Prepare projector slides comparing Meta ads and Google ads. Print the Campaign Structure Sheet (Section H) per learner. Prepare a sample budget table showing what ₦2,000, ₦10,000 and ₦50,000 per week can realistically buy. Have the Meta Ads Manager and Google Ads interfaces ready for a demo (no spend). Prepare a fictional case brief for the independent exercise. If the academy funds a small demonstration budget (₦2,000–₦5,000), prepare that approval and setup. Instructor note on tone: Paid ads can feel intimidating — learners worry about wasting money. Your job is to replace fear with structure. Ads are not gambling; they are a system with clear parts: objective, audience, creative, budget and measurement. When the maths works, ads work. When it does not, they do not. Today we learn to make the maths work — and to never spend more than we can afford to lose. Emphasise: this session teaches structure and thinking, not "how to spend money."

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain the difference between Meta ads and Google ads — who they reach, when they reach them, and what each is best
for.
2. Choose a campaign objective that matches a stated business goal, using the funnel stages from Session 1.
3. Define a target audience for a small local business, using location, interests and behaviours — and explain why
"everyone" is the wrong audience.
4. Describe the three parts of ad structure — campaign, ad set and ad — and what belongs at each level.
5. Propose a daily budget in naira that matches a stated goal, and explain what that budget can realistically buy (and what it
cannot).
6. Judge ad creative and landing-page fit — whether an ad's promise matches what the customer finds when they click.
7. Apply honest advertising rules — no fake discounts, no misleading claims, no buying engagement — to ad creative and
copy.

"And here is the most important thing: paid ads are renting attention. While you pay, you are seen. When you stop, you

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Meta ads vs. Google ads — the fundamental difference
The idea. Meta ads (Facebook and Instagram) and Google ads are the two main paid channels for a small business. They work
in fundamentally different ways, and understanding that difference is the foundation of choosing the right one.
Meta ads — interruption marketing. Meta ads appear in people's Facebook and Instagram feeds, stories and reels — places
where they are browsing, not searching. You are interrupting their scroll with something relevant. Because the person is not
actively looking for your product, your ad must stop the scroll and create interest. Meta ads are best for:
Awareness — reaching people who do not know you exist.
Demand creation — making people want something they were not looking for.
Retargeting — reaching people who visited your page or website but did not buy.
Google ads — intent marketing. Google ads appear when someone searches on Google, watches YouTube, or uses Google
Maps. The person is actively looking for something. You are answering their search with your offer. Because the person is
already looking, your ad must match their search and be the best answer. Google ads are best for:
High-intent searches — people typing "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt" or "phone repair near me".
Local searches — people looking for businesses near them.
Capturing demand — people who already know what they want and are choosing who to buy from.
The key comparison (say this):
"Meta ads are like a market trader calling out to passers-by: 'Come and look at this!' Google ads are like a shop with a sign
that says exactly what people are searching for when they walk down the street. Meta creates demand. Google captures
demand. Both are useful — but they do different jobs."
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Meta ad: An Instagram reel showing sizzling jollof rice with the text "Too tired to cook? We deliver ðŸ ²". Target: people in
Port Harcourt aged 25–45 who follow food pages. Goal: get them to visit her WhatsApp and place an order.
Google ad: A search ad for "jollof rice delivery Port Harcourt" showing "Mama Nkechi's Kitchen — Hot Jollof Delivered in
45 Min. ₦1,500. Order on WhatsApp." Target: people searching that phrase in Port Harcourt. Goal: get them to click and
order immediately.
Which one first? For most small businesses, Google ads come first — because the person is already looking. But if you have no
audience at all and need to build awareness first, Meta ads can create the demand that Google later captures. The answer
depends on your goal, your budget and your foundation.
F.2 Campaign objectives — matching the goal to the funnel
The idea. Before you spend a single naira, you must know what you want the ad to do. This is your campaign objective. The
objective determines who the platform shows your ad to, how it is priced, and how success is measured. Choosing the right
objective is the most important decision in paid advertising.
The six common objectives (simplified):
1. Awareness — get as many people as possible to see your ad. Best for new businesses with no audience. Like putting up a
billboard.
2. Reach — get your ad to as many unique people as possible. Similar to awareness but counts each person once.
3. Traffic — get people to click your ad and visit your website, Google Business Profile or WhatsApp.
4. Engagement — get likes, comments, shares and page follows. Useful for building a warm audience — but be careful:
engagement does not equal sales.
5. Leads — get people to send their contact details (via a form, WhatsApp message or phone call).
6. Sales — get people to place an order or make a purchase. The hardest and most expensive objective — but the one that
matters most.
Matching the objective to the funnel:
Funnel stage Best objective Why
Awareness Awareness or Reach They do not know you exist yet
Consideration Traffic or Engagement They are checking you out — drive them to your profile or website
Conversion Leads or Sales They are ready to buy — make it easy to order
Retargeting Sales or Leads They have visited before — remind them to come back
Say to the class:
"If you choose the wrong objective, you waste money. If your goal is orders but you choose 'engagement', the algorithm will
optimise for likes — and likes do not pay bills. If your goal is awareness but you choose 'sales', the algorithm will struggle
to find buyers — because most people who see your ad are not ready to buy yet. Match the objective to the funnel stage,
and the algorithm will do its job."
Common misconception. "I want likes and followers — that means my ad is working." No. Likes and followers are vanity
metrics — they feel good but do not pay bills. The only metrics that matter are the ones tied to your goal: clicks, leads, sales
and return on ad spend (ROAS). Optimise for customers, not likes.
F.3 Targeting — reaching the right people (not everyone)
The idea. Targeting is telling the platform who should see your ad. The platform has data on billions of people — their
location, age, interests, behaviours and more. Your job is to define a specific audience so your money is not wasted showing
your ad to people who will never buy.
The three targeting levels:
1. Location — where the person is. For a local business, this is the most important: "People in Port Harcourt" or "People
within 10km of GRA". Never target all of Nigeria if you only deliver to Port Harcourt — you will waste money on people in
Lagos and Abuja.
2. Demographics — age, gender, language. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen: "Ages 25–45, English and Pidgin speakers" — the
working professionals and parents who order food delivery.
3. Interests and behaviours — what they like, what they do. For Mama Nkechi: "People who follow food pages, people who
search for food delivery, people who have ordered food online before."
The golden rule of targeting: narrow is better.
"Targeting 'everyone in Port Harcourt' is the most expensive and least effective strategy. You will show your ad to students,
grandparents, people who never order food — and pay for every impression. Instead, narrow it: 'People in GRA and Trans-
Amadi, aged 25–45, who follow food pages and have ordered online before.' That is a smaller audience — but every
person in it is a potential customer. Narrow targeting costs less and converts more."
The avatar connection: Your target audience should match your customer avatar. If your avatar is Chinedu (28, professional in
GRA, orders food delivery), your targeting should be: location = GRA and Trans-Amadi, age = 25–45, interests = food delivery,
dining, convenience. The avatar is the blueprint; targeting is the execution.
Common misconception. "I should target as many people as possible to get the most for my money." No — a small, well-
targeted audience that converts beats a broad audience that ignores you. The algorithm rewards relevance. The more specific
your audience, the cheaper and more effective your ads become.
F.4 Ad structure — campaign, ad set, ad
The idea. Both Meta and Google ads have a three-level structure. Understanding this structure is essential — it keeps your ads
organised, your budget clear and your testing possible.
The three levels:
1. Campaign — the top level. This is where you set your objective (awareness, traffic, sales) and your total budget. One
campaign = one objective. If you want awareness AND sales, you need two campaigns.
2. Ad set — the middle level. This is where you define your audience (location, demographics, interests) and your budget
(daily or lifetime). One campaign can have multiple ad sets — for example, one targeting GRA and one targeting Trans-Amadi,
each with its own budget.
3. Ad — the bottom level. This is the actual creative — the image, video, text and call-to-action. One ad set can have multiple
ads — for example, two different images with different hooks — so you can test which performs better.
The structure in one sentence:
"Campaign = what you want (objective). Ad set = who you want (audience). Ad = what you show them (creative)."
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, ad structure:
Level What Example
Campaign Objective: Sales (orders on WhatsApp) Budget: ₦10,000 for 7 days
Ad set 1 Audience: GRA, 25–45, food delivery interests Budget: ₦5,000 (50% of
campaign)
Ad set 2 Audience: Trans-Amadi, 25–45, food delivery interests Budget: ₦5,000 (50% of
campaign)
Ad 1 (in Ad set 1) Photo of jollof, hook "Too tired to cook?", CTA "Order on WhatsApp"
Ad 2 (in Ad set 1) Reel of jollof being cooked, hook "Hot jollof in 45 minutes", CTA "Order

Why this structure matters: It lets you test. If Ad 1 gets more clicks than Ad 2, you learn that photos work better than reels for
this audience. If Ad set 1 (GRA) gets more orders than Ad set 2 (Trans-Amadi), you learn to shift budget to GRA. Structure
enables learning.
Common misconception. "I should put all my budget into one ad." No — testing multiple ads and audiences is how you learn
what works. A small budget spread across two or three options teaches you more than a large budget spent on one guess.
F.5 Naira budgets — what can you realistically buy?
The idea. Paid ads are rented attention. The more you pay, the more you get — but the relationship is not linear, and there is a
minimum threshold below which ads simply do not work. Here is what realistic budgets can buy for a small Nigerian business.
The minimum viable budget: For Meta and Google ads, a daily budget of at least ₦500–₦1,000 is the practical minimum.
Below this, the algorithm cannot gather enough data to optimise. A weekly budget of ₦3,500–₦7,000 is the minimum for a
meaningful test.

Say to the class:
"These are estimates — actual results depend on your targeting, your creative, your offer and your landing page. But
notice the pattern: even a small budget of ₦10,000 a week can bring real orders if the foundation is solid. And notice what

The maths that matters — Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC):
"CAC = total ad spend ÷ number of customers acquired. If you spend ₦10,000 and get 5 customers, your CAC is ₦2,000
per customer. Now — is that worth it? If each customer spends ₦1,500 per order and orders twice a month, they are worth
₦3,000 a month. If they stay for 6 months, they are worth ₦18,000. A CAC of ₦2,000 to acquire a customer worth
₦18,000 is excellent. But if they only order once and never return, a CAC of ₦2,000 for a ₦1,500 order is a loss. The maths
tells you whether ads work."
Budget rules for beginners:
Start small — ₦5,000 to ₦10,000 for a one-week test.
Never spend more than you can afford to lose.
Do not increase the budget until you have a proven winner (a positive ROAS).
Turn off ads that are losing money — do not throw good money after bad.
Common misconception. "I need ₦100,000 a week to make ads work." No — many small businesses run effective ad
campaigns on ₦10,000–₦20,000 a week. The key is not the size of the budget but the quality of the foundation, the targeting
and the creative. A small budget with a clear offer and a complete Google listing can outperform a large budget with a weak
foundation.
F.6 Ad creative and landing-page fit — the promise and the delivery
The idea. Ad creative is what people see — the image, video, text and call-to-action. Landing-page fit is whether what people
find when they click matches what the ad promised. If the ad says "hot jollof delivered in 45 minutes" and the customer clicks
and finds no order button, no menu and no way to reach you — the ad is wasted.
The creative–fit rule:
"The ad makes a promise. The landing page must deliver that promise — fast. If the ad says '₦1,500 jollof', the customer
should see ₦1,500 jollof on the page they land on. If the ad says 'order on WhatsApp', the customer should see a
WhatsApp button within 3 seconds of landing. If the promise and the delivery do not match, you lose the customer and
waste the money you spent on the click."
What makes good ad creative:
A clear hook — the image or first line stops the scroll.
A specific offer — one thing, one price, one benefit.
A clear CTA — "Order on WhatsApp", "Call now", "Book today".
Brand consistency — same colours, fonts and tone as your other channels.
Honesty — the ad promises what the business delivers.
What makes a good landing page (or Google Business Profile, or WhatsApp chat):
Fast — the customer can see what they need in 3 seconds.
Clear — one obvious next step (order button, call button, WhatsApp).
Matching — the offer in the ad is the offer on the page.
Trustworthy — photos, reviews, consistent NAP.

Common misconception. "A clever ad with a mystery CTA performs better." No — clarity beats cleverness in ads. Tell people

F.7 Honest advertising in ads — the rules that protect you
The idea. Paid ads amplify everything — including dishonesty. A misleading organic post might reach 200 people. A
misleading ad might reach 20,000. The platform's rules, the law and basic ethics all demand that ads be honest.
The five honest advertising rules for ads:
1. No fake discounts. "Was ₦3,000, now ₦1,500" is a lie if it was always ₦1,500. Real discounts only.
2. No invented urgency. "Only 2 plates left!" is a lie if there are 200. Real urgency only — and it must be true.
3. No misleading claims. "Lose 10kg in one week" for a food product is fraud. "Delivered in 45 minutes" must be true.
4. No discriminatory targeting. Do not exclude people based on ethnicity, religion or other protected characteristics. Target
by relevance — location, interests, behaviours — never by prejudice.
5. No buying engagement. Do not buy likes, comments or clicks on your ads. The platform detects this and penalises it —
and it distorts your data so you cannot tell what is working.
Transparent labels: If an ad is paid, it should be labelled as such (the platforms do this automatically with a "Sponsored" label).
If content is gifted or paid for in kind, say so. Never pass an ad off as an independent recommendation.
Say to the class:
"Honest advertising is not just the right thing to do — it is the smart thing to do. A misleading ad might get a few extra
clicks today. But when the customer arrives and finds the truth, they leave, they tell friends, they leave a bad review, and
they never come back. An honest ad might get fewer clicks — but the clicks it gets are from people who actually want
what you offer. Honest ads build businesses. Misleading ads build refund requests."
Common misconception. "Everyone exaggerates in ads — it is expected." No — exaggeration that crosses into falsehood is
fraud. "Our jollof is delicious" is opinion (fine). "Our jollof is the best in Nigeria" is a claim you cannot prove (risky). "Our jollof is
delivered in 45 minutes" is a verifiable promise (fine — if true). Know the difference between opinion, claim and verifiable
promise.
F.8 Summary of the lecture
Meta ads interrupt (awareness, demand creation). Google ads answer (high-intent searches).
Choose the objective that matches the funnel stage: awareness, traffic, leads or sales.
Target narrowly: location, demographics, interests — match your avatar.
Ad structure: campaign (objective) → ad set (audience) → ad (creative).
Budgets: ₦10,000/week can bring real orders if the foundation is solid. Calculate CAC.
Creative–fit: the ad's promise must match the landing page's delivery.
Honest advertising: no fake discounts, no invented urgency, no misleading claims, no discriminatory targeting, no buying
engagement.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chinedu runs a phone accessory shop in Port Harcourt — Chinedu's Gadgets — selling phone cases, chargers,
earpieces and screen protectors. He has a Facebook page with 800 followers and posts occasionally. A competitor two streets
away runs a small Meta ad campaign — a photo of a popular phone case with the text "Protect your phone in style — ₦2,500.
Free delivery in PH." The ad runs every day, gets 50–100 likes and brings 5–10 customers a week. Chinedu wants to compete.
He has ₦20,000 to spend on ads this month. He comes to you and says: "I want to run the same kind of ad. How do I make sure
I do not waste my money?"
Relevant facts.
Chinedu's products are similar in quality and price to his competitor's.
His Facebook page has 800 followers but low engagement — his posts get 3–5 likes.
His Google Business Profile is incomplete — no photos, no description, no reviews.
His WhatsApp ordering is informal — customers message him personally.
His competitor's ad works because it targets phone users in Port Harcourt with a specific, affordable product and a clear
CTA.
Chinedu does not know how Meta ads work — he has never set up a campaign.
Decision point. Chinedu is ready to spend ₦20,000 on ads. What should he do first?
Possible approaches.
1. Set up a Meta ad immediately — copy his competitor's ad and target "everyone in Port Harcourt who likes phones."
— This would waste money. The targeting is too broad, the foundation is broken (no Google listing, no reviews, informal
WhatsApp), and copying a competitor's ad is not a strategy.
2. Spend the ₦20,000 on a professional photographer to take better product photos for his Facebook page. — Better
photos help, but they do not fix the structural problems. The photos would improve his organic content but not his ad
readiness.
3. Fix the foundation first — complete the Google Business Profile, add photos and reviews, set up WhatsApp Business
— then run a targeted Meta ad with a specific product and a clear CTA. — This is the right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Chinedu should:
1. Fix the foundation first (free, one week):
Complete his Google Business Profile: name, category, address, phone, hours, 10+ photos of products, description with
keywords, products with prices.
Ask 5 recent customers for honest Google reviews.
Set up WhatsApp Business: profile, catalogue with products and prices, labels.
2. Then run a targeted Meta ad (₦20,000 over 4 weeks):
Objective: Sales (orders on WhatsApp).
Audience: Port Harcourt, aged 18–45, interests in mobile phones, phone accessories, technology.
Creative: A photo of his most popular phone case with the hook "Protect your phone in style ðŸ›¡ï¸" and the offer
"₦2,500 — free delivery in PH. Order on WhatsApp."
CTA: "Order on WhatsApp" — linking to his WhatsApp Business.
Two ad sets: one targeting phone users, one targeting people interested in fashion/accessories.
3. Measure and adjust: After one week, check the results. If one ad set is performing better, shift the budget. If the click-to-
order rate is low, fix the WhatsApp ordering experience.
Say to the class:
"Chinedu's competitor's ad works not because it is clever — but because the competitor has a complete foundation: a
Google listing customers trust, a WhatsApp ordering system that works, and a specific product at a clear price. If Chinedu
runs the same ad without that foundation, he will spend ₦20,000 and get nothing. Fix the foundation first — then the ad
amplifies what is already there."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (copy the competitor): fast but wasteful — broken foundation, broad targeting, no learning.
Approach 2 (better photos): improves organic content but does not fix the structural gaps.
Approach 3 (foundation first, then targeted ads): one week of free work, then a campaign that actually converts.
Professional connection. This is the single most common paid ads situation: a business sees a competitor's ad working and
wants to copy it. The answer is never "copy the ad" — it is "understand why it works and build the same foundation." The ad is
the visible tip of the iceberg. The foundation — Google listing, WhatsApp ordering, clear offer, honest reviews — is what
makes it work. Build the iceberg first; the tip takes care of itself.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Choosing the wrong objective
Symptoms: Campaign optimises for likes but gets no orders. Likely cause: Chose "engagement" instead of "sales" or "leads".
How to investigate: Ask: "What is your business goal? What did you tell the platform to optimise for?" Corrective steps: Match
the objective to the funnel stage. If the goal is orders, choose "sales". If the goal is WhatsApp messages, choose "leads". How to
confirm resolved: Objective matches the stated business goal. Prevention: The objective-funnel matching table in Section F.2
— use it for every campaign.
K.2 Targeting "everyone"
Symptoms: Ad is shown to thousands of people who never click or buy. Likely cause: Targeting is too broad — "everyone in
Port Harcourt" or "all ages". How to investigate: Check the audience definition. Is it narrower than the total population?
Corrective steps: Narrow by location (specific areas), age (match the avatar) and interests (match the avatar's behaviours).
How to confirm resolved: The audience definition matches the customer avatar. Prevention: The targeting exercise in Section
H — does your audience match your avatar?
K.3 No ad structure (everything in one ad)
Symptoms: One ad with one image and one audience — no testing, no learning. Likely cause: The learner does not
understand the campaign–ad set–ad structure. How to investigate: Ask: "How many audiences are you testing? How many
ad creatives?" Corrective steps: Build the structure: one campaign, two ad sets (different audiences), one ad concept per ad set.
Test and learn. How to confirm resolved: The campaign has at least two ad sets and two ad concepts. Prevention: The ad
structure explanation in Section F.4 and the practical exercise.
K.4 Ad promise does not match landing page
Symptoms: People click the ad but do not order or message. Likely cause: The landing page (Google listing, website,
WhatsApp) does not deliver what the ad promised. How to investigate: Click your own ad. What does the customer find? Does
it match the promise? Corrective steps: Fix the landing page before increasing the budget. Make sure the offer, the CTA and
the experience match. How to confirm resolved: A test click delivers exactly what the ad promised. Prevention: The
creative–fit rule in Section F.6 — check it before every campaign.
K.5 Spending too much too soon
Symptoms: ₦50,000 spent in the first week with no clear results. Likely cause: Started with a large budget before testing with a
small one. How to investigate: Ask: "What was your first budget? Did you test with a smaller amount first?" Corrective steps:
Start with ₦5,000–₦10,000 for a one-week test. Only scale what works. Turn off what does not. How to confirm resolved:
The learner has a small-test-first approach. Prevention: The budget rules in Section F.5 — start small, test, then scale.
K.6 Misleading ad claims
Symptoms: "Best phone repair in Nigeria!" or "Lose 10kg in one week with our tea!" Likely cause: Exaggeration to get clicks.
How to investigate: Ask: "Can you prove that claim? Is it true?" Corrective steps: Replace every unprovable claim with a
specific, verifiable one. "Best" → "4.3 stars on Google". "Lose 10kg" → remove. How to confirm resolved: Every claim in the ad
is verifiable. Prevention: The honest advertising rules in Section F.7.
K.7 Buying engagement on ads
Symptoms: "Let me buy 500 likes on my ad so it looks popular." Likely cause: Thinking bought engagement helps the
campaign. How to investigate: Ask: "Where will you buy these likes? Are they from real people?" Corrective steps: Never buy
engagement. It distorts your data, wastes money and violates platform rules. How to confirm resolved: Learner can explain
why bought engagement is harmful. Prevention: The ethics rule — no buying engagement, ever.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact objective chosen, or the exact landing page that does not match). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What is the main difference between Meta ads and Google ads?
Q2. Multiple choice. Which campaign objective should you choose if your goal is to get WhatsApp orders? a) Awareness b)

Q3. Explain. What are the three levels of ad structure, and what belongs at each level?
Q4. Practical. A food kitchen has a Facebook ad that says "Order hot jollof on WhatsApp — ₦1,500" but when customers click,

they fix?
Q5. Budget maths. A business spends ₦10,000 on ads and gets 4 customers. Each customer spends ₦2,000. What is the
Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) and the Return on Ad Spend (ROAS)? Is this good or bad?
Q6. Ethics. Your friend wants to write "Best jollof in all of Nigeria — guaranteed!" in his ad. What do you tell him, and why?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. Meta ads interrupt — they appear in people's feeds when they are browsing, not searching. They create demand and build
awareness. Google ads answer — they appear when someone is actively searching. They capture existing demand. Meta is best
for awareness and demand creation; Google is best for high-intent local searches.
A2. (c) Sales. The objective must match the goal. If the goal is WhatsApp orders, the objective is sales (or leads). Awareness,
engagement and reach do not optimise for orders.
A3. Campaign (top level) — the objective and total budget. Ad set (middle level) — the audience and budget. Ad (bottom
level) — the creative: image, video, text and CTA.
A4. The problem is a creative–fit failure. The ad promises a specific experience (WhatsApp ordering at ₦1,500) but the landing
experience (personal WhatsApp, no catalogue, no prices) does not deliver it. They should fix the WhatsApp ordering experience:
set up WhatsApp Business with a catalogue, clear prices and an easy "Reply YES to order" process — before spending more on
ads.
A5. CAC = ₦10,000 ÷ 4 = ₦2,500 per customer. Revenue = 4 × ₦2,000 = ₦8,000. ROAS = ₦8,000 ÷ ₦10,000 = 0.8x. This is
bad — they spent ₦10,000 to get ₦8,000 in revenue. They are losing money. They need to either reduce the cost per customer,
increase the conversion rate, increase the average order value, or improve repeat purchases.
A6. I would tell him not to write that. "Best in all of Nigeria" is a claim he cannot prove — it is not verifiable, and it could
mislead customers. Instead, he should write something specific and verifiable: "4.3 stars on Google — hot jollof delivered to
your door in 45 minutes." That is honest, credible and more trustworthy than an unprovable superlative. (Accept any answer
that identifies the claim as unprovable and suggests a verifiable alternative.)

"Copying a competitor's ad is a strategy." → No — understand why it works and build your own foundation.
"Exaggerated claims get more clicks." → No — misleading claims are fraud and destroy trust.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Objective matching drill: Give five business goals and ask the learner to choose the correct objective for each.
2. Targeting narrowing drill: Give a broad audience ("everyone in Nigeria") and ask the learner to narrow it to match a specific
avatar.
3. Budget maths drill: Give three budget scenarios (₦5,000, ₦10,000, ₦20,000) and ask the learner to calculate estimated
orders and ROAS for each.
4. Creative–fit drill: Give three ad–landing page pairs (one good fit, two bad fits) and ask the learner to identify which is
which and explain why.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the campaign structure with the objective and audience already filled in — the learner plans
the ad creative and calculates the budget maths.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full campaign structure, audience, creative, budget maths and honesty check.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to plan a two-week campaign with a budget of ₦30,000, including a week-one test
plan and a week-one adjustment plan based on hypothetical results (e.g., "Ad set 1 got 10 clicks and 2 orders; Ad set 2 got
50 clicks and 1 order — what do you do in week two?"). This requires applying the test-learn-adjust cycle.
