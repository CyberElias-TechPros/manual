# Capstone: Integrated Digital Marketing Campaign Plan

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Convert and Measure

**Difficulty:** Beginner (assembly, presentation and defence — not new technical skills)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–8. Learners should have completed all mini-projects: avatar, value proposition, Google Business Profile draft, content calendar, keyword worksheet, newsletter and broadcast drafts, campaign structure sheet, and measurement plan (KPIs, UTM links, A/B test, one-page report).

**Connection to previous work:** In Sessions 1–8 we built every piece of the puzzle: the audience, the positioning, the online presence, the content plan, the SEO and local search actions, the email and WhatsApp plan, the paid ad structure, and the measurement plan. Today we assemble everything into one complete, integrated campaign plan — the capstone. Each learner checks that every piece fits, writes it up, and presents and defends their decisions. This is the session that earns the certificate.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers or phones ready. Prepare projector slides showing the capstone structure (the eight components). Print the Capstone Checklist (Section H) per learner. Prepare a sample completed capstone (for demonstration only — do not distribute). Prepare presentation time slots (4–5 minutes per learner). Prepare peer feedback forms. Have the capstone assessment rubric ready (from Part 6). Prepare a course feedback form for the end of the session. Instructor note on tone: This is the final session — the one everything has been building toward. Learners may feel proud of what they have built, and they may also feel nervous about presenting. Your job is to make this feel like a celebration, not an exam. The capstone is not about perfection — it is about completeness, coherence and honesty. A plan that is simple, clear and genuinely affordable is better than a plan that is impressive but unrealistic. Remind them: they have been building this for eight sessions. Today they put it together and show it off.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a complete integrated digital marketing campaign plan covering all eight required components: cover page,
contents and ethics and scope statement, goals and KPIs, audience and positioning, online presence, channel and content
plan, SEO and owned channels, paid media and measurement, and timeline, budget and ethics statement.
2. Check internal consistency — ensuring that the audience matches the channels, the channels match the content, the
content matches the budget, and the measurement plan matches the goals.
3. Present the plan in a short talk (4–5 minutes), explaining at least three key decisions aloud in plain language.
4. Defend decisions under questioning — answering at least three questions accurately, explaining the reasoning behind
channel choices, budget choices and measurement choices.
5. Give and receive constructive peer feedback using a structured feedback form.
6. Reflect on the course as a whole — identifying what they have learned, what they can do now that they could not do
before, and what they want to learn next.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two documents side by side.
Document 1 — "The Random Folder": A folder with eight separate files: an avatar sheet, a content calendar, a keyword list, a
newsletter draft, a campaign structure sheet, a UTM link worksheet, a one-page report, and a Google Business Profile
screenshot. All good work — but scattered, unconnected, with no overall plan.
Document 2 — "The Integrated Plan": One document with a cover page, a table of contents, and all eight components
assembled into a single, coherent plan. The avatar connects to the channels. The channels connect to the content. The content
connects to the budget. The budget connects to the measurement. Everything fits.
What the instructor says:
"Eight sessions of work. Eight separate pieces. On the left, they are scattered — good pieces, but no picture. On the right,
they are assembled — one plan, one story, one strategy. Today you turn your folder into the document on the right. You
take everything you have built — your avatar, your content calendar, your keywords, your newsletter, your ad structure,
your measurement plan — and you put them together into one complete campaign plan. Not eight separate
assignments. One integrated plan. That is what a real marketer delivers. That is what you deliver today."
Why this opener works: It shows the transformation from scattered work to integrated plan. It makes the goal concrete and
visual. And it reassures learners that they already have all the pieces — today is about assembly, not starting from scratch.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "In Session 2, we built a customer avatar. What is the avatar's name for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, and what is one of
his pains?"
Expected answer: Chinedu; too tired to cook after work (or worried about food hygiene).
Common gap: generic answers. Prompt for specifics.
Question 2: "In Session 4, we learned about content pillars. What are the three content pillars for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen?"
Expected answer: Food (menu items, recipes, cooking tips), Behind-the-scenes (kitchen, preparation, team), and Customer
love (reviews, testimonials, celebrations).
Common gap: learners list random post ideas instead of pillars. Remind them: pillars are categories, not individual posts.
Question 3: "In Session 7, we learned about campaign objectives. If your goal is to get WhatsApp orders, what objective do you
choose?"
Expected answer: Sales (or leads).
Common gap: learners choose "engagement." Remind them: the objective must match the goal.
Question 4: "In Session 8, we learned about UTM links. What does utm_source tell you?"
Expected answer: Where the click came from (e.g., instagram, facebook, whatsapp).
Common gap: learners confuse the parameters. Quickly review: source, medium, campaign, content, term.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have every piece. The avatar, the content, the keywords, the email plan, the ad structure, the measurement plan.
Today you put them together. But assembly is not just copy-paste. You have to check that everything fits. Does your
audience match your channels? Do your channels match your content? Does your content match your budget? Does your
measurement plan match your goals? If one piece does not fit, the whole plan is weak. Today you check, you fix, you
assemble, and you present. That is the capstone."

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What the capstone is and why it matters
The idea. The capstone is the complete, integrated digital marketing campaign plan for a small business. It is not eight separate
assignments stapled together — it is one document that tells one story: who we are talking to, where we reach them, what we
say, how we pay for it, and how we know it worked.
Why it matters. In the real world, a business owner does not hire you to write a content calendar. They hire you to grow their
business. The capstone proves you can think like a marketer — not just a poster, not just a copywriter, not just an ad buyer, but
someone who sees the whole picture and can explain how the pieces work together.
What the capstone proves:
You can define a customer and position a business.
You can choose the right channels for a specific audience and goal.
You can plan content that serves a strategy, not just fills a calendar.
You can set a realistic budget and justify it.
You can measure results and make decisions based on data.
You can present and defend your thinking in plain language.
Say to the class:
"The capstone is not a test. It is a demonstration. It shows what you can do — not what you can memorise. A business
owner should be able to read your plan and start work on Monday morning. That is the standard. Not perfect. Not agency-
level. Just clear, complete, honest and usable."
F.2 The eight components of the capstone
The idea. The capstone has eight required components. Each one was built in a previous session. Today you assemble them and
check that they fit together.
The eight components:
1. Cover page, contents and ethics and scope statement. The learner's name, the course, the date, the business name, and a
one-paragraph description of the plan; a table of contents; and — opening the plan — the ethics and scope statement: the
business is fictional, this is a course exercise, what was in and out of scope, and a commitment to honest marketing. The ethics
and scope statement is a pass condition (no statement = no marking) and sits inside component 1.
2. Goals and KPIs. Two or three SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) with the exact metric
and timeframe. For example: "Increase WhatsApp orders from 10 to 20 per week within 4 weeks." The KPI is orders per week.
3. Audience and positioning. The customer avatar (who, where, pains, objections, platforms), the value proposition (what you
give, to whom, why you are different), and three messaging lines aimed at named pains.
4. Online presence. The Google Business Profile (or equivalent), the landing-page structure, brand consistency (name, logo,
colours, fonts, tone), and trust signals (reviews, photos, consistent NAP).
5. Channel and content plan. The chosen channels with reasoning (why these channels for this audience?), a one-week content
calendar (format, hook, CTA per item), and a repurposing note (how one idea becomes several posts).
6. SEO and owned channels. Ten or more keywords with volume/competition notes, a local SEO checklist (categories,
keywords, posts, photos, reviews, NAP), and a permission-based email/WhatsApp plan with opt-out.
7. Paid media and measurement. A campaign structure with a naira budget, KPIs, two UTM link examples, a report format, and
a one-variable A/B test.
8. Timeline, budget table and ethics statement. Four weeks of activity and costs (including the ₦0 organic option), plus an
ethics statement covering honest advertising, real reviews only, no bought engagement, permission-based messaging and
respectful data handling.
Say to the class:
"Eight components. You have built every one of them. Today you put them in order, check that they connect, and make
them into one document. The cover page is the door. The goals are the destination. The audience is the compass. The
channels are the vehicle. The content is the fuel. The budget is the map. The measurement is the mirror. And the ethics
statement is the foundation. Everything else sits on top of it."
F.3 Checking internal consistency — does every piece fit?
The idea. A plan can have all eight components and still be weak if the pieces do not fit together. Internal consistency means:
the audience matches the channels, the channels match the content, the content matches the budget, and the measurement
matches the goals.
The five consistency checks:
Check 1: Does the audience match the channels? If your avatar is Chinedu (28, professional in GRA, orders food delivery on his
phone), your channels should be the ones he uses: Instagram, Google Search, WhatsApp. If your plan targets Chinedu but
focuses on LinkedIn and Twitter, the pieces do not fit.
Check 2: Do the channels match the content? If your channels are Instagram and WhatsApp, your content should be visual
(photos, reels) and conversational (broadcasts, personal messages). If your channels are Instagram and WhatsApp but your
content is long blog posts and email newsletters, the pieces do not fit.
Check 3: Does the content match the budget? If your budget is ₦10,000 per week, your content plan should be realistic for
that budget — a few posts per week, one or two ads, one broadcast. If your content plan requires daily video production, a
professional photographer and a ₦50,000 budget, the pieces do not fit.
Check 4: Does the measurement match the goals? If your goal is orders, your KPIs should be orders, conversion rate and
revenue. If your goal is awareness, your KPIs should be reach and impressions. If your goal is orders but your KPIs are followers
and likes, the pieces do not fit.
Check 5: Does the timeline match the plan? If your plan says "post three times per week" but your timeline shows only two
posts in week one, the pieces do not fit. If your plan says "run ads in week two" but your timeline shows ads starting in week
one, the pieces do not fit.
Say to the class:
"A plan is like a chain. It is only as strong as its weakest link. If your audience is right but your channels are wrong, the
plan fails. If your channels are right but your content is wrong, the plan fails. If your content is right but your budget is
wrong, the plan fails. Today you check every link. You make sure every piece fits. That is what turns eight assignments into
one plan."

F.4 Assembling the document — structure and presentation
The idea. The capstone is assembled as one document. It should be clear, professional and easy to navigate. The reader should
be able to find any component in seconds.
The recommended document structure:
1. Cover page — name, course, date, business, one-paragraph summary.
2. Ethics and scope statement — fictional business, course exercise, in/out of scope, honest marketing commitment.
(Opens the plan. Part of component 1 — a pass condition: no statement, no marking.)
3. Table of contents — the eight components with page numbers.
4. Section 1: Goals and KPIs — two or three SMART goals with metrics and timeframes.
5. Section 2: Audience and positioning — avatar, value proposition, three messages.
6. Section 3: Online presence — Google Business Profile, landing page, brand, trust signals.
7. Section 4: Channel and content plan — channels with reasoning, one-week calendar, repurposing note.
8. Section 5: SEO and owned channels — keywords, local SEO checklist, email/WhatsApp plan.
9. Section 6: Paid media and measurement — campaign structure, budget, KPIs, UTM links, A/B test.
10. Section 7: Timeline and budget table — four weeks of activity and costs.
11. Section 8: Ethics statement — honest advertising, real reviews, no bought engagement, permission-based messaging,
data respect.
Presentation tips:
Use headings and subheadings — make it scannable.
Use tables for the calendar, budget and KPI tracking.
Use bullet points for lists — not paragraphs.
Include visuals where helpful — a screenshot of the Google Business Profile, a sample post, a sample ad.
Keep it honest — if something is a plan (not yet done), say so. "We will set up…" not "We have set up…"
Say to the class:
"Your capstone is a professional document. It should look like something you would show a client. Clear headings, clean
layout, no spelling errors. You do not need fancy design — you need clarity. A one-page summary at the top that says
what the plan is, who it is for, and what it will achieve. Then the detail underneath. Make it easy to read."
F.5 Presenting and defending — the viva
The idea. After assembling the plan, each learner presents it in a short talk (4–5 minutes) and answers questions. This is the
viva — the spoken defence of the plan. It proves the learner understands their own decisions and can explain them in plain
language.
The presentation structure (4–5 minutes):
1. Introduction (30 seconds): "My plan is for [business name]. The goal is [goal]. Here is what I will cover."
2. Audience and positioning (30 seconds): "My customer is [avatar name]. He/She cares about [pain]. My value proposition is
- one sentence]."
3. Channels and content (1 minute): "I chose [channels] because [reasoning]. My content plan includes [highlights]."
4. Budget and measurement (1 minute): "My budget is [amount] per week. I will measure [KPIs]. I will run [A/B test] to learn
- variable]."
5. Key decisions (1 minute): "The three most important decisions I made are: [decision 1] because [reason], [decision 2]
because [reason], [decision 3] because [reason]."
6. Conclusion (30 seconds): "This plan is realistic, affordable and measurable. It is ready to implement."
Anticipating questions: The viva will include questions like:
"Why these channels and not others?"
"What if week one fails?"
"How do you know your budget is enough?"
"What is the first thing you would change if results are poor?"
"How will you know if the plan is working?"
How to prepare for questions:
Know your reasoning for every decision. Why Instagram and not TikTok? Why ₦10,000 and not ₦50,000? Why orders and
not followers?
Be honest. If you do not know something, say "I do not know, but I would find out by…" — that is a better answer than
making something up.
Stay calm. The questions are not attacks — they are curiosity. The panel wants to understand your thinking.
Say to the class:
"The viva is not an interrogation. It is a conversation. You have done the work — now you explain it. The best answer to
any question is the honest one. 'I chose Instagram because my customer is there' is a perfect answer. 'I chose Instagram
because I saw a competitor using it' is a weak answer. Know your reasoning. Speak plainly. You know this plan better than
anyone in the room."
F.6 Giving and receiving peer feedback
The idea. After each presentation, peers give structured feedback. This teaches learners to evaluate marketing plans critically
and to receive feedback gracefully.
The peer feedback form (three sections):
1. What worked well? (At least two specific things.)
Example: "The channel choice was well reasoned — you explained why Instagram fits the customer."
Example: "The budget was realistic and clearly justified."
2. What could be improved? (At least two specific, constructive suggestions.)
Example: "The content calendar is good, but it does not show what happens on days with no posts."
Example: "The measurement plan tracks orders, but it does not say how you will track repeat customers."
3. Questions for the presenter. (At least one question.)
Example: "What will you do if the ad gets clicks but no orders?"
Example: "How will you handle negative reviews on Google?"
Rules for giving feedback:
Be specific. "The budget is good" is weak. "The budget is realistic because you showed what ₦10,000 buys" is strong.
Be kind. Feedback is about the work, not the person.
Be honest. If something does not fit, say so — but say it respectfully.
Rules for receiving feedback:
Listen without defending.
Ask questions if you do not understand.
Thank the person — even if the feedback is hard to hear.
Decide what to act on. Not all feedback is right, but all feedback is useful.
Say to the class:
"Feedback is a gift. It shows you what you cannot see yourself. The best marketers are not the ones who never make
mistakes — they are the ones who learn fastest from feedback. Today you practise both giving and receiving it. Be honest,
be kind, and be useful."
F.7 The ethics statement — the foundation of the plan
The idea. Every capstone includes an ethics statement — a short section that says how the plan will be executed honestly and
responsibly. This is not optional. It is the foundation that everything else sits on.
The ethics statement must cover:
1. Honest advertising. All claims will be true and verifiable. No fake discounts, no invented urgency, no misleading claims.
2. Real reviews only. Reviews will be asked for honestly from real customers. No fake reviews, no bought reviews, no reviews
from people who did not use the service.
3. No bought engagement. No buying followers, likes, views, comments or shares. All growth will be earned.
4. Permission-based messaging. Email and WhatsApp messages will only go to people who said yes. Easy opt-out will be
provided and honoured immediately.
5. Respectful data handling. Customer data will be collected only with consent, used only for the agreed purpose, kept secure
and never sold.
Say to the class:
"The ethics statement is not a formality. It is a promise. It says: I will grow this business honestly. I will not cheat, I will not
spam, I will not lie. That promise is what makes everything else in the plan trustworthy. A plan that is clever but dishonest
is worthless. A plan that is simple but honest is priceless."
F.8 Summary of the lecture
The capstone is the complete, integrated campaign plan — eight components assembled into one document.
The eight components: cover page, contents and ethics and scope statement, goals and KPIs, audience and positioning,
online presence, channel and content plan, SEO and owned channels, paid media and measurement, timeline, budget and
ethics.
Internal consistency means every piece fits: audience matches channels, channels match content, content matches
budget, measurement matches goals.
The viva is a 4–5 minute presentation where the learner explains at least three key decisions and answers questions.
Peer feedback teaches learners to evaluate plans critically and receive feedback gracefully.
The ethics statement is the foundation — honest advertising, real reviews, no bought engagement, permission-based

## J. Real-World Case Study

Situation. Emeka is a recent CEA Digital Marketing graduate. He completed the course three months ago and has been offering
social media management services to small businesses in Port Harcourt. A local restaurant, Taste of Naija, hires him to grow
their online presence. The restaurant has been open for one year, has a Facebook page with 300 followers, no Google Business
Profile, no website and no mailing list. The owner, Chef Obi, says: "I want more customers. Can you help me?"
Emeka remembers his capstone. He knows he needs to build a complete plan — not just post randomly. He sits down and
assembles a campaign plan for Taste of Naija, using everything he learned in the course.
Relevant facts.
Taste of Naija serves Nigerian dishes — jollof, egusi, suya, pounded yam — for lunch and dinner.
They are located in Trans-Amadi, near several offices and a market.
Their customers are mostly office workers (lunch) and families (dinner).
They have no online presence beyond a neglected Facebook page.
Chef Obi can afford ₦15,000 per month for marketing.
He wants more customers within 3 months.
Decision point. Emeka needs to present a plan to Chef Obi. What should the plan include, and how should he present it?
Possible approaches.
1. "I will post on Facebook every day and run a small ad." — This is random activity without strategy. No audience
definition, no content plan, no measurement. It might work by luck, but it is not a plan.
2. "I will build a complete plan: audience, channels, content, budget and measurement. I will present it to you and
explain every decision." — This is the right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 2. Emeka should:
1. Build the audience: Office workers in Trans-Amadi (lunch) and families in Trans-Amadi and Rumuokwuta (dinner). Avatar:
Kelechi, 30, office worker, wants a quick, affordable, tasty lunch. Pain: tired of the same food every day.
2. Set the channels: Instagram (visual food content), Google Search (local SEO — "lunch Trans-Amadi"), WhatsApp
(ordering and delivery).
3. Plan the content: Three posts per week — a food photo, a behind-the-scenes kitchen video, a customer review. Plus one
WhatsApp broadcast to past customers.
4. Set the budget: ₦15,000 per month = ₦3,750 per week. Start with a small Meta ad test (₦3,750 for one week) to learn
what works.
5. Define the measurement: Orders per week, conversion rate, ROAS. Set up UTM links for the ad.
6. Present the plan: Emeka presents the plan to Chef Obi in a 5-minute talk, explaining: "I chose Instagram and Google
because that is where your customers are. I set the budget at ₦15,000 because that is what you can afford. I will measure
orders, not followers, because orders pay bills. Here is the plan. Here is why it will work. Here is how we will know if it is
working."
Say to the class:
"Emeka's story is your story in three months. You will sit across from a business owner who wants more customers. They do
not want a content calendar — they want a plan. They do not want jargon — they want clarity. They do not want
promises — they want honesty. The capstone is your proof that you can deliver. It is the first real item in your portfolio.
When a client asks 'can you help me?', you say 'yes — and here is how.'"
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (random activity): fast to start, but no strategy, no measurement, no learning. Might work by luck, but probably
will not.
Approach 2 (complete plan): takes time to build, but every decision is reasoned, every naira is accounted for, and the client
trusts you because you can explain your thinking.
Professional connection. This is the difference between a hobbyist and a professional. A hobbyist posts and hopes. A
professional plans, executes, measures and adjusts. The capstone is your first professional deliverable. It is what you show
clients, what you put in your portfolio, and what earns you referrals.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Assembling without checking consistency
Symptoms: All eight components are present, but the audience does not match the channels, or the content does not match
the budget. Likely cause: The learner pasted everything together without checking how the pieces fit. How to investigate: Run
the five consistency checks. Which one fails? Corrective steps: Fix the mismatched piece. Change the channel, the content, the
budget or the KPI until everything fits. How to confirm resolved: All five consistency checks pass. Prevention: The consistency
check exercise in Section H — run it before presenting.
K.2 Presenting without explaining reasoning
Symptoms: The learner says "I chose Instagram" but cannot say why. Likely cause: The learner made decisions by habit or
guesswork, not by reasoning. How to investigate: Ask "why?" after every decision. If the learner cannot answer, the decision is
not reasoned. Corrective steps: For every decision, write one sentence: "I chose X because Y." If you cannot write the sentence,
reconsider the decision. How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain the reasoning for every key decision. Prevention:
The Presentation Planner in Section H — three key decisions with reasoning.
K.3 Making promises the plan cannot keep
Symptoms: "This plan will get you 1,000 followers in one month" or "This ad will double your sales." Likely cause: Exaggeration
to impress, or misunderstanding what a small budget can achieve. How to investigate: Ask: "What evidence do you have for
that claim? Is it realistic for a ₦10,000 budget?" Corrective steps: Replace every promise with a realistic expectation. "This plan
aims to increase orders by 50% within 4 weeks" — not "This plan will double your sales." How to confirm resolved: Every
claim in the plan is realistic and honest. Prevention: The honest measurement rules from Session 8 — no promising what you
cannot prove.
K.4 Ignoring the ethics statement
Symptoms: The ethics statement is missing, or it is one sentence: "I will be honest." Likely cause: The learner thinks ethics is a
formality, not a foundation. How to investigate: Ask: "What specifically will you do to ensure honest advertising? How will you
handle reviews? How will you handle customer data?" Corrective steps: Write a full ethics statement covering all five points:
honest advertising, real reviews, no bought engagement, permission-based messaging, respectful data handling. How to
confirm resolved: The ethics statement is specific and complete. Prevention: The ethics statement explanation in Section F.7.
K.5 Reading from the document during the presentation
Symptoms: The learner reads the capstone word for word, never looks up, and runs out of time. Likely cause: The learner is
nervous and uses the document as a security blanket. How to investigate: Ask the learner to explain one decision without
looking at the document. If they cannot, they do not know their own plan. Corrective steps: Practice speaking from notes, not
from the document. Use bullet points, not paragraphs. Know your three key decisions by heart. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can present the three key decisions without reading. Prevention: The presentation practice in Section H — stand up,
speak, time it.
K.6 Being defensive when receiving feedback
Symptoms: The learner argues with every piece of feedback, or dismisses it entirely. Likely cause: Taking feedback personally,
or not understanding its purpose. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you learn from that feedback? What will you change?"
Corrective steps: Remind the learner: feedback is about the work, not the person. Listen, ask questions, thank the person, and
decide what to act on. How to confirm resolved: The learner receives feedback gracefully and acts on at least one piece.
Prevention: The peer feedback rules in Section F.6 — be specific, be kind, be honest.
K.7 Rushing the assembly and submitting a messy document
Symptoms: The document has no headings, no table of contents, spelling errors, and components are out of order. Likely
cause: Leaving the assembly to the last minute. How to investigate: Look at the document. Can you find any component in 5
seconds? If not, it is not organised. Corrective steps: Add a table of contents. Use headings. Use tables for the calendar and
budget. Proofread for spelling. How to confirm resolved: The document is clean, organised and professional. Prevention: The
assembly demonstration in Section G.2 — show the structure, then give time to assemble.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact consistency check that failed, or the exact decision they cannot explain). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

Plain-English summary. Today you assembled the capstone — the complete, integrated digital marketing campaign plan. You
learned that the capstone has eight components: cover page, contents and ethics and scope statement, goals and KPIs,
audience and positioning, online presence, channel and content plan, SEO and owned channels, paid media and measurement,
and timeline, budget and ethics. You learned to check internal consistency — making sure the audience matches the
channels, the channels match the content, the content matches the budget, and the measurement matches the goals. You
learned to present and defend your plan in a 4–5 minute talk, explaining at least three key decisions and answering
questions. You learned to give and receive peer feedback — specifically, kindly and honestly. And you learned that the
ethics statement is the foundation: honest advertising, real reviews, no bought engagement, permission-based messaging and
respectful data handling.
Five important takeaways:
1. The capstone is one plan, not eight assignments. Every piece must fit — audience, channels, content, budget,
measurement.
2. Internal consistency is quality control. Run the five checks. Fix what does not fit.
3. The viva is a conversation, not an interrogation. Know your reasoning. Speak plainly. Be honest.
4. Feedback is a gift. Give it specifically and kindly. Receive it gracefully and act on it.
5. Ethics is the foundation. A plan that is clever but dishonest is worthless. A plan that is simple but honest is priceless.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner assembles all eight components into one coherent document.
Learner runs the five consistency checks and fixes any mismatches.
Learner presents three key decisions with clear reasoning ("I chose X because Y").
Learner answers questions honestly — including "I do not know, but I would find out by…"
Learner gives feedback that is specific, kind and useful.
Learner's ethics statement covers all five points specifically.
Learner's document is clean, organised and professional.
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"The capstone is just pasting everything together." → No — it is checking that every piece fits and fixing what does not.
"I should promise big results to impress." → No — honest expectations build trust; false promises destroy it.
"The ethics statement is a formality." → No — it is the foundation. Without it, the plan is just tactics.
"Feedback is personal criticism." → No — it is about the work, and it is how you improve.
"Reading from the document is fine." → No — know your plan well enough to speak plainly.
"More channels = better plan." → No — the right channels for the audience beat every channel for everyone.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Consistency check drill: Give a plan with three intentional mismatches and ask the learner to find and fix them.
2. Decision reasoning drill: Give five channel choices and ask the learner to write "I chose X because Y" for each.
3. Ethics statement drill: Give a vague ethics statement ("I will be honest") and ask the learner to rewrite it covering all five
points specifically.
4. Presentation practice: Have the learner present to you alone, then give one piece of feedback and have them present
again.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the capstone structure with headings already created — the learner fills in each section.
Provide the consistency check table with the questions already written — the learner writes the answers.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full assembly, consistency check, presentation and peer feedback.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to assemble the capstone for a different business than their own (e.g., a salon instead
of a kitchen), adapting every component to the new context. This tests whether they understand the principles or have just
memorised one plan.
Timing guidance. The priority is the guided practical (Section H) — learners assembling and checking their capstone. If the
room is slow, shorten the lecture (combine F.3 and F.4) and reduce the case study (Section J) to a 5-minute discussion. If
learners struggle with the consistency check, walk through one example on the board step by step. Protect the assembly time
— a learner who leaves without a complete capstone has no certificate deliverable.
Capstone link. This session is the capstone. The document assembled today is the final deliverable. The presentation is the viva.
Together they are 35% of the course grade (30% capstone + 5% viva). The learner's working folder — all worksheets from
Sessions 1–8 — should be complete and organised. The capstone is the proof that the learner can think like a marketer: plan,
execute, measure, adjust, and explain. That is the skill. That is the certificate. That is the beginning.
