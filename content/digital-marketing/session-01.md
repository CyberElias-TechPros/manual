# Digital Marketing Foundations

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Foundations

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes Course fee ₦35,000 (paid once at enrolment — all sessions, materials and the certificate included)

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with digital marketing)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Basic smartphone use — scrolling, typing, opening apps, taking a photo, using WhatsApp or social media. No prior marketing, design, website or analytics knowledge required. Learners without a smartphone may pair with a classmate and use academy computers, but must tell the instructor at the start of the session.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. There is no previous digital marketing session. It connects to everyday life: every learner has a phone, uses social media, searches Google, and has seen online shops and adverts. Many learners already post about a business or side hustle without any plan — today we give that activity a structure.

**Preparation before class:** Print the eight channel cards (Section F.2) and the funnel-stage sorting cards (Section F.3) — one set per group of 3–4 learners. Prepare a projector slide showing the running example business, Mama Nkechi's Kitchen (fictional Port Harcourt food business). Print the Funnel and Journey Mapping Worksheet (Section H) per learner. Have a whiteboard or flip chart ready with four columns drawn: Awareness, Consideration, Conversion, Retention. Prepare a short 2 minute verbal introduction of Mama Nkechi's Kitchen (name, location, what she sells, current situation). Confirm the class understands the academy's Ethics and Professional Conduct rules (Section 15 of the Design Document) — read them aloud at the start. Instructor note on tone: This session must feel empowering, not overwhelming. Many learners have been told digital marketing is "complicated" or "expensive." Your job in the first 105 minutes is to prove the opposite: that they already live inside digital marketing, that the ideas are simple, and that a clear plan is worth more than any expensive tool. Keep the mood practical, friendly and confident.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define digital marketing in their own words and explain why it matters for a small Nigerian business, using at least one
everyday example from their own phone use.
2. Name and describe the eight main digital marketing channels (website, search, social media, content, email, WhatsApp,
paid ads, Google Business Profile) and match each channel to the job it does for a business.
3. Explain the four marketing funnel stages — Awareness, Consideration, Conversion and Retention — in their own words,
and correctly classify at least 4 of 5 given marketing tactics to the correct funnel stage.
4. Map a simple customer journey for a named customer of a real or fictional business, describing what the customer sees,
thinks and does at each stage from first hearing about the business to becoming a repeat customer.
5. Explain why channels work together rather than alone, using the running example of Mama Nkechi's Kitchen to show
how one customer might move across three or four channels before buying.
6. Apply the academy's ethics rules by stating, in their own words, what honest, permission-based marketing means and why
buying followers or posting fake reviews is wrong.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show a slide with a simple question and a photo of a busy Port Harcourt street market or a
phone showing Instagram. Do not explain anything yet.
The question on the screen:
"Hands up — who has used their phone today to look for something to buy, eat, watch or visit?"
Pause. Nearly every hand goes up. Nod and acknowledge.
Then ask:
"Keep your hand up if you found what you were looking for on your phone — maybe on Google, Instagram, WhatsApp, or
a friend's page."
Most hands stay up. A few drop. Say:
"The hands that dropped — you are the customers digital marketers dream about. Someone did not reach you today.
Today, we learn how to make sure businesses like yours are the ones that get found."
Then introduce the running example. On the projector, show a simple slide:
Mama Nkechi's Kitchen A fictional small food business in Port Harcourt, Rivers State. Sells home-cooked meals, soups
and small chops for delivery and pickup. Has been running for two years. Has walk-in and WhatsApp customers. Has an
Instagram page with a few hundred followers — posts once in a while, no plan. Wants more orders but does not know
where to start.
Say:
"For the next three weeks, this is our client. Every session, we will add one piece to her marketing plan. By Session 9, you will
hand her a complete campaign she can actually use. You can also use your own real or fictional business — but Mama
Nkechi's Kitchen is the example I will use."
Expected learner response: Learners will nod, smile, or say things like "my auntie has a kitchen like that" or "that is my side
hustle." Accept all answers warmly. The goal is recognition: this is someone like me, and this problem is real.
Why this opener works: It starts from the learner's own experience (everyone uses their phone to find things), creates instant
personal connection, and frames digital marketing not as a technical subject but as a bridge between a business and the people
already looking for it — which is exactly what it is.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. Keep it moving — no question should take more than 90
seconds.
Question 1: "What apps do you use on your phone every day? Name three."
Expected answer: WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Google, YouTube, Chrome, Gmail, maps, banking apps — any
three.
Common gap: some learners name only one or two. Prompt gently: "What do you open first in the morning?"
Question 2: "Have you ever searched for something on Google — a food, a place, a price? What did you type?"
Expected answer: anything realistic — "jollof rice near me", "barber shop Port Harcourt", "price of Ankara fabric".
Common gap: some learners have only ever browsed social media feeds and never typed a search. Acknowledge this: "That
is fine — some of you are browsers, some are searchers. Both are customers, and both matter today."
Question 3: "When you see a post or advert on Instagram or Facebook, what makes you stop and look — and what makes you
scroll past?"
Expected answer: "a nice picture", "something cheap", "a video", "a friend shared it", "it looked fake", "too long", "too many
words".
Common gap: learners give vague answers. Draw out the two sides: what stops (visual, relevance, curiosity) and what sends
them away (boring, fake, too long, not for them). Write both lists on the board.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already know more than you think. You know what stops you and what sends you away. You know the apps people
use. You know what you type when you want something. Today we take that everyday knowledge and give it a structure
— a map of the places businesses show up online, and the steps a customer takes from 'I have never heard of them' to 'I
am a regular customer'. That map is the foundation of everything we will build in this course."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1 with three apps, spend 2 extra minutes asking learners to name apps
they see on each other's phones before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What digital marketing actually is
The idea. Digital marketing is how a business finds people, talks to them and wins their custom using the internet and digital
tools — phones, websites, social media, search engines, email and messaging apps.
Plain-English definition. Digital marketing is everything a business does online to get noticed, build trust and sell — from a
Google search result to an Instagram post to a WhatsApp message. If a customer sees it, reads it or clicks it on a phone or
computer, it is digital marketing.
Why it exists. Before the internet, a small business found customers through its location, word of mouth, flyers, radio and TV.
Those still work — but most people now spend hours every day on their phones. A young person in Port Harcourt might walk
past a shop every day and never notice it, but search "food delivery near me" at 7pm and order from a kitchen they have never
visited. Digital marketing makes sure your business is the one they find.
What it does:
Gets you found — someone looking for what you sell can discover you.
Builds trust — a stranger sees your page, reads reviews, looks at photos and decides you are real.
Starts a conversation — they message you, call you, send a DM.
Turns interest into a sale — they order, pay, book, come to your shop.
Keeps them coming back — you stay in touch, remind them, reward them.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader in Mile 1 or Mile 3 market in Port Harcourt. She has a stall (her location), she
calls out to passers-by (advertising), she lets you taste a piece of what she sells (social proof), she remembers your name and
asks about your family (retention), and she writes her number on a piece of paper and says "call me when you need more"
(direct marketing). Digital marketing is doing all of those things — but on a phone screen, to people who are not physically
walking past your stall.
Important limitation / misconception. Digital marketing is not magic. It will not save a bad product, fix terrible customer
service or make a dishonest business succeed for long. It is a megaphone and a bridge — it amplifies what is already there. If
the food is good and the service is fair, digital marketing brings more customers. If the food is bad, digital marketing just helps
more people discover how bad it is, faster.
How a learner recognises digital marketing in the wild. Open any app on your phone. Every time you see a business name, a
product photo, a "sponsored" label, a review, a search result, a message from a shop — that is digital marketing. You are
surrounded by it. The question is whether your business or your client's business is the one doing it well.
F.2 The eight main digital marketing channels
The idea. A channel is simply a place or method a business uses to reach customers. There are many, but eight cover almost
everything a small business needs. You do not need all eight — you need the two or three that match where your customers
actually spend time.
Plain-English definition. A channel is a road between a business and a customer. Social media is a road. Google Search is a road.
WhatsApp is a road. Your job is to pick the roads your customers actually walk on — not to build eight roads and leave seven of
them empty.
The eight channels, explained one by one:
1. Website. A website is your business's home on the internet — a collection of pages on a web address (like
www.example.com). For a small business, a simple one-page site is often enough: what you sell, where you are, how to contact
you, and how to order. A website is owned — nobody can change the rules or delete it overnight. Think of it as your shop
building: it is permanent, you control what is inside, and customers can visit any time. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, a simple
one-page site showing her menu, location and order number would be enough to start.
2. Search (Google). When someone types "caterer in Port Harcourt" or "jollof rice delivery near me" into Google, the results
they see are the search channel. Businesses try to appear high in those results — this is called SEO (Search Engine
Optimisation), which we cover in Session 5. Search is powerful because the person is already looking — they have intent. If
someone is typing "food near me" at 7pm, they are hungry now. That is a warm customer.
3. Social media. Platforms like Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Twitter (X) and YouTube where people share content and
businesses post, interact and build an audience. Social media is where you show your personality, your food, your work, your
customers. It is the channel most small businesses already use — but often without a plan. We fix that in Session 4. For Mama
Nkechi's Kitchen, Instagram and Facebook are the right social channels because food photographs well and her customers are
already there.
4. Content. Content is the material you create to inform, entertain or inspire — blog posts, videos, photos, infographics,
guides, recipes. Content marketing means giving something useful for free that makes people trust you and want to buy. A
food kitchen might share a "how to make the perfect jollof" video — people watch it, trust the kitchen, and order. Content is
the fuel for social media and search. Without content, social media is an empty shop with no products.
5. Email. Sending messages directly to a person's email inbox. Email is owned — you have the list, nobody can take it away, and
you can reach people without depending on an algorithm. It is the best channel for repeat business: newsletters, special offers,
reminders. For a small business, even a simple monthly email to 50 customers can bring orders. We cover this in Session 6.
6. WhatsApp. In Nigeria, WhatsApp is not just a messaging app — it is a business tool. WhatsApp Business lets a business
create a profile, a catalogue of products, labels for customers, and broadcast messages. For many small Nigerian businesses,
WhatsApp is the sales channel — customers message, order, pay on delivery. It is personal, direct and trusted. We cover it in
Session 6. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, WhatsApp is already her main order channel — we will make it work harder.
7. Paid ads. Paying a platform (Meta for Facebook/Instagram, Google for Search and YouTube) to show your business to more
people. Paid ads are rented attention — while you pay, you get seen; when you stop, you disappear. They can work well for a
new business that has no audience yet, but they must be done carefully and with a real budget. We cover this in Session 7. For
Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, a small paid campaign could help her reach new customers in her area — but only after her free
channels are working.
8. Google Business Profile. A free listing on Google that shows your business name, address, phone number, photos, reviews
and opening hours when people search for you or your category on Google Search and Google Maps. For a local business — a
kitchen, a salon, a shop — this is often the single most important channel, because it puts you in front of people searching for
exactly what you sell, in your area. We cover it in Session 3. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, setting up a complete Google Business
Profile is the first practical win we will aim for.
How the channels work together (the key lesson):
"A customer rarely sees one thing and buys. A real journey looks like this: someone sees a friend's Instagram story about
Mama Nkechi's jollof (social media). Curious, they Google 'Mama Nkechi's Kitchen Port Harcourt' (search) and find her
Google Business Profile with good photos and reviews (Google Business Profile). They visit her one-page website to see the
full menu (website). They message her on WhatsApp to ask about delivery (WhatsApp). They order (conversion). A week
later, she sends a WhatsApp broadcast: 'New soup menu this week' (WhatsApp again), and they order again (retention).
That is not five separate tactics — that is one customer journey across five channels, all working together."
Common misconception. Learners often think they must be on all eight channels at once, posting everywhere every day. This is
the fastest way to burn out and fail. The right approach: pick two or three channels that match where your customers are, do
them well, and expand later. For Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, the right starting three are: Google Business Profile, Instagram, and
WhatsApp. Not Facebook, not TikTok, not email, not paid ads — not yet. We will add channels as we learn them.
How a learner recognises which channels a business uses. Open the business's Instagram page — that is social media.
Google the business name — if a listing appears with a map, that is Google Business Profile. Check if they have a web address
— that is a website. Ask how you order — if it is WhatsApp, that is the WhatsApp channel. Every business is using at least one
channel; the skill is seeing which ones and judging whether they are doing them well.
F.3 The marketing funnel — awareness, consideration, conversion, retention
The idea. Not every customer is ready to buy the moment they hear about you. Some have never heard of you. Some have
heard but are not sure. Some are ready to buy. Some have bought and might buy again. The funnel is a way of thinking about
these four stages, so you know what to say and where to say it depending on where the customer is.
Plain-English definition. The funnel is the journey a customer travels from first discovering you to becoming a loyal repeat
buyer. It is called a funnel because many people enter at the top (awareness) and fewer and fewer reach the bottom (purchase)
— like water poured into a wide funnel narrowing to a small opening.
The four stages, explained:
Stage 1 — Awareness. The customer has just discovered you exist. They do not know you, do not trust you yet, and probably
were not actively looking for you. They saw your Instagram post, heard a friend mention you, or saw your Google listing. At this
stage, your job is simple: get noticed and make a good first impression. You do not ask for a sale here — you ask for attention.
Example: an Instagram reel showing sizzling jollof rice with steam rising. The viewer thinks "that looks good" and maybe
watches to the end. That is awareness.
Stage 2 — Consideration. The customer now knows you exist and is actively thinking about whether to buy. They are
comparing you to alternatives. They are reading your reviews, checking your menu, asking a friend, looking at your photos,
checking your prices. At this stage, your job is to build trust and answer their questions. Example: the customer visits Mama
Nkechi's Google Business Profile, reads five reviews saying "food is always hot and on time", and looks at photos of the small
chops. They are considering.
Stage 3 — Conversion. The customer decides to buy. They place an order, make a booking, pay a deposit, walk into your
shop. This is the moment you have been working toward. At this stage, your job is to make buying easy and frictionless.
Example: the customer messages Mama Nkechi on WhatsApp: "Please send one plate of jollof and small chops to GRA." She
replies with the price, the customer pays via transfer or on delivery, and the food is sent. Conversion complete.
Stage 4 — Retention. The customer has bought once. Now the real opportunity begins: will they come back? Most
businesses ignore this stage and lose customers to competitors who do not. At this stage, your job is to stay in touch, reward
them and remind them you exist. Example: two weeks later, Mama Nkechi sends a WhatsApp broadcast to her customer list:
"Good morning! New bean cake recipe this week. Reply YES to order." The customer replies and orders again. That is retention
— and it is far cheaper than finding a brand-new customer.
Why the funnel matters (the key lesson):
"If you try to sell to someone at the awareness stage, you will fail. If you send a 'buy now' message to someone who has
never heard of you, they will ignore you or block you. If you only focus on getting new customers and never follow up, you
will lose the ones you already won. The funnel tells you what to say and when. At awareness: show them something good.
At consideration: prove you are trustworthy. At conversion: make it easy to buy. At retention: stay in touch. Match the
message to the stage."
Real-life comparison. The funnel is like dating. At first, someone sees you across the room (awareness). Then they come over
and talk to you, ask questions, check if you are real (consideration). Then they agree to go on a date (conversion). Then, if the
date went well, they keep seeing you — a relationship (retention). You would not propose marriage the first time you see
someone across the room. Marketing works the same way. Do not rush the stages.

Common misconception. "The funnel is about tricking people into buying." No. The funnel is about understanding where a
person is in their decision and giving them the right information and experience at each stage. Pushing a sale too early is not
persuasive — it is pushy. Building trust at the right pace is what actually works.
How a learner recognises funnel stages in real life. Think about the last thing you bought online. Trace back: where did you
first see it (awareness)? What did you check before buying (consideration)? How did you actually buy it (conversion)? Have you
bought from that business again, and did they stay in touch (retention)? Every purchase you have ever made follows this
pattern.
F.4 The customer journey — mapping the real path a customer takes
The idea. The funnel is the theory — the four stages. The customer journey is the practice — the specific steps a real customer
takes, channel by channel, from first hearing about you to becoming a repeat buyer. Mapping the journey means writing down
Plain-English definition. A customer journey is the story of one customer's experience with your business, told step by step:
what they see, what they think, what they do, and where they do it. Mapping means drawing that story out so you can see the
whole path at a glance.
Why it exists. Without a journey map, you are guessing. You might spend all week on Instagram posts when your customers
actually find you through Google. You might write long captions when your customers decide based on reviews. Mapping the
journey shows you the actual path customers take, so you can put your effort where it matters.
The five parts of a customer journey map:
1. Who — the specific customer (we will build a detailed customer avatar in Session 2; for now, a simple description is fine).
2. What they see — the thing that reaches them (an ad, a post, a search result, a friend's recommendation).
3. What they think — their reaction ("that looks good", "is this real?", "how much?").
4. What they do — the action they take (click, search, message, visit, order, nothing).
5. Where it happens — the channel (Instagram, Google, WhatsApp, website, in person).

The relationship (say it slowly):

drives. You pick the roads your channels give you. You use the funnel to know what to say at each stage. You use the

F.6 The ethics foundation — honest marketing from day one
The idea. Before we build any channel or write any post, we must agree on the rules. Digital marketing gives you power — the
power to reach thousands of people. That power must be used honestly. The academy's Ethics and Professional Conduct rules
(Section 15 of the Design Document) are not optional. They are the foundation.
The five rules that matter most today (read aloud and explain each):
1. Honest advertising. Never claim what you cannot prove. If the jollof is ₦1,500, say ₦1,500. If delivery takes 2 hours, say 2
hours — not "30 minutes" to look good. If you say "made fresh every morning", it must be true. Fake discounts, invented
urgency ("only 2 left!" when there are 200) and miracle results ("this cream will make you fair in 7 days") are all dishonest.
They might work once — and then destroy your reputation for good.
2. No fake reviews. Never write a review for your own business, pay someone to write a good review, or ask a friend to
pretend they are a customer. Reviews are the digital word of mouth — they are the most trusted thing a business has. Fake
reviews are fraud. If a real customer leaves a bad review, reply professionally and fix the problem — do not delete it (if you
can even) or retaliate. A business that responds well to a bad review often earns more trust than one with only perfect fives.
3. No buying engagement. Never buy followers, likes, views, comments or shares. Bought followers are fake accounts —
they will never buy from you, they distort your data (so you cannot tell what is working), and platforms penalise accounts
that use them. Growth must be earned. 500 real followers who actually buy are worth more than 50,000 fake ones who never
will.
4. Respect privacy and data (NDPR-minded). Nigeria's Data Protection Regulation (NDPR) says you must collect only the
data you need, use it only for the agreed purpose, keep it secure and never sell it. In practice: do not scrape phone numbers
from Facebook groups. Do not buy a list of phone numbers. Do not add people to WhatsApp broadcasts without their
permission. In this course, we use only our own data and invented business data — never real customer lists.
5. Permission-based messaging and anti-spam. Email and WhatsApp messages go only to people who said yes. Identify
yourself, give an easy opt-out ("reply STOP to unsubscribe"), honour it immediately, and never spam groups. Unsolicited
bulk messaging is spam — it is illegal under NDPR-minded practice, it annoys people and it gets your number blocked. We
cover the details in Session 6, but the rule starts today: always ask before you send.
Why ethics matter for a small business (say this):
"A big company can survive a scandal. A small business cannot. Mama Nkechi's Kitchen lives on trust — her customers
order because they trust her food and her word. If she lies in an advert, uses fake reviews or spams people, she might get a
few orders today and lose her reputation for good. Honest marketing is not just the right thing to do — it is the smart
thing to do for a small business. Trust is the most valuable asset a small business has. We protect it."
The academy's ethics motto (say it together with the class):
Honest claims. Real reviews. Earned growth. Asked permission. Protected data.
Common misconception. "Everyone else is doing it — fake followers, fake reviews, spam. If I do not, I will fall behind." This is
false. The businesses that win long-term are the ones that build real trust. Fake growth collapses — when the platform catches
on, when customers realise, when the data makes no sense. Real growth is slower but it is permanent. And in a course like this,
we teach the permanent way.
How a learner applies ethics from today. Before you post anything, send anything or claim anything, ask three questions: Is
this true? Would I be comfortable if the customer knew exactly how I got their attention? Would I do this if the customer were
standing in front of me? If the answer to any of these is no — do not do it.
F.7 Summary of the lecture
Digital marketing is how a business finds people, talks to them and wins their custom using the internet and digital tools.
The eight main channels are: website, search, social media, content, email, WhatsApp, paid ads and Google Business
Profile. Pick the two or three that match your customers — not all eight.
The funnel has four stages: Awareness (they discover you), Consideration (they check you out), Conversion (they buy),
Retention (they come back). Match your message to the stage.
The customer journey is the specific path one customer takes through the funnel, channel by channel. Map it to see where
to improve.
Channels work together. No single channel does the whole job. Build a chain: each channel points to the next step.
Ethics come first. Honest claims, real reviews, earned growth, asked permission, protected data — always.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Adaeze runs a small Ankara clothing shop in Port Harcourt. She sells ready-made dresses and takes custom orders.
She has an Instagram page with 600 followers — mostly friends and family. She posts a photo of a new dress maybe once a
month, with captions like "New stock. Call us." She has no Google Business Profile, no website, and no WhatsApp Business
account — she uses her personal WhatsApp. She gets a few customers a week, mostly people who walk past her shop or hear
about her from a friend. She wants more customers but does not know where to start.
Relevant facts.
Adaeze's dresses are well made and fairly priced (₦3,000–₦15,000).
Her Instagram page has beautiful photos of her dresses — but no prices, no location, no way to order except "call us."
She does not appear when someone searches "Ankara dress shop Port Harcourt" on Google.
She keeps her customers' phone numbers in her personal phone but never follows up after a sale.
A competitor, Zara's Fabrics, has a Google Business Profile with 4.8 stars and 30 reviews, a simple website with a catalogue,
and a WhatsApp Business account. Zara gets customers every week from Google searches and WhatsApp enquiries.
Decision point. Adaeze has ₦5,000 to spend on marketing this month. She is thinking about three options:
1. Boost her Instagram posts with ₦5,000 in paid ads.
2. Hire someone to manage her social media full-time.
3. Spend the ₦5,000 on a small printing run of flyers to distribute in her area.
What should she do?
Possible approaches.
1. Run Instagram ads with ₦5,000. This could get her posts in front of more people — but if those people click and find a
page with no prices, no location and no clear way to order, they will leave. Ads bring people to the door; they do not build
the house first.
2. Hire a social media manager. This could work long-term — but it is a recurring monthly cost (₦20,000–₦50,000), and
Adaeze does not have that yet. She needs to build her foundation first.
3. Print and distribute flyers. Flyers can work for a local shop — but they are one-time, hard to measure, and most flyers end
up in the bin. The ₦5,000 might buy 500 flyers and zero customers.
Recommended solution. None of the three. Adaeze's problem is not awareness — her dresses are good and her Instagram
photos are beautiful. Her problem is that the chain is broken: people who find her on Instagram cannot easily find her location,
prices or reviews, and people searching on Google cannot find her at all. The ₦5,000 is better spent — or rather, not spent at all
— on fixing the foundation first:
1. Set up a Google Business Profile (free). Add her shop name, address, phone number, photos of her best dresses, opening
hours, and start asking real customers to leave honest reviews. This makes her visible when people search "Ankara dress
shop Port Harcourt" — the exact moment someone is looking to buy.
2. Fix her Instagram page (free). Add prices to her photos, put her location and WhatsApp number in the bio, and post once
a week with a clear call-to-action ("DM 'DRESS' to order"). This turns her existing page into a real sales channel.
3. Switch to WhatsApp Business (free). Set up a catalogue with her dresses and prices, create labels for customers, and send a
gentle follow-up after each purchase: "Thank you! How do you love your dress? Reply with a photo and get 10% off your
next order." This builds retention at no cost.
4. Only then, consider paid ads. Once the foundation is solid — Google listing, clear Instagram page, WhatsApp catalogue
— a small paid campaign (₦5,000 over two weeks) would work because the chain is complete. People who click the ad will
find a real business they can trust and buy from.
Trade-offs.
Option 1 (ads now): fast reach, but wasted money because the chain is broken. The ₦5,000 would buy clicks, not
customers.
Option 2 (hire a manager): too expensive for Adaeze's current stage. She needs to build the foundation herself first, then hire
help when the business can afford it.
Option 3 (flyers): cheap and local, but unmeasurable and unlikely to reach the young women in GRA who are already
searching on Google.
Recommended (fix the foundation): free or nearly free, builds permanent assets (Google listing, WhatsApp catalogue, better
Instagram), and makes any future spending — ads, flyers, or hiring — work much better.
Professional connection. This is exactly what a digital marketing consultant does in their first week with a client: audit the
foundation before recommending any spending. A consultant who immediately says "run ads" without checking the Google
listing, the website and the follow-up process is wasting the client's money. The foundation first, the spending second. This is
the mindset you are building in this course — and it is why Session 3 (online presence) comes before Session 7 (paid ads).

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Confusing digital marketing with "social media"
Symptoms: Learner says "digital marketing is Instagram" or "I need to go viral." Likely cause: Social media is the most visible
channel, so learners assume it is the only one. How to investigate: Ask: "Is a Google search result social media? Is a WhatsApp
message social media? Is a website social media?" Corrective steps: Re-explain the eight channels. Show that social media is
one channel among eight, and that the funnel and journey span all of them. How to confirm resolved: Learner can name at
least four channels that are not social media. Prevention: Use the eight-channel cards from the first session; keep them visible
on the wall.
K.2 Putting a tactic in the wrong funnel stage
Symptoms: Learner says "a Google review is awareness" or "an Instagram post is conversion." Likely cause: The learner has not
separated discovering the business from deciding to buy. How to investigate: Ask: "At this step, has the customer already
found the business? If yes, it is probably consideration or later." Corrective steps: Re-explain: Awareness = they discover you
exist. Consideration = they found you and are deciding. Conversion = they buy. Retention = they come back. How to confirm
resolved: Learner correctly sorts a new tactic and explains why. Prevention: Use the sorting exercise regularly; make learners
explain their reasoning, not just pick a column.
K.3 Thinking one channel does the whole job
Symptoms: Learner maps a journey with only one channel (e.g., "she sees an Instagram post and buys"). Likely cause: The
learner has not yet internalised that real customers move across channels. How to investigate: Ask: "If that channel
disappeared tomorrow, would the customer still find the business?" Corrective steps: Re-draw the worked example journey
(Chinedu) and point out every channel change. Ask the learner to add a second channel to each step of their own journey. How
to confirm resolved: Learner's journey includes at least three different channels. Prevention: In every example you give, use at
least three channels. Never tell a story with only one.
K.4 Ignoring the retention stage
Symptoms: Learner's funnel has an empty Retention column; their journey ends at the first purchase. Likely cause: The learner
thinks marketing ends when the customer buys. How to investigate: Ask: "What happens after the customer buys? Do they
ever hear from the business again?" Corrective steps: Explain that retention is cheaper than finding new customers. A repeat
customer spends more, refers friends and gives reviews. Show Mama Nkechi's WhatsApp broadcast example. How to confirm
resolved: Learner's journey includes at least one post-purchase step. Prevention: In every journey you build together, always
ask: "What happens next week? Next month?"
K.5 Mapping an unrealistic journey
Symptoms: Learner's journey includes TV ads, billboards, radio or a website she cannot afford. Likely cause: The learner is not
yet thinking about what a small business can realistically do. How to investigate: Ask: "How much does that cost? Can Mama
Nkechi afford that? What free tools could she use instead?" Corrective steps: Remind learners of the "no paid subscription" rule
and the realistic toolset: smartphone, free Google tools, free Canva, free WhatsApp Business. How to confirm resolved: Every
step in the learner's journey uses a free or already-owned tool. Prevention: At the start of every journey exercise, remind
learners: "Small business. Phone only. No budget for ads yet."
K.6 Thinking the funnel is about tricking people
Symptoms: Learner says "the funnel is how you manipulate people into buying." Likely cause: The learner has heard "funnel"
used in a pushy, salesy context. How to investigate: Ask: "Is pushing a sale to someone who has never heard of you persuasive
or just annoying?" Corrective steps: Re-explain: the funnel is about understanding where the customer is and giving them the
right information at each stage. Pushing too early drives people away. Building trust at the right pace is what works. How to
confirm resolved: Learner explains the funnel as a way to help the customer decide, not force them. Prevention: Always pair
the funnel explanation with the dating analogy — it keeps the tone right.
K.7 Forgetting that channels must connect
Symptoms: Learner's journey has steps that do not lead to each other (e.g., Instagram post → nothing → website → nothing
→ sale). Likely cause: The learner has not drawn the arrows between steps. How to investigate: Ask: "How does the customer
get from step 1 to step 2? What makes them take that next action?" Corrective steps: Have the learner add arrows between
every step and label what drives the transition (e.g., "the bio says 'order on WhatsApp' → customer taps the link"). How to
confirm resolved: Every step in the journey has an arrow to the next, with a reason. Prevention: In every journey you build on
the board, always draw the arrows and say what drives each transition.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can point to the
exact step where the journey breaks). Teach learners to confirm before concluding — ask them to show you the step, not just
say "it is wrong."

## N. Reflection and Recap
<!-- kind:reflection -->
Plain-English summary. Today you learned the map of digital marketing. You learned that digital marketing is how a business
finds people, talks to them and wins their custom using the internet and digital tools — phones, websites, social media, search
engines, email and messaging apps. You met the eight main channels: website, search, social media, content, email, WhatsApp,
paid ads and Google Business Profile — and you learned to pick the two or three that match where your customers actually are,
rather than trying to be everywhere at once. You learned the marketing funnel: Awareness (they discover you), Consideration
(they check you out), Conversion (they buy), Retention (they come back) — and why you must match your message to the
stage. You learned to map a customer journey: the specific path one customer takes, channel by channel, from first hearing
about you to becoming a repeat buyer. And you learned the most important idea of all: channels work together. No single
channel does the whole job — you must build a chain, and every link must be strong. Finally, you learned that ethics come
first: honest claims, real reviews, earned growth, asked permission, protected data — always.
Five important takeaways:
1. Digital marketing is how a business reaches customers online — and everyone with a phone is already part of it.
2. There are eight main channels; pick the two or three that match your customers, not all eight.
3. The funnel has four stages — Awareness, Consideration, Conversion, Retention — and your message must match the
stage.
4. The customer journey is the real path a customer takes; map it to find the weak links.
5. Channels work together — build the chain, and never skip ethics.
Questions to help you recognise what you can now do:
Can I name the eight digital marketing channels and explain what each one does?
Can I sort a marketing tactic into the correct funnel stage and explain why?
Can I map a customer journey with at least five steps, each with a channel?
Can I explain why channels need to work together, using a real example?
Can I state the academy's ethics rules in my own words?
Connection to the next session. You now have the map — the channels, the funnel and the journey. But there is one thing
missing: who is the customer? A funnel and a journey are only as good as the person they describe. Next session we build a
customer avatar — a detailed picture of one specific customer, with their goals, pains, objections and favourite platforms —
so that every tactic, message and journey we build is aimed at a real person, not at "everybody." Because marketing to everyone,
as you will learn, is marketing to nobody.

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Digital marketing is social media." → No — social media is one of eight channels; Google, WhatsApp, email and more are
equally important.
"The funnel is about pushing people to buy." → No — the funnel is about matching the message to the customer's stage;
pushing too early drives them away.
"More channels = better." → No — pick two or three that match your customers and do them well. Spreading thin across
eight channels is how businesses fail.
"The customer journey is the same for everyone." → No — different customers have different journeys; that is why we
build an avatar (Session 2) first.
"Fake followers and reviews are a shortcut." → No — they are fraud, they distort data and they destroy trust. Real growth is
earned.
"Marketing ends when the customer buys." → No — retention is where the profit is; a repeat customer is cheaper than a
new one.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Channel naming drill: Show a screenshot of a business's Instagram page, Google listing and website. Ask the learner to
name which channel each one is and what job it does.
2. Funnel sorting drill: Give five new tactic cards (not used in class) and ask the learner to sort them and explain each choice.
3. Journey building drill: Give the learner a started journey (first two steps) and ask them to add three more steps, naming the
channel for each.
4. Weakest link drill: Give the learner a completed journey and ask them to circle the weakest step and suggest a fix.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a partially completed funnel table (three tactics already placed per stage) and ask the learner
to add one more per stage. Provide a journey template with the first three steps filled in; the learner adds three more.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full funnel sort and journey build for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen, plus the
independent exercise for Blessing's salon.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to build two different customer journeys for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen — one for a
young professional (Chinedu) and one for a student with a smaller budget — and explain how the journeys differ and why
the marketing should differ. Then ask them to identify which funnel stage is most important for each customer type and
why.
