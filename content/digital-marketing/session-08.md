# Analytics, Tracking and Optimisation

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Convert and Measure

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (concepts and reading, not heavy software)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–7. Learners should be able to build an avatar, write a value proposition, plan content, set up a Google Business Profile, draft permission-based messages, and structure a paid ad campaign. No prior analytics experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 7 we learned how to spend money on ads — objectives, targeting, budgets and creative. But spending without measuring is like driving with your eyes closed. Today we learn the measurement layer: how to choose the right numbers to watch, read a basic Google Analytics report, build UTM links so every click traces to its source, run a simple A/B test, and write a one-page report that says what happened and what to change. This is the session that makes every other session pay for itself.

**Preparation before class:** Academy computers or phones with internet ready. Prepare projector slides showing a sample Google Analytics dashboard (sessions, users, traffic sources, top pages). Print the KPI Matching Worksheet (Section H) per learner. Prepare a sample UTM link builder demonstration. Have a sample one-page report ready for the demo. Prepare a fictional analytics dataset for the independent exercise. Print the A/B Test Planner worksheet. Print the SIMULATION brief — "Integrated Campaign Sprint" (Part 5, Template J) — instructor-held: do not hand it out until minute 66. Instructor note on tone: Many learners find analytics intimidating — they think it is for "data people." Your job is to replace fear with clarity. Analytics is not about being a mathematician; it is about being a detective. You are looking for clues: where did people come from, what did they do, and what should we change? Every small business owner can learn to read a basic report and make one good decision from it. That is the skill we build today.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define KPIs and explain the difference between vanity metrics and actionable metrics, choosing KPIs that match a stated
business goal.
2. Read a basic Google Analytics report — identifying sessions, users, traffic sources, top pages and events — and explain
what each metric tells them.
3. Build correctly tagged UTM links using a free UTM builder, with a consistent naming convention, so every click traces to
its exact source.
4. Plan a simple A/B test — changing one variable only, defining a success metric, and deciding when to keep the winner.
5. Write a one-page report that says what happened, what it means, and what to change next.
6. Apply honest measurement practice — no cherry-picking data, no claiming causation from correlation, no ignoring
results that contradict what they hoped was true.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two screenshots side by side.
Screenshot 1 — "The Vanity Dashboard": A social media analytics page showing: 10,000 followers, 50,000 likes, 500
comments, 2,000 shares. Big numbers. Impressive.
Screenshot 2 — "The Business Dashboard": A simple table showing: 200 website visitors, 45 WhatsApp messages, 12 orders
placed, ₦18,000 revenue, ₦3,000 ad spend. Smaller numbers. Less impressive.
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses. Both looking at their numbers. The first one is celebrating — 10,000 followers, 50,000 likes. The second
one is planning — 200 visitors, 45 messages, 12 orders, ₦18,000 in revenue. Which business is actually doing better? The
first one has big numbers, but I do not know if they made a single naira. The second one has smaller numbers, but I know
exactly what is happening: people are visiting, people are messaging, people are buying, and they are making more than
they spent. Big numbers feel good. But small numbers that tell the truth are worth more. Today you learn to be the second
business — the one that measures what matters."
Why this opener works: It creates an immediate emotional contrast between vanity and substance. It sets up the central
question: what should you actually measure? And it reassures learners that analytics is not about big numbers — it is about the
right numbers.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 KPIs — measuring what matters, not what feels good
The idea. A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a number that tells you whether you are moving toward your goal. Not every
number is a KPI. A KPI is a number you can act on — a number that helps you decide what to do next.
Vanity metrics vs. actionable metrics. This is the most important distinction in measurement.
Vanity metrics feel good but do not help you make decisions. Examples: followers, likes, impressions, total page views.
They go up, but you cannot tell what to do differently because of them.
Actionable metrics tell you something you can act on. Examples: click-through rate, conversion rate, cost per lead, revenue
per customer, bounce rate. They tell you what is working and what is not.
Say to the class:
"If your follower count goes from 1,000 to 2,000, what do you do differently? Nothing — you do not know why it grew or
what to repeat. But if your click-through rate goes from 1% to 3%, you know something in your ad or post is working —
and you can do more of it. Vanity metrics make you feel good. Actionable metrics make you make better decisions."
Matching KPIs to goals — the golden rule:
Goal KPI to watch Why
Awareness (people know you exist) Reach, impressions You want to know how many people saw you
Traffic (people visit your site/profile) Sessions, users, click-through rate You want to know if people are clicking
Leads (people contact you) Messages, form submissions, calls You want to know if people are raising their
hand
Sales (people buy) Orders, revenue, conversion rate You want to know if people are paying
Retention (people come back) Repeat purchases, returning
visitors
You want to know if people are loyal
The KPI-goal matching rule: Choose one primary KPI per goal. Do not try to watch everything — you will watch nothing. If
your goal is orders, your primary KPI is orders (or conversion rate). If your goal is awareness, your primary KPI is reach.
Everything else is secondary.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Goal: Get more WhatsApp orders.
Primary KPI: Number of WhatsApp messages received (leads) and number of orders placed (conversion).
Secondary KPI: Click-through rate on ads (are people clicking?), cost per message (how much does each enquiry cost?).
Vanity metric to ignore: Follower count on Instagram.
Common misconception. "I should track everything so I do not miss anything." No — tracking everything means you pay
attention to nothing. Choose one primary KPI per goal, watch it weekly, and act on it. You can always add more later.
F.2 Google Analytics basics — reading the story your website tells
The idea. Google Analytics (GA4) is a free tool that tracks what happens on your website. It tells you how many people visited,
where they came from, what they did, and whether they took action. You do not need to be a data scientist to read it — you
need to know five things.
The five things every beginner must know:
1. Sessions. A session is one visit to your website. If I visit your site, look at three pages, and leave, that is one session. If I come
back tomorrow, that is another session. Sessions tell you how much traffic you are getting.
2. Users. A user is a unique person. If I visit your site five times, that is five sessions but one user. Users tell you how many real
people are coming.
3. Traffic sources. This tells you where your visitors came from. The main sources are:
Organic search — people who found you on Google.
Direct — people who typed your URL or clicked a saved link.
Social — people who clicked a link on Instagram, Facebook, etc.
Referral — people who clicked a link on another website.
Paid — people who clicked an ad.
4. Top pages. This tells you which pages on your site people actually visit. If your menu page gets 500 views but your order page
gets 10, you have a problem — people are not finding the order page.
5. Events. An event is any action someone takes on your site: clicking a button, playing a video, submitting a form, clicking a
WhatsApp link. Events tell you what people are doing, not just where they went.
Say to the class:
"Google Analytics is like a shopkeeper who stands at the door and counts everyone who walks in, notes where they came
from, watches which aisles they browse, and writes down what they buy. You do not need to know everything it tracks. You
need to know: how many people came, where they came from, what they looked at, and whether they took action. That is
the story. Everything else is detail."
Worked example — reading a GA4 dashboard for Mama Nkechi's Kitchen:
Metric Number What it means
Sessions (this week) 320 320 visits to the website
Users 210 210 unique people
Top traffic source Social (45%) Most visitors came from Instagram
Top page Menu page (180 views) People are looking at the menu
WhatsApp button clicks 45 45 people clicked the WhatsApp order button
Conversion rate 14% 45 out of 320 visitors clicked to order
What the story tells us: People are finding the website from Instagram (good — the content is working). They are looking at
the menu (good — they are interested). And 14% of them are clicking to order on WhatsApp (good — the ordering process is
working). But 86% are leaving without ordering. That is the opportunity — why are 86% leaving? Is the menu unclear? Is the
price too high? Is the WhatsApp button hard to find? The data tells you where to look.
Common misconception. "I need to understand every feature of Google Analytics." No — you need to understand five things:
sessions, users, traffic sources, top pages and events. Master those, and you can read any basic report. The rest is detail you
learn over time.
F.3 UTM links — knowing exactly where every click comes from
The idea. A UTM link is a normal link with a few extra words added to the end that tell Google Analytics exactly where the click
came from. Without UTM links, all your social media clicks show up as "Social" — you cannot tell which post, which platform
or which campaign brought the person. With UTM links, you know exactly.
The five UTM parameters:
Parameter What it tells you Example
```text
utm_source
```
Where the click came from (platform) instagram , facebook , whatsapp , email
```text
utm_medium
```
What type of channel social , email , paid , organic
```text
utm_campaign
```
Which campaign or post week1-launch , jollof-promo , birthday-cakes
```text
utm_content
```
Which specific ad or creative (optional) photo-jollof , reel-cooking
```text
utm_term
```
Which keyword (optional, for paid search)
```text
jollof+delivery
```
How to build a UTM link:
1. Start with your normal URL: https://mamankitchen.com/order
2. Add a question mark: https://mamankitchen.com/order?
3. Add the first parameter: https://mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=instagram
4. Add an ampersand and the next parameter: https://mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social
5. Continue until all parameters are added: https://mamankitchen.com/order?
```text
utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=week1-launch&utm_content=photo-jollof
```
The naming convention rule: Be consistent. Decide on your format and stick to it. Use lowercase, use hyphens (not spaces),
and use the same names every time. If you use instagram one week and Instagram the next, Google Analytics will treat them as
two different sources. Consistency is everything.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen UTM links:
Link UTM tagged version
Instagram post (jollof photo)
```text
mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=week1-
```
```text
launch&utm_content=photo-jollof
```
WhatsApp broadcast
```text
mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=whatsapp&utm_medium=social&utm_campaign=week1-launch
```
Facebook ad
```text
mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=facebook&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=jollof-
```
```text
promo&utm_content=reel-cooking
```
Email newsletter
```text
mamankitchen.com/order?utm_source=email&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=weekly-menu
```
Say to the class:
"Without UTM links, you are guessing. You know people came from social media, but you do not know which post, which
platform or which campaign. With UTM links, you know exactly. You know that the Instagram photo brought 20 clicks, the
WhatsApp broadcast brought 15, and the Facebook ad brought 5. Now you know where to spend your time and money.
UTM links turn guessing into knowing."
Common misconception. "UTM links are only for big businesses with websites." No — if you have a Google Business Profile, a
landing page, or even a link-in-bio page, you can use UTM links. Anywhere you can put a link, you can tag it. And the free UTM
builder does the work for you — you just fill in the blanks.
F.4 A/B testing — changing one thing, learning one thing
The idea. An A/B test is when you show two versions of something to see which performs better. Version A is the original (the
"control"). Version B is the change (the "variant"). You split your audience, show each group one version, and measure which
one wins.
The golden rule of A/B testing: change ONE variable only.
If you change the image AND the headline AND the CTA all at once, and version B wins, you do not know which change caused
the win. Was it the image? The headline? The CTA? You cannot tell. But if you change only the image, and version B wins, you
know the image was the reason. One variable. One lesson.
What you can A/B test:
Ad creative (image A vs. image B)
Ad copy (headline A vs. headline B)
CTA (button text A vs. button text B)
Landing page (layout A vs. layout B)
Email subject line (subject A vs. subject B)
WhatsApp broadcast message (message A vs. message B)
How to plan an A/B test:
1. Choose one variable to test. (e.g., the image in your Instagram ad)
2. Create two versions. Version A (control) and Version B (variant) — identical except for the one variable.
3. Define your success metric. (e.g., click-through rate, or number of orders)
4. Decide how long to run the test. (e.g., 3 days, or until you have at least 100 clicks per version)
5. Decide your decision rule. (e.g., "If version B gets a higher click-through rate after 100 clicks, I keep B. If not, I keep A.")
6. Run the test, measure, and keep the winner.
Worked example — Mama Nkechi's Kitchen A/B test:
Element Version A (control) Version B (variant)
Image Photo of jollof rice Reel of jollof being cooked
Headline "Too tired to cook?" "Hot jollof in 45 minutes"
CTA "Order on WhatsApp" "Order on WhatsApp"
Everything else Same Same
Variable being tested: The image (photo vs. reel).
Success metric: Click-through rate.
Duration: 3 days or 100 clicks per version.
Decision rule: Whichever version has a higher click-through rate after 100 clicks wins.
Result: Version B (reel) got 4.2% CTR; Version A (photo) got 2.8% CTR. Winner: Version B. Keep the reel. Next test: try a
different headline.
Say to the class:
"A/B testing is how you stop guessing and start knowing. You have two ideas for an ad image. Instead of arguing about
which is better, you test it. You show each to a group of people, you measure the results, and you keep the winner. It is not
about being clever — it is about being curious. The businesses that win at marketing are not the ones with the best ideas.
They are the ones that test the most ideas."
Common misconception. "I need a huge audience to run an A/B test." No — you need enough data to see a pattern. For a
small business, 50–100 clicks per version is enough to see a clear winner. You do not need thousands. You need enough to be
confident, and you need to change one variable at a time.
F.5 Reporting — saying what happened and what to change
The idea. A report is a short document that tells someone what happened, what it means, and what to do next. It is not a data
dump — it is a story with a point. A good report answers three questions: What happened? So what? Now what?
The one-page report structure:
Section What it says Example
1. What happened
The key numbers,
stated plainly
"This week we got 320 website visitors, 45 WhatsApp messages and 12 orders."
2. So what
What the numbers
mean
"Traffic is up 20% from last week. The Instagram content is working. But 86% of
visitors did not order — the ordering process may be confusing."
3. Now what
What you will
change
"Next week we will: (1) add a clearer 'Order on WhatsApp' button to the menu
page, (2) test a new ad image, and (3) send a WhatsApp broadcast to past
customers."
The "so what" rule: Every number in your report must be followed by a "so what." Not "we got 320 visitors" — but "we got 320
visitors, which is up 20% from last week, which means our Instagram content is working." The number alone is noise. The
number plus the meaning is signal.
Who is the report for?
For yourself: to decide what to do next.
For the business owner: to show them what their money is doing.
For a client: to prove your work is working.
Say to the class:
"A report is not a homework assignment. It is a tool. It tells you what is working, what is not, and what to change. If you
cannot write a one-page report about your marketing, you do not understand your marketing. The report forces you to
think: what happened, what does it mean, and what do I do now? That thinking is the skill."
Common misconception. "A report should include every number I can find." No — a report should include the numbers that
matter for the goal. If the goal is orders, the report includes orders, conversion rate and cost per order. It does not include
follower count, impressions or bounce rate unless they help explain the story. Less is more. One page. Three sections. Clear
language.
F.6 Honest measurement — the rules that protect your data
The idea. Measurement is only useful if it is honest. If you cherry-pick data, ignore uncomfortable results or claim causation
from correlation, you are not measuring — you are lying to yourself.
The four honest measurement rules:
1. No cherry-picking. Do not share only the numbers that make you look good. If your ad got 100 clicks but zero orders, that
is the result. Report it.
2. No claiming causation from correlation. If sales went up the same week you posted more, it does not mean the posts
caused the sales. Maybe it was payday. Maybe a competitor closed. Maybe the weather was bad and people ordered in.
Correlation is not causation. Be careful with your claims.
3. No ignoring uncomfortable results. If your favourite ad is losing money, turn it off. If your best-performing post is a
mistake you made, learn from it. The data does not care about your feelings.
4. No measuring what you cannot act on. If a number does not help you make a decision, do not track it. Track what you can
change.
Say to the class:
"Honest measurement is not just about ethics — it is about effectiveness. If you lie to yourself with your data, you make
bad decisions. You keep the ad that is losing money because you only looked at the clicks, not the orders. You post more of
the wrong content because you only looked at the likes, not the sales. Honest data leads to good decisions. Bad data leads
to wasted money."
F.7 Summary of the lecture
KPIs are numbers that tell you whether you are moving toward your goal. Choose one primary KPI per goal. Ignore vanity
metrics.
Google Analytics tells you five things: sessions, users, traffic sources, top pages and events. Read the story, not just the
numbers.
UTM links tell you exactly where every click came from. Use a consistent naming convention. Turn guessing into knowing.
A/B testing means changing one variable, measuring the result, and keeping the winner. One variable. One lesson.
Reporting answers three questions: what happened, so what, and now what. One page. Clear language.
Honest measurement means no cherry-picking, no false causation, no ignoring uncomfortable results.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi runs a small hair salon in Port Harcourt — Ngozi's Glam — offering braids, weaves and natural hair care. She
has been posting on Instagram for six months and has 1,200 followers. She recently set up a simple website with a menu and a
WhatsApp order button. She spent ₦10,000 on a Meta ad last week and wants to know if it worked. She comes to you with a
screenshot of her Instagram Insights and asks: "Did my ad work? I do not know what to look at."
Relevant facts.
Ngozi's Instagram ad ran for 7 days with a budget of ₦10,000.
The ad got 2,500 impressions, 180 clicks, and 12 likes.
Her website (which she did not tag with UTM links) got 400 sessions total last week.
She received 25 WhatsApp messages and booked 8 appointments from the ad.
Each appointment is worth ₦5,000 on average.
She does not know how many of the 400 website sessions came from the ad.
She is happy about the 12 likes on the ad.
Decision point. Ngozi wants to know: did the ₦10,000 ad work? What should she do next?
Possible approaches.
1. "The ad got 12 likes and 2,500 impressions — that is great! Let me run it again." — This focuses on vanity metrics.
Likes and impressions do not tell her if the ad made money.
2. "The ad got 180 clicks and 8 appointments. Each appointment is ₦5,000, so that is ₦40,000 in revenue. The ad
worked!" — This is better, but it assumes all 8 appointments came from the ad. Without UTM links, she cannot be sure.
3. "Let me calculate the real numbers: cost per click, cost per appointment, and return on ad spend. And let me set up
UTM links so I know exactly how many website visits came from the ad." — This is the right answer.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ngozi should:
1. Calculate the key metrics:
Cost per click: ₦10,000 ÷ 180 clicks = ₦55.56 per click.
Cost per appointment: ₦10,000 ÷ 8 appointments = ₦1,250 per appointment.
Revenue from appointments: 8 × ₦5,000 = ₦40,000.
Return on Ad Spend (ROAS): ₦40,000 ÷ ₦10,000 = 4x. (For every ₦1 spent, she got ₦4 back.)
2. Interpret the results: A ROAS of 4x is strong — the ad is profitable. But she cannot be sure all 8 appointments came from
the ad. Some might have come from her organic Instagram posts or word of mouth. She needs UTM links to know for
certain.
3. Set up UTM links for the next campaign: Tag the ad's destination URL with
utm_source=instagram&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=salon-promo . Now she will know exactly how many website visits came from
the ad.
4. Write a one-page report: What happened (180 clicks, 8 appointments, ₦40,000 revenue). So what (ROAS is 4x — the ad is
profitable, but she needs UTM links to be sure). Now what (run the ad again with UTM links, test a new image, and consider
increasing the budget slightly since ROAS is strong).
Say to the class:
"Ngozi's story is every small business owner's story. She spent money, got some results, but does not know exactly what
worked. The likes feel good, but they do not pay the bills. The 8 appointments are good — but are they from the ad?
Without UTM links, she is guessing. With UTM links, she knows. And with a simple ROAS calculation, she knows whether
the ad made money. That is the power of measurement. It turns 'I think it worked' into 'I know it worked — and here is the
proof.'"
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (focus on likes): feels good, tells her nothing, leads to wasted money.
Approach 2 (focus on appointments): better, but assumes all appointments came from the ad — risky without UTM links.
Approach 3 (calculate real metrics + set up UTM links): takes 10 minutes, gives her the truth, and sets up every future
campaign for success.
Professional connection. This is the difference between a business that guesses and a business that knows. The guessers keep
spending on ads that might be working. The knowers track every click, calculate their ROAS, and scale what works.
Measurement is not overhead — it is how you make money.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Tracking vanity metrics instead of actionable metrics
Symptoms: Learner reports follower count and likes as evidence of success. Likely cause: Vanity metrics feel good and are easy
to see. How to investigate: Ask: "If that number went up, what would you do differently?" Corrective steps: Replace vanity
metrics with actionable ones tied to the goal. Followers → conversion rate. Likes → click-through rate. How to confirm
resolved: The learner's primary KPI is tied to a business action (orders, messages, calls). Prevention: The KPI-goal matching
table in Section F.1 — use it for every goal.
K.2 Not using UTM links (or using them inconsistently)
Symptoms: All social media clicks show up as "Social" in Google Analytics — cannot tell which post or campaign brought the
visitor. Likely cause: Learner does not know about UTM links, or uses different naming each time. How to investigate: Look at
the traffic sources in GA4. Is everything just "Social" or "Direct"? Corrective steps: Build UTM links for every post, ad and
broadcast. Use a consistent naming convention (lowercase, hyphens). How to confirm resolved: GA4 shows specific sources
like "instagram" and "whatsapp" separately. Prevention: The UTM link builder demo in Section G.3 and the practical exercise.
K.3 Changing too many variables in an A/B test
Symptoms: Version B wins, but the learner does not know why. Likely cause: Changed the image, headline and CTA all at once.
How to investigate: Ask: "What is different between version A and version B?" If the answer is more than one thing, the test is
invalid. Corrective steps: Redesign the test with only one variable. Test the other variables in separate tests. How to confirm
resolved: The test has exactly one difference between A and B. Prevention: The A/B test golden rule in Section F.4 — one
variable, one lesson.
K.4 Writing a data dump instead of a report
Symptoms: The report is three pages of numbers with no meaning or recommendations. Likely cause: The learner does not
understand the purpose of a report. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the 'so what' of this report? What should the reader do
differently?" Corrective steps: Restructure the report: What happened (the numbers), So what (the meaning), Now what (the
actions). One page. How to confirm resolved: The report has three sections and fits on one page. Prevention: The one-page
report structure in Section F.5.
K.5 Cherry-picking data
Symptoms: The learner shares only the positive numbers and ignores the negative ones. Likely cause: Fear of bad results, or
wanting to look successful. How to investigate: Ask: "What about the numbers that are not good? What do they tell you?"
Corrective steps: Report all the relevant numbers — good and bad. The bad ones tell you what to fix. How to confirm
resolved: The report includes both strengths and weaknesses. Prevention: The honest measurement rules in Section F.6.
K.6 Claiming causation from correlation
Symptoms: "Sales went up because I posted more" — but other factors may have caused the increase. Likely cause: Assuming
that because two things happened at the same time, one caused the other. How to investigate: Ask: "What else changed that
week? Was it payday? Did a competitor close? Was the weather different?" Corrective steps: Use careful language: "Sales went
up the same week we posted more — this may be connected, but we cannot be certain without more data." How to confirm
resolved: The learner uses careful, honest language about causation. Prevention: The honest measurement rules in Section F.6.
K.7 Giving up on analytics because it is "too complicated"
Symptoms: Learner avoids looking at data, says "I am not a data person." Likely cause: Intimidation by the number of metrics
and features. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the one number you care about most for your business?" Corrective steps:
Start with one KPI. Just one. Watch it weekly. Add more later. Analytics is a skill, not a talent — it grows with practice. How to
confirm resolved: The learner is tracking at least one KPI and can explain what it tells them. Prevention: The "five things you
need to know" approach in Section F.2 — keep it simple.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it (e.g., the learner can show you
the exact KPI they are tracking, or the exact UTM link they built). Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

What happened: 200 visitors (up 33%), 30 WhatsApp messages (up 50%), 10 orders (up 43%), ₦15,000 revenue (up 43%).
So what: Everything is up significantly. The increase in WhatsApp messages (50%) is higher than the increase in visitors
(33%), which means the website is converting better — more people who visit are taking action. The 10 orders from 200
visitors is a 5% conversion rate — solid for a small business.
Now what: (1) Keep doing whatever drove the increase — was it a specific post or ad? (2) Test a new ad image to see if
we can improve the click-through rate. (3) Send a WhatsApp broadcast to past customers to encourage repeat orders.
Reflection sentence: "The most important thing I learned about measurement is that numbers tell a story — but only if you
know which numbers to read and you are honest about what they are saying."

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What is the difference between a vanity metric and an actionable metric? Give one example of each.
Q2. Multiple choice. Which of these is the best primary KPI for a business whose goal is to get more WhatsApp orders? a)
Follower count b) Number of WhatsApp messages received c) Number of likes on posts d) Total impressions
Q3. Explain. What are the five UTM parameters, and what does each one tell you?
Q4. Practical. A business owner says: "My ad got 500 likes and 2,000 impressions — it is working!" But she got zero orders.
What is the problem, and what should she measure instead?
Q5. A/B testing. You want to test whether a photo or a reel performs better in your Instagram ad. You change the image (photo
→ reel) AND the headline ("Too tired to cook?" → "Hot jollof in 45 minutes"). Version B wins. What is wrong with this test, and
what can you conclude?
Q6. Reporting. What are the three sections of a one-page report, and what does each section say?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q6)
A1. A vanity metric feels good but does not help you make decisions (e.g., followers, likes, impressions). An actionable metric
tells you something you can act on (e.g., click-through rate, conversion rate, cost per lead, orders). The key test: if the number
went up or down, would you do something differently? If yes, it is actionable. If no, it is vanity.
A2. (b) Number of WhatsApp messages received. This is the action that matches the goal. Follower count, likes and impressions
are vanity metrics — they do not tell you if people are ordering.
A3. The five UTM parameters are: (1) utm_source — where the click came from (e.g., instagram, facebook, whatsapp). (2)
utm_medium — what type of channel (e.g., social, email, paid). (3) utm_campaign — which campaign or post (e.g., week1-
launch, jollof-promo). (4) utm_content — which specific ad or creative (e.g., photo-jollof, reel-cooking). (5) utm_term —
which keyword (optional, for paid search).
A4. The problem is that she is measuring vanity metrics (likes, impressions) instead of actionable metrics (orders, conversion
rate, cost per order). Likes and impressions do not pay bills. She should measure: number of orders, conversion rate (orders ÷
clicks), and cost per order (ad spend ÷ orders). If she got zero orders from 2,000 impressions, the ad is not working — no
matter how many likes it got.
A5. The test changed two variables (image and headline), so you cannot tell which one caused the win. Was it the reel? The new
headline? Both? You cannot conclude anything from this test. The correct approach: change only one variable. Test the image
first (keep the headline the same). Then test the headline separately (keep the image the same). One variable. One lesson.
A6. The three sections are: (1) What happened — the key numbers, stated plainly. (2) So what — what the numbers mean;
what is working and what is not. (3) Now what — what you will change or do next. A good report answers all three in one page
with clear language.

## O. Instructor Evaluation Notes
<!-- kind:instructor_notes -->
Observable signs of genuine competence:
Learner chooses actionable KPIs over vanity metrics without being prompted.
Learner builds UTM links with correct parameters and consistent naming.
Learner plans an A/B test with exactly one variable.
Learner writes a report with a clear "so what" — not just a list of numbers.
Learner calculates ROAS correctly and interprets it honestly.
Learner uses careful language about causation ("may be connected" not "caused").
Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"Followers and likes prove the marketing is working." → No — only actions that match the goal prove it.
"I need to track every metric." → No — one primary KPI per goal. Track what you can act on.
"UTM links are optional." → No — without them, you cannot tell which post or campaign brought the visitor.
"If I change three things and it works, I know what worked." → No — you do not know which of the three caused it.
"A report should include every number." → No — one page, three sections, clear language.
"Correlation means causation." → No — be careful. Sales going up the same week you posted more does not prove the
posts caused it.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. KPI sorting drill: Give 10 metrics (some vanity, some actionable) and ask the learner to sort them into two columns and
explain why.
2. UTM building drill: Give five scenarios (Instagram post, Facebook ad, WhatsApp broadcast, email newsletter, Google ad)
and ask the learner to build a UTM link for each.
3. A/B test design drill: Give three poorly designed tests (each changing multiple variables) and ask the learner to fix them.
4. Report writing drill: Give a table of numbers and ask the learner to write a three-section report in 10 minutes.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide the KPI matching table with the goals already filled in — the learner writes only the KPI and
the explanation. Provide a UTM link template with the parameters listed — the learner fills in the values.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full KPI table, UTM links, A/B test plan and one-page report.
Level 3 (challenge): Ask the learner to design a two-week measurement plan for a ₦20,000 ad campaign, including: the
KPIs to track, the UTM links to use, an A/B test to run in week one, and a decision rule for week two based on hypothetical
results (e.g., "If ROAS is below 1x, what do you do? If it is above 3x, what do you do?"). This requires applying the full test-
learn-adjust cycle.
