# Finding Your First Customers and Selling

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Win and Price Clients

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with selling)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 4 costed price list and quote. Learners must be willing to speak and role-play in class. No prior sales experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 4 you learned to cost and price your work — you know what to charge and can defend it. Today we get you customers: how to find them, how to pitch, how to sell, and how to follow up. A price without a customer is just a number.

**Preparation before class:** Print the 60-second pitch template (Section F.3); print the sales conversation steps (Section F.5); print the ten-name pipeline template (Section H); prepare scenario cards for the role-play (price objection, "I will think about it", a busy customer); have the FastFix case pack ready; arrange the room for pair role-play. Instructor note on tone: Many learners are afraid of selling — they think it means being pushy or begging. Reframe it: selling is helping someone solve a problem they already have. If your offer is genuine, selling is service. Keep the mood encouraging and practical — everyone will practise, no one will be mocked.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain why most customers come from nearby — referrals, networking, word of mouth — and why these are cheaper
than advertising.
2. Ask for referrals without begging, using a simple script.
3. Build a 60-second elevator pitch: who you serve, the problem, what you deliver, and what happens next.
4. Plan three referral/networking actions for the next week.
5. Role-play a full sales conversation: approach, ask, listen, propose, handle a price objection, agree a clear next step, and
follow up.
6. Explain why most sales come on the second or third contact, and why silence is not always rejection.
7. Produce a written 60-second pitch script and a ten-name pipeline with planned approaches and follow-up dates (LO7).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two "customer-finding stories" without labelling them.
Story A: "Chidi from FastFix waits for customers to come to his shop. He has a signboard, but it is old and faded. He does
not ask happy customers for referrals. He does not talk to other shop owners. He posts on his personal Facebook page once
a month. He wonders why business is slow."
Story B: "Adaeze from Adaeze Fashion asks every happy customer: 'Do you know one other person who might need a dress
like this?' She gives them a small discount on their next order for every referral. She talks to other business owners at the
market and refers customers to them too. She posts on WhatsApp status every week with a clear photo and price. She has
more orders than she can handle."
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses. Hands up — which one has more customers? … Story B, almost always. Why? Because Adaeze goes to
the customer — she asks, she networks, she follows up. Chidi waits. Today we learn to be Adaeze. You will learn how to
find customers nearby, how to pitch in 60 seconds, how to sell without being pushy, and how to follow up. Selling is a skill
— and you will learn it."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B asks for referrals", "B is active", "A is waiting", "B helps others". Accept all
answers and draw out the difference: active vs passive.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you are afraid of selling — you think it means begging or being pushy?" Many
hands go up. Say:
"Good — that means you have good character. Selling is not begging. Selling is helping someone solve a problem they
already have. If your offer is genuine, the customer needs you as much as you need them. Today you learn to connect the
two."
Why this opener works: It uses the running example and a contrasting example, it isolates proactivity as the variable, and it
reframes selling as service — which lowers the fear barrier.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the eight components of the full cost of a job?"
Expected answer: Materials, data, transport, tools, packaging, overhead share, waste allowance, your time.
Common gap: learners remember four or five. Clarify: "The eight components are your checklist — use it every time you
cost a job."
Question 2: "What are the seven parts of a clear quote?"
Expected answer: Business name, date/number, customer, description, quantity/unit price, total, terms (validity, payment,
exclusions).
Common gap: learners remember the price but not the terms. Clarify: "The terms are what prevent disputes — never skip
them."
Question 3: "What is break-even?"
Expected answer: The point where revenue covers cost — the minimum you must sell to survive.
Common gap: learners confuse it with profit. Clarify: "Break-even is where you stop losing money; profit starts after."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You can cost, price and quote. Now you need customers. The best customers are not found through adverts — they are
found through people. Referrals, networking, word of mouth — these are free and they work. Today you learn to use them,
and you learn to sell when the customer is in front of you."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the eight cost components, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the board
before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Where customers come from — the nearby principle
The idea. Most small business customers come from nearby — from people who know you, trust you, or are one introduction
away. This is not a limitation — it is an advantage. Nearby customers are cheaper to reach, easier to trust, and more likely to
return.
The four nearby sources — teach them one by one:
1. Referrals: Happy customers tell others. This is the cheapest and most trusted source — a recommendation from a friend is
worth more than any advert.
2. Networking: Meeting people who can send customers to you — other business owners, community leaders, church
groups, market associations, WhatsApp groups.
3. Word of mouth: What people say about you when you are not in the room. Good service creates word of mouth; bad
service creates the opposite.
4. Showing up where the customer already is: Being present in the places your customer goes — the market, the church,
the school, the WhatsApp group — so they can find you.
Why nearby beats advertising:
Cost: Referrals and networking are free; adverts cost money.
Trust: A recommendation from a friend is trusted; an advert is doubted.
Speed: A referral can become a customer today; an advert takes weeks to work.
Fit: Nearby customers are in your area, so you can serve them well.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. The trader who knows her regular customers by name, asks about their families,
and tells them when fresh stock arrives — that trader has customers for life. The trader who sits silently and waits — that
trader hopes. The difference is not luck; it is relationship.
Important limitation / misconception. "I need social media adverts to get customers." No — adverts can help, but they are
not the foundation. A business with no customers and no money should start with referrals and networking — free and fast.
Adverts come later, when you have the money and the offer is proven.
How a learner recognises a nearby customer: They can name at least ten people who fit their customer description — and
they know where those people are.
F.2 Asking for referrals — without begging
The idea. Most happy customers would refer you — if you asked. But most business owners never ask, because they feel it is
begging. It is not. Asking for a referral is a service: you are connecting a friend to something they need.
The referral ask — a simple script:
1. Ask for feedback first: "Are you happy with the work I did for you?"
2. If yes, ask for the referral: "Do you know one other person who might need something like this?"
3. Make it easy: "Could you send them my number? Or could I call you when I am near them?"
4. Offer a thank-you: "Thank you — as a thank-you, I will give you 10% off your next order."
Why this works:
You ask only when the customer is happy — so the referral is genuine.
You make it easy — one person, one action.
You give a thank-you — so the customer feels appreciated, not used.
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor. A doctor does not beg for patients. A doctor asks a happy patient: "Do you know
someone else with this problem?" The patient refers a friend — because the doctor helped them. The referral is a compliment,
not a favour.
Misconception to correct. "Asking for referrals is begging." No — it is a normal part of business. A customer who is happy
wants to help you. Asking gives them the chance.
Say this to the class:
"The most dangerous phrase in business is 'I do not want to bother people.' You are not bothering them — you are
offering them a solution. A referral is a gift you give a friend: the gift of a good service. Ask with confidence."
F.3 The 60-second elevator pitch — who, problem, deliverable, next step
The idea. An elevator pitch is a short, clear description of your business that you can deliver in 60 seconds — the time it takes
to ride in an elevator with a potential customer. It is not a sales pitch; it is a conversation starter.
The four parts — teach them one by one:
1. Who you serve: A specific customer in a specific place.
FastFix example: "I help office workers and students around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi."
2. The problem: What bothers them.
FastFix example: "whose phone screen or battery fails during the workday."
3. What you deliver: The solution and the outcome.
FastFix example: "I repair screens and batteries the same day, with a 30-day warranty."
4. What happens next: A clear, easy next step.
FastFix example: "Call or WhatsApp me, and I will tell you if I can fix your phone — free diagnosis."
The full 60-second pitch for FastFix (read it aloud):
"I help office workers and students around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi whose phone screen or battery fails during the
workday. I repair screens and batteries the same day, with a 30-day warranty. If your phone is giving you trouble, call or
WhatsApp me — I will tell you if I can fix it, free. My name is Chidi, and I am at Mile 3 Market."
Why 60 seconds? Because attention is short. If you cannot explain your business in 60 seconds, the customer will not
understand it — and they will not buy. The pitch is not about saying everything; it is about saying enough to start a
conversation.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who says "I sell phones" gets ignored. A trader who says "I fix your
phone screen today, with a warranty — bring it now" gets a customer. The difference is not the product — it is the pitch.
Misconception to correct. "A pitch is a speech." No — a pitch is a conversation starter. You deliver it, then you stop and listen.
The customer's response tells you what to say next.
F.4 Networking and word of mouth — value first, with manners
The idea. Networking is meeting people who can send customers to you — and sending customers to them. Word of mouth is
what people say about you when you are not in the room. Both are built the same way: value first, with manners.
The networking rules — teach them one by one:
1. Go where the customer is: Market associations, church groups, business groups, WhatsApp groups, community meetings.
2. Give before you ask: Help someone, share useful information, refer a customer to another business — before you ask for
anything.
3. Be consistent: Show up regularly. A person who appears once is a stranger; a person who appears monthly is a contact.
4. Be specific about what you do: "I repair phones" is vague. "I repair phone screens and batteries with a 30-day warranty" is
specific — people can refer you.
5. Follow up: After meeting someone, send a message: "Nice to meet you at the market meeting. I repair phones — if you
ever need one fixed, call me."
Word of mouth — how to build it:
Deliver excellent service: The foundation of word of mouth is a happy customer.
Ask for feedback: "Are you happy with the work?" If yes, ask for a referral. If no, fix it.
Make it easy to talk about you: A clear name, a clear offer, a clear price — so people can describe you accurately.
Say thank you: A customer who refers someone deserves a thank-you — a discount, a small gift, or a sincere message.
Real-life comparison. Think of a church. A church member who helps in the kitchen, greets newcomers, and stays after the
service to talk — that member has friends and influence. A member who arrives late, sits alone, and leaves immediately —
that member is invisible. Networking is the same: show up, help, stay.
Misconception to correct. "Networking is using people." No — networking is building relationships. A network built on use
collapses; a network built on value grows.
F.5 The sales conversation — seven steps from approach to follow-up
The idea. Selling is not a single moment — it is a conversation with seven steps. Most beginners skip steps and wonder why
the customer says no. Learn the steps, and the conversation flows.
The seven steps — teach them one by one:
1. Approach: Start the conversation warmly. "Hello, I am [name]. I help [customer type] with [problem]."
2. Ask: Use open questions to understand the customer's situation. "Tell me about your phone — what is wrong with it?"
3. Listen: Listen more than you talk. The customer will tell you what they need — if you let them.
4. Propose: Offer your solution based on what you heard. "I can replace your screen today with a quality part and a 30-day
warranty. It will cost ₦6,500."
5. Handle objections: When the customer says "it is too expensive" or "I will think about it", respond with value — not
pressure. "I understand. The price includes a quality part and a 30-day warranty — if the screen fails again, I fix it free.
Would that give you peace of mind?"
6. Agree a next step: Never end with "okay, call me." Agree a specific action. "So I will order the part tomorrow and text you
when it arrives — is that okay?"
7. Follow up: Most sales are lost here. Contact the customer after the agreed time. "Hello, the part arrived — can you bring
your phone tomorrow?"
Why most sales come on the second or third contact: The first contact builds awareness. The second builds trust. The third
closes the sale. Most beginners give up after the first contact — and lose the sale to someone who followed up.
Real-life comparison. Think of a farmer. A farmer does not plant and harvest the same day. They plant (approach), water (ask
and listen), nurture (propose and handle objections), and harvest (agree and follow up). Selling is the same — it is a process,
not a moment.
Misconception to correct. "Silence means rejection." No — silence often means "I am thinking" or "I am busy." A customer
who does not answer today may answer next week. Follow up — politely and persistently.
F.6 WhatsApp and word of mouth — selling without a shop
The idea. In Nigeria, WhatsApp is not just a messaging app — it is a business tool. Many small businesses find and serve
customers entirely through WhatsApp. Learn to use it well.
The WhatsApp selling rules:
1. Use WhatsApp Business (we set it up in Session 8) — it has a profile, catalogue, and labels.
2. Post on status regularly: A clear photo, a clear price, and a clear call to action — "Call or WhatsApp to order."
3. Reply fast: A customer who waits hours for a reply will go to someone else. Reply within minutes, even if it is "I will get
back to you in one hour."
4. Use labels: Label customers as "new", "quoted", "paid", "completed" — so you can follow up.
5. Send a catalogue: When a customer asks "what do you do?", send a clear catalogue — not a long voice note.
6. Follow up with a message: After a job, send a message: "I hope you are happy with the repair. If you know anyone else who
needs a phone fixed, I will appreciate the referral."
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader with a loudspeaker. The trader who announces her products clearly, answers
questions quickly, and follows up with regular customers — that trader sells more. WhatsApp is the digital loudspeaker.
Misconception to correct. "WhatsApp is for friends, not business." No — WhatsApp is where your customers are. A business
that ignores WhatsApp is invisible to its own market.
F.7 Summary of the session
Most customers come from nearby — referrals, networking, word of mouth, showing up where the customer is.
Ask for referrals without begging — ask when the customer is happy, make it easy, say thank you.
The 60-second pitch has four parts: who, problem, deliverable, next step.
Networking is value first, with manners — give before you ask, be consistent, follow up.
The sales conversation has seven steps: approach, ask, listen, propose, handle objections, agree a next step, follow up.
Most sales come on the second or third contact — follow up.
WhatsApp is a business tool — reply fast, use labels, follow up.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Show the class how to deliver a 60-second pitch and run a sales conversation — live — so they can do it themselves.
Setup: Open a blank Google Doc titled "60-Second Pitch — FastFix". Have the sales conversation steps on the projector. Invite
a volunteer to play a customer.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A blank pitch template on the projector.
The sales conversation steps on the projector.
A volunteer briefed to play a customer with a phone problem.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the 60-second pitch (3 minutes)
Say: "I will deliver the FastFix pitch in 60 seconds. Watch the four parts."
Deliver the pitch aloud, timing it:
"I help office workers and students around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi whose phone screen or battery fails during the
workday. I repair screens and batteries the same day, with a 30-day warranty. If your phone is giving you trouble, call or
WhatsApp me — I will tell you if I can fix it, free. My name is Chidi, and I am at Mile 3 Market."
Say: "Four parts: who (office workers and students), problem (screen or battery fails), deliverable (same-day repair with
warranty), next step (call or WhatsApp for a free diagnosis). Sixty seconds. Clear. Easy to remember."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — the sales conversation (8 minutes)
Say: "Now the sales conversation. I will play the seller; my volunteer will play a customer with a broken phone. Watch the seven
steps."
Step 1 — Approach: "Hello, I am Chidi from FastFix. I help people with phone repairs. What seems to be the problem with
your phone?"
Step 2 — Ask: "Tell me about it — when did it start? What happened?"
Volunteer (customer): "My screen cracked yesterday when I dropped it. I need it fixed, but I am worried about the cost."
Step 3 — Listen: (Nod, do not interrupt.) "I understand — a cracked screen is stressful, especially when you need your phone
for work."
Step 4 — Propose: "I can replace your screen today with a quality part and a 30-day warranty. It will cost ₦6,500. I can have it
ready by 5 p.m."
Step 5 — Handle objection: "That is more than I expected."
Instructor: "I understand. The price includes a quality part and a 30-day warranty — if the screen fails again within 30 days, I fix
it free. Would that give you peace of mind?"
Volunteer: "Okay, that sounds fair."
Step 6 — Agree a next step: "So I will order the part tomorrow morning and text you when it arrives. Can you bring your
phone tomorrow afternoon?"
Volunteer: "Yes, that works."
Step 7 — Follow up: "Thank you. I will text you tomorrow when the part arrives. If you have any questions before then, call
me."
Say to the class: "Seven steps. Approach, ask, listen, propose, handle the objection, agree a next step, follow up. Notice what I
did not do: I did not pressure. I did not beg. I asked, listened, proposed, and agreed a clear next step. That is selling."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — the referral ask (2 minutes)
Say: "After the job is done and the customer is happy, I ask for a referral."
Instructor (to volunteer): "I hope you are happy with the repair. Do you know one other person who might need a phone
fixed?"
Volunteer: "Yes, my colleague has a battery problem."
Instructor: "Could you send them my number? And as a thank-you, I will give you 10% off your next repair."
Say: "Ask when the customer is happy. Make it easy — one person. Say thank you. That is the referral ask."
G.5 Questions to ask during the demo
"What are the four parts of the pitch?" (Who, problem, deliverable, next step.)
"What are the seven steps of the sales conversation?" (Approach, ask, listen, propose, handle objection, agree next step,
follow up.)
"What did I do when the customer said 'that is more than I expected'?" (I acknowledged the concern, then offered value —
the warranty — not pressure.)
"Why is follow-up important?" (Most sales come on the second or third contact — follow-up is where the sale is won.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes a 60-second pitch, practises it with a partner, and role-plays a sales
conversation.
Starting materials: A pen and notebook or phone notes app; the pitch template and sales conversation steps (projected and
printed).
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your name and today's date at the top of your workbook or notes app.
2. Write your 60-second pitch: Use the template (Worksheet 5.1) — who, problem, deliverable, next step. (Worksheet 5.1.)
3. Practise your pitch: Stand up and deliver your pitch to your partner. Time it — 60 seconds or less.
4. Partner feedback: Your partner says one thing that is clear and one question they have.
5. Revise your pitch based on the feedback.
6. Role-play the sales conversation: You play the seller; your partner plays a customer with a problem. Use the seven steps
(Worksheet 5.3). Your partner uses one of the scenario cards (price objection, "I will think about it", busy customer, curious
customer).
7. Swap roles: Your partner now plays the seller; you play the customer.
8. Debrief: Tell your partner one thing they did well and one thing to improve.
9. Write your referral ask: Write your own referral ask script (Worksheet 5.2) — based on the model in Section F.2.
Expected result: A written 60-second pitch, a practised sales conversation, and a referral ask script.
Points to pause on and predict:
After step 3: "Was your pitch 60 seconds or less? Was it clear?"
After step 6: "Which step was hardest? Why?"
After step 8: "What did your partner do well? What could they improve?"
Troubleshooting while walking the room:
Learner's pitch is too long → Ask: "What is the one thing the customer needs to know? Cut everything else."
Learner's pitch is vague → Ask: "Who is the customer? What is the problem? What do they get?"
Learner skips steps in the role-play → Stop them. "You jumped from approach to propose. Go back — ask and listen first."
Learner pressures the customer → Remind them: "Selling is helping, not pressuring. Acknowledge the concern, then offer
value."
Learner does not follow up → Ask: "What is the next step? When will you contact them?"
Completion checklist (learner ticks each):
60-second pitch written with all four parts
Pitch delivered in 60 seconds or less
Pitch revised based on partner feedback
Sales conversation role-played with all seven steps
Referral ask script written
Debrief completed with partner

## I. INDEPENDENT CLASS EXERCISE (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:exercise -->
Task (new task, done alone): Write a ten-name pipeline — ten named potential customers with a planned approach and
follow-up date for each.
Scenario. You have a business idea and a 60-second pitch. Now you need to find your first ten customers. These are not
strangers — they are people you know or can reach through your network: family, friends, classmates, church members,
market contacts, WhatsApp groups, other business owners.
Requirements:
1. Write down ten names — real people you know or can reach. (If you cannot think of ten, write five and explain how you
will find five more.)
2. For each person, write:
Name
How you know them (family, church, school, market, WhatsApp group)
Planned approach (how you will contact them — call, WhatsApp, visit, message)
Follow-up date (when you will contact them again if they do not respond)
3. Write one sentence: "My first three customers will most likely come from ______, because ______."

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Kelechi is a barber in Port Harcourt. He works in a small shop near a busy junction. He is good at his work — his cuts
are clean and his customers are happy. But he has the same problem every month: not enough customers. He sits in his shop
and waits. He does not ask for referrals. He does not talk to other business owners. He posts on his personal Facebook page
once a month — a photo of a haircut with the caption "Fresh cut." He wonders why he is not busier.
Relevant facts.
Kelechi cuts 10–15 heads a week.
His shop is near a market, a church, and a bus stop — lots of foot traffic.
He has been cutting hair for three years — he is skilled.
He charges ₦1,500 per cut — a fair price.
His customers are happy — but he never asks them for referrals.
Decision point. What is Kelechi's real problem?
Possible approaches.
1. Lower his prices — "maybe I am too expensive."
2. Move to a bigger shop — "maybe my location is the problem."
3. Become active: ask for referrals, network with nearby businesses, post on WhatsApp status regularly, follow up with
customers.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Kelechi's problem is not his skill, his price, or his location — it is that he is passive. He
waits for customers instead of going to them. A barber who asks every happy customer "Do you know one other person who
needs a haircut?" and offers a small discount for referrals — that barber doubles his customers without spending a naira.
Reasoning / example actions for Kelechi:
Referral ask: After every cut, ask: "Are you happy with your haircut? Do you know one other person who needs a cut? Send
them my number — and I will give you ₦200 off your next cut."
WhatsApp status: Post a photo of a fresh cut every week — with a clear price and a clear call to action: "Fresh cut —
₦1,500. Call or WhatsApp to book."
Networking: Talk to the tailor next door, the phone accessories seller, and the food vendor — refer customers to them,
and ask them to refer to you.
Follow up: Keep a list of customers and text them every 3–4 weeks: "It has been a while — time for a fresh cut? Call me."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (lower prices) reduces his income without increasing customers — he will be busier and poorer.
Approach 2 (move to a bigger shop) costs money and does not solve the real problem — a bigger shop with the same
passivity will still be empty.
Approach 3 (become active) costs nothing but effort — and it solves the real problem.
Professional connection. The difference between a busy barber and an empty barber is not talent — it is customer
relationship management. A barber who knows his customers by name, asks about their families, and texts them when it is
time for a cut — that barber has customers for life. Selling is not a single skill — it is a set of habits.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Waiting for customers
Symptoms: The learner sits and waits; business is slow; they wonder why. Likely cause: They are passive — they do not ask,
network, or follow up. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you do this week to find customers? Did you ask for referrals? Did
you follow up?" Corrective steps: Become active — ask for referrals, network, post on WhatsApp, follow up. How to confirm
resolved: The learner can name three actions they took this week to find customers. Prevention: Customers do not come to
you — you go to them.
K.2 Pitching instead of asking
Symptoms: The learner talks about their business non-stop; the customer looks uncomfortable. Likely cause: They are pitching
instead of having a conversation. How to investigate: Ask: "What questions did you ask? What did the customer say?"
Corrective steps: Use the seven steps — approach, ask, listen, propose. Ask more than you talk. How to confirm resolved:
The learner asks open questions and listens. Prevention: Selling is a conversation, not a speech.
K.3 Pressuring the customer
Symptoms: The learner pushes the customer to buy; the customer feels uncomfortable and leaves. Likely cause: They are
desperate, not helpful. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you say when the customer hesitated?" Corrective steps:
Acknowledge the concern, offer value, and give the customer space. "I understand — take your time. Here is my number if you
have questions." How to confirm resolved: The learner handles objections with value, not pressure. Prevention: Selling is
helping, not pressuring.
K.4 No follow-up
Symptoms: The learner meets a potential customer, gives their number, and never hears from them again. Likely cause: They
did not follow up — they waited for the customer to call. How to investigate: Ask: "Did you agree a next step? Did you follow
up?" Corrective steps: Agree a specific next step and follow up on the agreed date. How to confirm resolved: The learner can
state when they will follow up and with whom. Prevention: Most sales come on the second or third contact — follow up.
K.5 A vague pitch
Symptoms: The learner says "I do hair" or "I sell phones" — and the customer does not understand. Likely cause: The pitch has
no structure — no who, problem, deliverable, next step. How to investigate: Ask: "What is your 60-second pitch? Can you
deliver it now?" Corrective steps: Use the four parts — who, problem, deliverable, next step. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can deliver a clear 60-second pitch. Prevention: The pitch is a structure — use it every time.
K.6 Not asking for referrals
Symptoms: The learner has happy customers but never gets referrals. Likely cause: They feel it is begging — so they never ask.
How to investigate: Ask: "Have you asked any happy customer for a referral?" Corrective steps: Use the referral ask script —
ask when the customer is happy, make it easy, say thank you. How to confirm resolved: The learner has asked at least one
customer for a referral. Prevention: A referral is a gift you give a friend — ask with confidence.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

"A pitch is a speech." → A pitch is a conversation starter — deliver it, then listen.

Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Pitch timing drill: ask the learner to deliver their pitch — time it. If over 60 seconds, cut until it fits.
2. Referral ask practice: role-play a happy customer and ask the learner to make the referral ask — check for the three parts

3. Sales conversation step sorting: give seven shuffled steps and ask the learner to put them in order.
4. Pipeline building: help the learner list ten names — start with family, friends, church, school — and add a follow-up date
for each.

Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed pitch template (who and problem already filled); learner writes the

Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write a follow-up message for a customer who has not responded in two weeks —
polite, specific, and with a clear next step. Also ask them to plan a referral incentive (e.g., a discount or a small gift) and
explain why it works.
