# Capstone: Your Business and Freelance Plan

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Run and Grow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (assembly and presentation, not new theory)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** Sessions 1–8 completed: offer sheet, validation notes, money rules, costed price list, pitch script, client agreement, sample invoice, tracker, WhatsApp Business profile, seven-day marketing plan. Learners must bring their working folder (Google Drive or printed pack).

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 8 you built your free digital presence — WhatsApp Business profile, Google Business Profile steps, and a seven-day marketing plan. Today you stop collecting pieces and assemble the machine: every document from Sessions 1–8 becomes one page of decisions, one financial snapshot, and one spoken pitch. This is the session the whole course has been building toward.

**Preparation before class:** Print the one-page plan canvas (Section F.4) for each learner; print the capstone checklist (Section H); prepare the projector with the FastFix finished plan as a model; have the FastFix case pack ready; bring a timer for pitch rehearsals; prepare a simple peer-feedback form (two stars and a wish).

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Assemble a one-page business and freelance plan from the artefacts produced in Sessions 1–8, with every section filled
and internally consistent.
2. Build a simple financial snapshot — launch costs, monthly fixed costs, unit price, break-even and a first-month revenue
target — with correct arithmetic in naira.
3. Deliver a 60-second pitch and a 3–4 minute talk that explains the plan, then defend key decisions (price, validation,
break-even, first seven days) under questioning.
4. Check internal consistency across the plan, price list and financial snapshot so the numbers match everywhere they
appear.
5. Give and receive structured peer feedback using a simple two-stars-and-a-wish format.
6. State honest limits — three things the plan has not yet proven and what will be done next to test them.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two versions of the same business idea side by side. Do not label them yet.
Version A (scattered): A desk with eight separate papers — an offer sheet, a price list, a pitch script, an agreement, an
invoice, a tracker, a WhatsApp screenshot, a marketing plan. Each paper is good on its own. Nothing connects them.
Version B (assembled): The same eight documents, but now arranged around a single one-page plan in the centre. Arrows
show how each document feeds one section of the plan. The financial snapshot sits below the plan. The pitch script sits to
the right.
What the instructor says:
"Version A is where most people stop. They have good pieces — a price list here, a pitch there — but the pieces do not
talk to each other. A customer asks 'how much?' and the answer is on one paper. A mentor asks 'how will you find
customers?' and the answer is on another. Version B is what you are building today: one page that holds the whole
business, with every piece in its place. By the end of this session, you will have Version B for your own business."
Ask for a show of hands: "Which version would you show to a mentor, a bank, or a customer?" The class will choose Version B.
Then reveal the hook: "The difference between A and B is not more work — it is assembly. You already did the work in
Sessions 1–8. Today you put it together, you check that the numbers match, and you learn to explain it out loud. That is what
a real business plan is: not a 40-page document nobody reads, but one page of decisions you can defend."
Why this opener works: It shows the destination before the journey, uses the learners' own artefacts (not a stranger's example),
and frames the session as assembly rather than new learning — which reduces anxiety and focuses attention on the task.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. Point to the relevant section of the Version B diagram as
each answer comes.
Question 1: "What are the seven sections of the one-page plan canvas?"
Expected answer: Who we serve / The problem / What we sell / How we price / How customers find us / What it costs
us / First-month target and next 30 days.
Common gap: learners name the documents (price list, invoice) instead of the plan sections. Clarify: the canvas sections are
decisions, not documents. The documents are the evidence behind each decision.
Question 2: "What is break-even, and how do you calculate it?"
Expected answer: Fixed costs ÷ (unit price − variable cost per unit) = number of units to break even. Or in service
terms: fixed costs ÷ profit per job = number of jobs to break even.
Common gap: learners confuse break-even with profit. Clarify: break-even is the point where total revenue equals total
cost — you have not lost money, but you have not made profit yet. Every sale after break-even is profit.
Question 3: "What are the four parts of a 60-second pitch?"
Expected answer: Who you serve / the problem they have / what you do about it / what happens next (the ask or the
next step).
Common gap: learners describe the pitch as "introducing yourself." Clarify: the pitch is not a biography — it is a problem-
solution-ask in sixty seconds.
Question 4: "What three things must every invoice contain?"
Expected answer: Business name and contact / description of the job or item / amount in naira with payment details
and terms. (Also acceptable: date, invoice number, payment terms.)
Common gap: learners forget payment terms. Clarify: an invoice without payment terms is a request, not a business
document — the customer needs to know how and when to pay.
Question 5: "What is the honest-limits note, and why does the capstone require it?"
Expected answer: Three things the plan has not yet proven, and what you will do next to test them. It is required
because a plan that claims to know everything is a plan that cannot be trusted. Honesty about limits is a sign of a real
businessperson, not a weakness.
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have spent eight sessions building the pieces. You have an offer, a price, a pitch, an agreement, an invoice, a tracker,
a profile and a marketing plan. Today you put them on one page, you check that the numbers agree with each other, and
you stand up and explain your business to the room. That is what business owners do — not once, but every time someone
asks 'what do you do?' or 'how much do you charge?' or 'how will this work?' Let us build."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the seven canvas sections, spend 3 extra minutes writing them on the board with
one-line descriptions before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a one-page business plan is
The idea. A one-page business plan is a single sheet — physical or digital — that holds the most important decisions about a
business in one place. It is not a summary of a longer plan; it is the plan. Every section is a decision you have made, written
down clearly enough that a stranger can read it and understand the business in two minutes.
Plain-English definition. A one-page business plan is your business on one sheet of paper: who you serve, what problem you
solve, what you sell, how you price it, how customers find you, what it costs you, and what you will do in the first month. If a
section is blank, the plan is not finished — it is a to-do list.
Why one page. A 40-page plan is written for a bank or an investor, and most small businesses never need one. A one-page plan
is written for you — the owner — so you always know what you decided and why. It is also the document you show a mentor,
a partner, or a customer who asks "how does this work?" One page forces you to make decisions. A blank space on a one-page
plan is a decision you have avoided.
What it is not. It is not a certificate, a licence, or a guarantee of success. It is a thinking tool — a way of checking that your
offer, your price, your costs and your marketing all point in the same direction. If they do not, the one-page plan shows you
where the mismatch is.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader in Port Harcourt who has been selling for ten years. She has no written plan,
but she has a plan in her head: "I sell rice to the women in this market. I buy from the same supplier every Monday. I charge
₦2,000 more than the shop next door because I sell by the cup, not the bag. I restock when I have ₦50,000 in the box." That is a
one-page plan — it just lives in her memory instead of on paper. Writing it down means you can check it, change it, and show
it to someone else.
How a learner recognises a finished plan. Read the page and ask: Could a stranger understand the business in two minutes?
Does every section have a real answer (not "TBD")? Do the numbers in the price list match the numbers in the financial
snapshot? If yes to all three, the plan is finished.

Section 1 — Who we serve. This is the customer you chose in Session 2 and validated in the same session. It must be specific:
"phone owners in Diobu and Trans-Amadi who need same-day repair" — not "everyone with a phone." If the learner validated
with three customer conversations, the answer to "who we serve" is the person who actually said "yes, I have this problem and I
would pay for it."
Section 2 — The problem. One sentence, in the customer's own words if possible. "My phone screen breaks and I cannot
afford to replace the phone" is a real problem. "People need phones" is not a problem — it is a fact. The problem sentence
should make the reader feel the pain.
Section 3 — What we sell. The deliverable, not the skill. "Phone screen replacement with a 30-day warranty" is a deliverable.
"Phone repair" is a skill. The deliverable includes what the customer receives, how long it takes, and what guarantee comes with
it.
Section 4 — How we price. The pricing model (per job, per hour, or package) and the price range from the costed price list.
This section should name the model and give the starting price — not the whole price list, which lives in the supporting pack.
Section 5 — How customers find us. The primary channel (WhatsApp Business, word of mouth, Google Business Profile, or
one social platform) and the first action the learner will take. "Post the catalogue on WhatsApp Business and send a broadcast to
20 contacts" is an action. "Use social media" is not.
Section 6 — What it costs us. The financial snapshot in miniature: launch costs, monthly fixed costs, and the break-even
number. The full snapshot lives in the supporting pack; the plan holds the headline numbers.
Section 7 — First-month target and next 30 days. A revenue target in naira (not "as much as possible") and a short list of
actions for the first seven days. The target should be realistic for a Port Harcourt beginner — not a wish. The actions should be
specific: "Day 1: set up WhatsApp Business catalogue. Day 2: message 20 contacts. Day 3: post first status."
Common misconception. Learners think the plan is a creative writing exercise. It is not. Every sentence in the plan is a decision
— something you have chosen and will act on. If a sentence could be removed without changing what you do on Monday
morning, it does not belong on the plan.
F.3 The financial snapshot — the numbers that must agree
The idea. The financial snapshot is a small Google Sheet that holds the money side of the plan in one place: what it costs to
launch, what it costs to run each month, what you charge, how many jobs or sales you need to break even, and what you aim to
earn in the first month. It is the section most learners avoid — and the section that most often reveals a problem.
Why it matters. A plan without numbers is a story. A plan with numbers is a business. The financial snapshot is where you
discover that your price is too low, your break-even is too high, or your first-month target is fantasy. Better to discover it on
paper than in the market.
The five numbers every snapshot must hold:
Number What it is How to get it
Launch costs One-time costs to start — tools, registration, initial
stock, branding
Add up every one-time cost from the
costed price list and the offer sheet
Monthly fixed costs Costs that happen every month whether you sell or not
— rent share, data, transport, phone credit
Add up the monthly overhead from the
costed price list
Unit price What you charge for one job or one item From the costed price list
Variable cost per unit What one job or one item costs you — materials, data,
transport per job
From the costed price list
Break-even How many jobs or sales you need to cover fixed costs Fixed costs ÷ (unit price − variable cost
per unit)
Worked example — FastFix Phone Repair:
Launch costs: ₦15,000 (basic tools ₦8,000, CAC business name registration — verify current fee, initial stock of screens
and chargers ₦5,000, Canva logo and flyer ₦0 free tier).
Monthly fixed costs: ₦12,000 (market stall share ₦5,000, data and phone credit ₦3,000, transport ₦2,500, miscellaneous
₦1,500).
Unit price (screen replacement): ₦8,000.
Variable cost per screen replacement: ₦4,500 (screen ₦3,500, adhesive and materials ₦500, transport to market ₦500).
Break-even: ₦12,000 ÷ (₦8,000 − ₦4,500) = ₦12,000 ÷ ₦3,500 = 3.43 → 4 screen replacements per month to break
even.
Say this to the class: "Four screen replacements. That is one per week. If Chidi does one screen replacement a week, he covers
his fixed costs. Every screen after that is profit. That is the whole business in one number."
First-month revenue target. This should be a realistic number based on the learner's own market. For FastFix: 10 screen
replacements at ₦8,000 = ₦80,000 revenue, minus variable costs (10 × ₦4,500 = ₦45,000) = ₦35,000 gross profit, minus
fixed costs ₦12,000 = ₦23,000 net for the month. That is a realistic first month for a busy market stall — not a fantasy.
The consistency check. The numbers in the financial snapshot must match the numbers in the costed price list, which must
match the numbers in the plan. If the price list says ₦8,000 and the snapshot says ₦6,000, the plan is broken. The instructor's
job is to help learners find and fix these mismatches.
Common misconception. Learners think the financial snapshot is "accounting" and therefore too hard for them. It is not. It is
five numbers and one division. If a learner can calculate change in a market, they can build a financial snapshot.

F.5 The pitch — sixty seconds that explain the whole plan
The idea. The pitch is the spoken version of the one-page plan. It is sixty seconds long — no more — and it covers the same
ground as the canvas: who you serve, the problem, what you do, how you price, and what happens next. The pitch is not a
performance; it is a conversation starter. Its job is to make the listener ask a question — because a question means they are
interested.
The four-part pitch structure:
What you say Time
1. Who and problem
"I help [specific customer] who [specific problem]." 15 seconds
2. What you do
"I [deliverable] so that [outcome for the customer]." 20 seconds
3. How you price
"I charge [price range] using [pricing model]." 10 seconds
4. What happens next
"If you know someone who needs this, [ask]." 15 seconds
Worked example — FastFix Phone Repair:
"I help phone owners in Diobu and Trans-Amadi whose screens break and who cannot afford to replace the whole phone. I
replace screens and batteries on the spot, usually in under an hour, with a 30-day warranty. I charge ₦8,000 for a screen
replacement, which includes the part and the labour. If you know someone with a broken screen, tell them to message me
on WhatsApp at 0803-XXX-XXXX — I will give them a quote before I start any work."
Timing check: That is 55 seconds. It names the customer, the problem, the deliverable, the price, and the next step. It does not
ramble. It does not apologise. It does not say "I think maybe I could possibly."
Why sixty seconds. Because that is how long a stranger will listen. A customer at a bus stop, a mentor at a networking event, a
friend who asks "so what are you doing now?" — they will give you sixty seconds. If you cannot explain your business in sixty
seconds, you do not yet understand it well enough. The pitch is a test of clarity, not a test of charisma.
The defence — the four questions you must be able to answer:
After the pitch, the listener will ask questions. The capstone viva asks four specific questions. The learner must be able to answer
all four without bluffing.
1. "Why this price?" — The answer comes from the costed price list. "The screen costs ₦3,500, materials ₦500, my time
₦1,500, and I add ₦2,500 profit. That is ₦8,000. I checked three competitors in the market and they charge between
₦7,000 and ₦10,000."
2. "How did you validate that?" — The answer comes from the validation notes. "I spoke to five phone owners in Diobu.
Three said they had broken a screen in the last six months and paid between ₦6,000 and ₦12,000 to fix it. Two said they
would use a same-day service if it came with a warranty."
3. "What is your break-even?" — The answer comes from the financial snapshot. "My fixed costs are ₦12,000 a month. I
make ₦3,500 profit per screen replacement. So I need four screen replacements a month — one per week — to cover my
costs."
4. "What will you do in your first seven days?" — The answer comes from the marketing plan and the next-30-days section.
"Day 1: finish my WhatsApp Business catalogue. Day 2: message 20 contacts. Day 3: post my first status with a before-and-
after photo. Day 4: ask my first customer for a testimonial. Day 5: post the testimonial. Day 6: review my numbers. Day 7:
plan week two."
Say this to the class: "These four questions are not tricks. They are the questions every customer, every mentor, and every bank
will ask. If you can answer them without looking at your notes, you know your business. If you cannot, you have more work to
do — and that is fine. That is what this session is for."
Common misconception. Learners think the pitch is about being smooth or charismatic. It is not. A clear, honest, specific pitch
beats a smooth, vague one every time. The goal is not to impress — it is to be understood.
F.6 Assembling the supporting pack — the evidence behind the plan
The idea. The one-page plan is the decision. The supporting pack is the evidence. The capstone requires at least three
supporting documents from the list below. The plan says "I charge ₦8,000" — the pack shows the costed price list that proves
it. The plan say "I validated with five customers" — the pack shows the validation notes with their actual words.
The supporting pack checklist:
Document From session What it proves
Costed price list S4 The price is based on cost, not guesswork
Validation notes S2 Real customers said they have the problem
One-page client agreement S6 The learner can set scope professionally
Sample invoice S7 The learner can bill properly
Income/expense tracker S7 The learner can keep records
WhatsApp Business profile S8 The learner can be found for free
Seven-day marketing plan S8 The learner has a plan, not just an idea
The rule of three. The capstone requires at least three of these documents. But a strong plan has all seven — because every
section of the plan is stronger when the evidence is in the pack. A plan that says "I charge ₦8,000" with a costed price list in the
pack is more credible than the same plan with no price list.
How to assemble. The learner creates a folder in Google Drive called CEA-Biz-FirstnameLastname-Capstone . Inside, they place: the
one-page plan (Google Doc), the financial snapshot (Google Sheet), and the supporting documents (already in their working
folder from Sessions 1–8). They share the folder with the instructor. That is the submission.
Common misconception. Learners think the pack is a filing exercise. It is not. The pack is what makes the plan believable. A
plan without evidence is a wish. A plan with evidence is a business.
F.7 Presenting and defending — the viva
The idea. The viva is the short talk and question-and-answer that follows the pitch. It is 3–4 minutes total: 60 seconds of
pitch, 2–3 minutes of questions. The viva is not an exam — it is a conversation about the learner's business. The instructor
is not trying to catch the learner out; the instructor is asking the questions a real customer or mentor would ask.
The structure of the viva:
Time What happens
Pitch 60 seconds The learner delivers the pitch from memory or notes
Time What happens
Question 1: Price 1 minute "Why this price?" — learner explains the costing
Question 2: Validation 1 minute "How did you validate that?" — learner shares what customers said
Question 3: Break-even 1 minute "What is your break-even?" — learner explains the maths
Question 4: First seven days 1 minute "What will you do in your first seven days?" — learner lists the actions
Peer feedback 2 minutes One classmate gives two stars and a wish
What the instructor listens for:
Clarity: Can the learner explain the business in plain English without jargon?
Honesty: Does the learner admit what they do not know? (This is a strength, not a weakness.)
Consistency: Do the numbers in the pitch match the numbers in the plan and the snapshot?
Specificity: Does the learner name real customers, real prices, real actions — or vague generalities?
What the instructor does not do:
Do not interrupt the pitch unless it goes over 90 seconds.
Do not ask questions the learner has not been told to expect.
Do not mark the viva on charisma or confidence — mark it on clarity, honesty and consistency.
Common misconception. Learners think the viva is a test they can fail. It is not. The viva is a rehearsal for the real
conversations they will have with customers, mentors and partners. The instructor's feedback is the same feedback they will
get in the market — but in a safe room, where mistakes cost nothing.
F.8 Summary of the session
A one-page business plan is the whole business on one sheet: seven sections, each a decision.
The financial snapshot holds five numbers — launch costs, fixed costs, unit price, variable cost, break-even — and they
must match the price list.
The pitch is sixty seconds: who, problem, what, price, next step.
The defence is four questions: why this price, how validated, what break-even, what first seven days.
The supporting pack is the evidence — at least three documents from Sessions 1–8.
The viva is a conversation, not an exam — clarity, honesty and consistency are what count.
The honest limits section is the most important writing on the page — it shows the learner knows what they do not know.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Chidi Okonkwo — the FastFix Phone Repair owner from our running example — has been operating for six months.
He followed the plan he built in this course. Here is what happened:
Month 1: He did 8 screen replacements and 12 battery replacements. He earned ₦104,000. His fixed costs were ₦12,000. He
made ₦28,000 net.
Month 2: He did 10 screen replacements and 15 battery replacements. He earned ₦145,000. He made ₦41,000 net.
Month 3: A competitor opened in the same market. Chidi's screen replacements dropped to 6 for the month. He earned
₦82,000. He made ₦18,000 net.
The problem. Chidi is worried. His income dropped in Month 3. He is thinking about lowering his prices to match the
competitor. He comes to you — his CEA instructor — for advice.
Relevant facts.
Chidi's break-even is 4 screen replacements per month. He did 6 in Month 3 — still above break-even, but barely.
The competitor charges ₦7,000 for a screen replacement — ₦1,000 less than Chidi.
Chidi has a 30-day warranty; the competitor does not.
Chidi has 40 WhatsApp Business contacts who have bought from him before. The competitor has no customer list.
Chidi's validation notes show that customers chose him because of the warranty and the same-day service, not because of
price.
Decision point. What should Chidi do — lower his prices, or something else?
Possible approaches.
1. Lower his screen replacement price to ₦7,000 to match the competitor.
2. Keep his price at ₦8,000 and emphasise the warranty and same-day service in his marketing.
3. Add a new service — phone cleaning and software updates — at ₦1,500, to bring in extra revenue.
4. Do nothing and wait to see if the competitor leaves.
Recommended solution. Approach 2, with elements of Approach 3.
Reasoning:
Approach 1 starts a price war. If Chidi lowers to ₦7,000, the competitor may lower to ₦6,000. Chidi's break-even rises from
4 to 5 screen replacements per month — he needs more work just to survive. And he loses the margin that makes the
business worth running.
Approach 2 plays to Chidi's strengths. His validation notes show customers chose him for the warranty and same-day
service — not price. He should remind his 40 existing customers of this. A WhatsApp broadcast to his customer list is free
and takes 10 minutes.
Approach 3 adds a low-cost, high-margin service. A phone cleaning and software update costs Chidi almost nothing (his
time and a small amount of data) but can earn ₦1,500. If he does 10 of these in a month, that is ₦15,000 — almost
enough to cover his fixed costs on its own.
Approach 4 is passive. The competitor is not going to leave because Chidi ignores them. Chidi needs to act — but act on
his strengths, not his weaknesses.
What Chidi actually did (the real ending): Chidi kept his price at ₦8,000. He sent a WhatsApp broadcast to his 40 customers:
"Screen replacement with 30-day warranty — same-day service at Mile 3 Market. Reply 'SCREEN' for a quote." He got 12 replies
and did 8 screen replacements in two weeks. He also added phone cleaning at ₦1,500 and did 15 in the month. His Month 4
revenue was ₦98,000 — back above his Month 2 level. He did not lower his price once.
Professional connection. This is why the capstone requires a financial snapshot and a marketing plan. Without the snapshot,
Chidi would not have known his break-even was 4 — and he might have panicked and cut prices. Without the marketing plan,
he would not have known to message his 40 existing customers. The plan is not a document you file and forget — it is the tool
you use when things get difficult.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Blank sections on the plan
Symptoms: One or more of the seven sections is empty or says "TBD." Likely cause: The learner has not found the document
that holds the answer, or has not made the decision yet. How to investigate: Ask "which document has this answer?" If the
learner does not know, point to the relevant session's artefact. Corrective steps: Open the working folder, find the document,
transfer the answer to the plan. How to confirm resolved: Every section has a real answer — no blanks, no "TBD." Prevention:
Before the session, check that the learner's folder has all seven documents from Sessions 1–8.
K.2 Numbers that do not match across documents
Symptoms: The price in the plan is ₦8,000 but the price list says ₦6,000. Or the break-even in the snapshot does not match the
one in the plan. Likely cause: The learner changed a number in one place and forgot to change it everywhere. How to
investigate: Pick one number (the unit price) and trace it through the plan, the price list, and the snapshot. Where does it
change? Corrective steps: Decide which number is correct, then update every document to match. How to confirm resolved:
The same number appears everywhere it is mentioned. Prevention: Do the consistency check (Section H, step 10) before
finishing the plan.
K.3 Break-even not rounded up
Symptoms: The break-even is written as "3.43 jobs per month." Likely cause: The learner did the division but did not round up.
How to investigate: Ask "can you do 0.43 of a job?" The learner will say no. Corrective steps: Round up to the next whole
number. 3.43 becomes 4. How to confirm resolved: The break-even is a whole number. Prevention: Remind the class that
break-even is always rounded up — you cannot do a fraction of a job.
K.4 Vague first-month target
Symptoms: The target is "as much as I can" or "a lot of customers." Likely cause: The learner has not calculated a naira figure.
How to investigate: Ask "how many jobs is that? At what price?" Corrective steps: Calculate: number of jobs × price =
revenue target. Write the naira figure. How to confirm resolved: The target is a specific naira amount. Prevention: The
financial snapshot requires a first-month target — build it there first, then transfer it to the plan.
K.5 Honest limits are vague
Symptoms: The honest limits say "I need to learn more" or "I hope it works." Likely cause: The learner does not understand
what an honest limit is. How to investigate: Ask "what specifically do you not know yet? What will you do to find out?"
Corrective steps: Write three specific unknowns and three specific tests. "I have not proven that 20 contacts will reply. I will test
this by messaging 20 contacts in week one and recording the replies." How to confirm resolved: Each limit names a specific
unknown and a specific test. Prevention: Explain that honest limits are experiments, not confessions.
K.6 Pitch is too long
Symptoms: The pitch goes over 90 seconds. Likely cause: The learner is including too much detail — background, history,
multiple services. How to investigate: Time the pitch. Where does it go over? Corrective steps: Cut everything except who,
problem, what, price, next step. Practice with a timer. How to confirm resolved: The pitch is 60 seconds or less. Prevention:
Rehearse with a timer before the viva. Sixty seconds is shorter than you think.
K.7 Pitch is too vague
Symptoms: The pitch says "I do phone repair" and "I charge reasonable prices." Likely cause: The learner has not used the
costed price list or the offer sheet. How to investigate: Ask "what is your starting price? What is your deliverable?" Corrective
steps: Fill in the specific price and the specific deliverable from the documents. How to confirm resolved: The pitch names a
specific price and a specific deliverable. Prevention: The pitch structure (Section F.5) requires a price and a deliverable — do
not let the learner skip them.
K.8 The learner cannot answer one of the four viva questions
Symptoms: The learner freezes or bluffs when asked "why this price?" or "what is your break-even?" Likely cause: The learner
has not connected the question to the document that holds the answer. How to investigate: Ask "which document has the
answer to that question?" Corrective steps: Open the document, find the answer, practise saying it out loud. How to confirm
resolved: The learner can answer all four questions without bluffing. Prevention: Before the viva, have the learner practise the
four questions with a partner.
K.9 The supporting pack is incomplete
Symptoms: The learner submits the plan but only one or two supporting documents. Likely cause: The learner does not
understand that the pack is the evidence. How to investigate: Ask "which document proves your price? Which proves your
validation?" Corrective steps: Identify the missing documents and add them to the folder. How to confirm resolved: At least
three supporting documents are in the folder. Prevention: The capstone checklist (Section F.6) lists the seven possible
documents — check the folder against it before submission.
K.10 The learner apologises for the plan
Symptoms: The learner says "I know it is not very good" or "I am not really a business person." Likely cause: The learner is
comparing themselves to an imaginary perfect businessperson. How to investigate: Ask "what is one thing in this plan that you
are sure about?" Corrective steps: Point out the sections that are strong. Remind the learner that the plan is a first version, not a
final exam. How to confirm resolved: The learner can name one strong thing about the plan. Prevention: Normalise
imperfection from the start. Say: "Every business plan is a first draft. The goal is not perfection — it is honesty and clarity."
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: Check before fixing — trace the number, time the pitch, ask which document holds the
answer. Confirm the cause, then correct.

Common misconceptions to watch for and correct:
"A plan is a creative writing exercise." → No — every sentence is a decision you will act on.
"The financial snapshot is accounting, and accounting is too hard for me." → It is five numbers and one division. If you can
calculate change, you can build a snapshot.
"The pitch is about being charismatic." → No — it is about being clear. A clear, honest, specific pitch beats a smooth,
vague one every time.
"The viva is an exam I can fail." → It is a rehearsal for real conversations. The questions are the questions customers and
mentors actually ask.
"Honest limits are confessions of weakness." → They are experiments. They show you know what to test next — which is a
sign of strength.
"The plan is finished now." → The plan is a living document. It should be updated every month as you learn.
"I need to lower my prices when a competitor appears." → Not necessarily. Check your break-even first. Compete on your
strengths, not your weaknesses.
Suggested remedial exercises for learners who struggle:
1. Section-by-section assembly: If the learner cannot fill the whole canvas, fill one section at a time across three sessions.
Start with "who we serve" and "what we sell" — the easiest sections.
2. Break-even drill: Give five simple scenarios (fixed costs, price, variable cost) and ask the learner to calculate break-even for
each. Repeat until it is automatic.
3. Pitch timer drill: Have the learner deliver the pitch while you time it. Cut whatever makes it go over 60 seconds. Repeat
until it fits.
4. Viva rehearsal: Have the learner practise the four viva questions with a partner. The partner asks the questions; the learner
answers from their documents. Repeat until the learner can answer without bluffing.
5. Consistency check drill: Give the learner a plan with three deliberate mismatches (price in plan does not match price list,
break-even does not match snapshot, first action does not match marketing plan). Ask them to find and fix the
mismatches.
Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): Provide a plan canvas with the first three sections already filled in (from the learner's offer sheet). The
learner fills sections 4–7 and the honest limits. Provide a financial snapshot with the numbers filled in — the learner only
calculates the break-even.
Level 2 (standard): The session as written — full plan, full snapshot, full pitch, full viva.
Level 3 (challenge): The learner builds the plan for their own business and for a second, different business (e.g., a product
business if their main plan is a service business). They also write a 30-day action plan with specific, dated tasks — not just
"message 20 contacts" but "message 20 contacts by 10am on Day 2, record replies in tracker by 5pm." They also identify
one risk in their plan and write a one-paragraph contingency.
