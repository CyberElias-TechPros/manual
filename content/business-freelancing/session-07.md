# Invoicing, Getting Paid and Basic Accounting

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 3 — Module 3: Run and Grow

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with invoicing and accounting)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 6 one-page agreement and scope. Learners must be able to do basic arithmetic. No prior accounting knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 6 you learned to manage clients and write agreements. Today we handle the money: quotes vs invoices vs receipts, what a complete invoice contains, how to track income and expenses in Google Sheets, how to chase late payments politely, and how to handle taxes in plain terms. Getting paid is the whole point of business.

**Preparation before class:** Print the invoice template (Section F.2); print the payment-chasing sequence (Section F.5); print the income/expense tracker template (Section F.3); have the FastFix case pack ready; prepare a sample Google Sheets tracker on the projector; bring a sample invoice from a real-shaped job. Print the SIMULATION — THE FIRST CLIENT enquiry script (Section J) and set a 15-minute timer — today's simulation is 15% of the course. Instructor note on tone: Many learners feel shy about chasing money — they think it is rude or desperate. Reframe it: chasing payment is professional. You did the work, you agreed a price, and you deserve to be paid. A polite chase is not rude — it is how a real business operates. Keep the mood practical and confidence-building.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Distinguish between a quote, an invoice and a receipt — and explain when each is used.
2. Produce a complete invoice: business name, date, invoice number, customer, description, quantity, unit price, total,
payment details and terms.
3. Set up an income and expense tracker in Google Sheets and record transactions correctly.
4. Follow a polite payment-chasing sequence: friendly reminder on due date, follow-up call, firm written message with the
invoice attached, documented escalation.
5. Explain in simple terms the taxes a small Nigerian business must consider (income tax, withholding tax, VAT awareness) and
why to check current rules on official FIRS/state sources.
6. Develop the habit of setting aside a fixed percentage of every payment for tax.
7. Raise a complete invoice and start a working tracker (LO9).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two "payment stories" without labelling them.
Story A: "Chidi from FastFix repaired a customer's phone. He did not give an invoice — he just told the customer the price.
The customer paid cash. Two weeks later, the customer came back and said 'I already paid you' — but Chidi had no record.
They argued. The customer was angry. Chidi lost the customer and his reputation."
Story B: "Adaeze from Adaeze Fashion sewed a dress. She gave the customer an invoice — clear, with the price, the
payment terms, and her bank details. The customer paid by transfer. Adaeze recorded the payment in her tracker. Two
weeks later, the customer came back for another dress — and brought a friend. Adaeze has a record, the customer has a
receipt, and both are happy."
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses. Hands up — which one got paid properly and kept the customer? … Story B, almost always. Why?
Because Adaeze had a system — an invoice, a record, a receipt. Chidi had nothing — and when there is a dispute,
nothing means no proof. Today we learn to be Adaeze. You will learn the difference between a quote, an invoice and a
receipt, how to write a complete invoice, how to track your money, how to chase late payments politely, and how to handle
taxes in plain terms. Getting paid is the whole point of business — today you learn to do it properly."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B had an invoice", "A had no proof", "B kept records", "A argued with the
customer". Accept all answers and draw out the difference: system vs no system.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you have ever done work and not been paid — or been paid late?" Many hands go
up. Say:
"That is not a failure of kindness — that is a failure of paperwork. And paperwork is learnable. Today you learn it."
Why this opener works: It uses a situation everyone recognises (payment disputes), it isolates documentation as the variable,
and it frames invoicing and records as protection — not bureaucracy.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the seven parts of a one-page agreement?"
Expected answer: Parties, scope, price, timeline, payment schedule, cancellation, signatures.
Common gap: learners remember four or five. Clarify: "The agreement is what you agreed — the invoice is how you get
paid."
Question 2: "What are the four communication standards?"
Expected answer: Reply fast, confirm in writing, use plain language, never leave a customer guessing.
Common gap: learners remember two or three. Clarify: "The communication standards apply to payment messages too —
reply fast, confirm in writing."
Question 3: "What is scope creep, and how do you handle it?"
Expected answer: When the customer asks for extras beyond the agreed scope. You quote for the extra — you do not do it
for free.
Common gap: learners remember "quote for extras" but not the term. Clarify: "Scope creep is the customer asking for more
— your response is always: quote, don't refuse, don't do it for free."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You can manage clients and write agreements. Now you get paid. The agreement says what you will do and what you
will charge — the invoice is how you ask for the money, and the receipt is how you prove you were paid. Today we learn all
three — plus how to track your money, chase late payments, and handle taxes."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the seven parts of the agreement, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the
board before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 The paperwork of getting paid — quote, invoice, receipt
The idea. Three documents matter in getting paid: a quote (before the job), an invoice (after or at a stage), and a receipt
(proof of payment). Most beginners use one word for all three — and cause confusion. Learn the difference.
The three documents — teach them one by one:
1. Quote (before the job):
What it is: A written estimate of what the job will cost — given before you start work.
When to use it: When the customer asks "how much will this cost?" — you send a quote.
What it contains: Description, quantity, unit price, total, validity (e.g., 7 days), payment terms, exclusions.
FastFix example: Chidi sends a quote: "Screen repair for iPhone 11 — ₦6,500. Valid for 7 days. 50% before repair, 50%
on collection."
Say this: "A quote is an offer — the customer can accept it, negotiate it, or walk away. It is not a demand for payment."
2. Invoice (after or at a stage):
What it is: A formal request for payment — given when the job is done (or at an agreed stage).
When to use it: When the job is complete — or when you need a stage payment (e.g., 50% before, 50% on delivery).
What it contains: Business name, date, invoice number, customer name, description, quantity, unit price, total,
payment details (bank account), payment terms (due date), and a thank-you.
FastFix example: After the repair, Chidi sends an invoice: "Invoice 001 — Screen repair for iPhone 11 — ₦6,500.
Payment due by 17 October. Bank: GTB 0123456789 — Chidi Okonkwo."
Say this: "An invoice is a request for payment — it is the most important document in getting paid. No invoice, no
payment."
3. Receipt (proof of payment):
What it is: A document that proves the customer has paid.
When to use it: When the customer pays — you send a receipt.
What it contains: Business name, date, receipt number, customer, description, amount paid, payment method (cash,
transfer, POS), and a thank-you.
FastFix example: After payment, Chidi sends a receipt: "Receipt 001 — Payment for Invoice 001 — ₦6,500 —
received by transfer on 17 October. Thank you."
Say this: "A receipt is proof — it protects both sides. If the customer later says 'I did not pay', you have proof. If you
later say 'I did not receive payment', the customer has proof."
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who writes nothing — no price, no record, no receipt — is helpless
when there is a dispute. A trader who writes the price, gives a receipt, and keeps a record — that trader is protected. The
paperwork is not bureaucracy — it is protection.
Misconception to correct. "An invoice is the same as a quote." No — a quote is before the job (an offer), an invoice is after the
job (a request for payment). Using them correctly prevents confusion and disputes.
F.2 What a complete invoice contains
The idea. An incomplete invoice causes late payment — the customer does not know how much to pay, how to pay, or when
to pay. A complete invoice has ten parts.
The ten parts — teach them one by one:
1. Business name and contact: Your name, phone number, and (if you have it) your business name and address.
FastFix example: "FastFix Phone Repair — Mile 3 Market, Diobu, Port Harcourt — 0803-XXX-XXXX."
2. Date: The date the invoice is issued.
FastFix example: "Date: 10 October 2026."
3. Invoice number: A unique number for each invoice — so you can reference it.
FastFix example: "Invoice 001."
4. Customer name: Who the invoice is for.
FastFix example: "Customer: Mr. Emeka Obi."
5. Description of the job: What you did — in clear language.
FastFix example: "Screen repair for iPhone 11."
6. Quantity and unit price: How many, and the price per unit.
FastFix example: "Quantity: 1 — Unit price: ₦6,500."
7. Total amount: The full amount in naira.
FastFix example: "Total: ₦6,500."
8. Payment details: How the customer can pay — bank name, account number, account name.
FastFix example: "Bank: GTB — Account: 0123456789 — Name: Chidi Okonkwo."
9. Payment terms: When the payment is due.
FastFix example: "Payment due by: 17 October 2026."
10. Thank-you and terms: A polite note and any terms (e.g., late payment fee).
FastFix example: "Thank you for your business. Please pay by the due date. A 5% late fee applies after 7 days."
Say this: "Ten parts. If any part is missing, the invoice is incomplete — and an incomplete invoice is a late invoice. Write a
complete invoice every time."
Real-life comparison. Think of a restaurant bill. A good bill has the restaurant name, the date, the items, the prices, the total,
and how to pay. A bad bill says "₦5,000" on a piece of paper — and you wonder what you are paying for. An invoice is the
business version of the good bill.
Misconception to correct. "An invoice is just the amount." No — an invoice is the amount plus the context — what, how,
when, where. The context is what makes payment easy.
F.3 The income and expense tracker — your money memory
The idea. The tracker is where you record every naira that comes in and every naira that goes out. It is your money memory —
without it, you are guessing.
The simple tracker — teach the structure:
Date Description Type Income (₦) Expense (₦) Balance (₦)
10 Oct Screen repair — Customer A Income 6,500 16,500
10 Oct New screen stock Expense 3,500 13,000
11 Oct Battery replacement — Customer B Income 4,500 17,500
11 Oct Transport Expense 500 17,000
12 Oct Charger sale — Customer C Income 1,500 18,500
12 Oct Data for WhatsApp Expense 500 18,000
The rules of the tracker:
1. Record every transaction — even ₦500.
2. Use the right column — income in the income column, expense in the expense column.
3. Calculate the balance after each transaction.
4. Review weekly — add up income and expenses, calculate profit, decide one change.
The weekly review — five minutes:
Total income this week: 6,500 + 4,500 + 1,500 = ₦12,500
Total expenses this week: 3,500 + 500 + 500 = ₦4,500
Profit this week: 12,500 − 4,500 = ₦8,000
What sold well: Screen repairs (highest price).
What cost too much: Screen stock (₦3,500 — can I find a cheaper supplier?).
What I will change: Order screen stock in bulk to reduce cost.
Real-life comparison. Think of a student tracking pocket money. If they write down every naira received and every naira spent,
they know exactly how much they have — and they can plan. If they do not, they run out mid-week and wonder why. A
business is the same — just with bigger numbers.
Misconception to correct. "I will remember what I earned." No — you will forget. Write it down. The tracker is your memory.
F.4 The polite payment-chasing sequence
The idea. Late payment is the most common cash-flow problem in small business. The fix is a polite, persistent sequence —
not anger, not silence. Most late payments are not dishonesty — they are forgetfulness or cash-flow problems on the
customer's side.
The four-step sequence — teach them one by step:
1. Friendly reminder (on the due date):
When: The day the payment is due.
What to say: "Hello [name], I hope you are well. I wanted to remind you that the payment of ₦6,500 for Invoice 001 is
due today. Please let me know if you have any questions. Thank you."
Why it works: It is polite, it assumes the customer forgot, and it gives them an easy way to respond.
2. Follow-up call (2–3 days after the due date):
When: 2–3 days after the due date, if no payment.
What to say: "Hello [name], I sent a reminder about Invoice 001 — I wanted to check if you received it. Is everything
okay with the payment?"
Why it works: A call is harder to ignore than a message — and it shows you are serious.
3. Firm written message (7 days after the due date):
When: 7 days after the due date, if still no payment.
What to say: "Hello [name], I am writing about Invoice 001 — ₦6,500, due on 17 October. I have not received
payment. Please pay by 25 October. If I do not hear from you, I will have to [stop further work / take further steps]. I
hope we can resolve this quickly. Thank you."
Attach the invoice to the message — so the customer has everything they need.
Why it works: It is firm but professional — and it documents your effort.
4. Documented escalation (14+ days after the due date):
When: 14+ days after the due date, if still no payment.
What to do: Send a final message with a clear deadline and consequence. If the amount is large, consider a formal
demand letter (from a lawyer) or a small claims process. For small amounts, weigh the cost of chasing against the
amount — sometimes the lesson is worth more than the money.
Why it works: It shows you are serious — and it protects your rights.
The golden rule of chasing payment: The first message should never be angry. Anger makes the customer defensive — and
defensive customers do not pay. Be polite, be firm, be persistent.
Real-life comparison. Think of a landlord. A landlord who shouts at a tenant on the first day of late payment gets anger — not
rent. A landlord who sends a polite reminder, then a call, then a firm letter — that landlord gets paid. The sequence works
because it is polite, firm and documented.
Misconception to correct. "Chasing payment is rude." No — you did the work, you agreed a price, and you deserve to be
paid. A polite chase is not rude — it is professional.
F.5 Taxes in plain terms — what every small business owner must know
The idea. Taxes are one of the most feared and misunderstood parts of business. The good news: for a small beginner, the
obligations are simple — and the rules change, so you check, you do not memorise.
The three taxes to be aware of — teach them in plain terms:
1. Income tax: Tax on the profit your business makes. If your business is registered and making profit, you may have an
income tax obligation. The rate and thresholds change — check the current rules on the official FIRS or state tax website.
Say this: "Income tax is on profit — not on sales. If you make no profit, you may pay nothing. But you must check the
current rules."
2. Withholding tax (WHT): When a corporate client pays you, they may deduct a percentage (currently around 5–10% —
verify the current rate) and remit it to the tax authority on your behalf. This is not an extra cost to you — it is a prepayment
of your tax. Keep records of all WHT deductions — they count toward your tax obligation.
Say this: "Withholding tax is not a penalty — it is a prepayment. If a corporate client deducts it, they give you a WHT
credit note — keep it safe."
3. VAT (Value Added Tax): VAT is a tax on goods and services, currently at 7.5% (verify the current rate). Businesses above a
certain turnover threshold are required to charge VAT and remit it. Small businesses below the threshold may not need to
charge VAT — but you must check the current threshold.
Say this: "VAT is not your money — it is collected for the government. If you are required to charge it, you must remit
it. If you are not, you do not charge it. Check the current threshold."
The habit of setting aside for tax:
Every time you receive a payment, set aside a fixed percentage (e.g., 10%) for tax.
Put it in a separate envelope or account — do not spend it.
When tax is due, you have the money ready.
Say this: "The habit of setting aside is more important than the exact percentage. The percentage changes — the habit
does not."
Where to check current tax information:
FIRS (Federal Inland Revenue Service): firs.gov.ng
State tax authority: your state's official tax website
Never rely on a friend's memory, a WhatsApp forward, or a roadside "tax consultant". Check the official source — or ask a
licensed tax professional.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader. A trader who sets aside 10% of every sale for tax never has a tax problem — the
money is always there. A trader who spends everything and hopes for the best — that trader has a crisis when the tax officer
comes. The habit is the difference.
Misconception to correct. "Taxes are only for big companies." No — if you are making profit, you have tax obligations. But
the obligations are small for small businesses — and they change. Check, do not guess.
Say this to the class:
"We are not teaching you to be a tax consultant. We are teaching you three things: (1) taxes exist and you should know
about them, (2) the rules change — check the official source, and (3) set aside a percentage of every payment — the
habit is what matters. When you are ready, talk to a licensed tax professional for advice on your specific situation."
F.6 Summary of the session
Three documents: a quote (before), an invoice (after or at a stage), a receipt (proof of payment).
A complete invoice has ten parts — business name, date, invoice number, customer, description, quantity/unit price, total,
payment details, terms, thank-you.
The income and expense tracker is your money memory — record everything, review weekly.
The payment-chasing sequence: friendly reminder, follow-up call, firm written message, documented escalation — the
first message should never be angry.
Taxes in plain terms: income tax (on profit), withholding tax (prepayment), VAT (for some businesses). Check current rules
on official sources. Set aside a percentage of every payment.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Show the class how to write a complete invoice, set up a tracker, and chase a late payment — live — so they can do it
themselves.
Setup: Open a blank Google Doc titled "Invoice — FastFix". Open a blank Google Sheet titled "Tracker — FastFix". Have the
payment-chasing sequence on the projector. Project both.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A blank invoice template on the projector.
A blank tracker template on the projector.
The payment-chasing sequence on the projector.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — write a complete invoice (5 minutes)
Say: "We will write a complete invoice for a FastFix screen repair. Ten parts — watch."
Fill in the invoice template on the projector, narrating each part:
FastFix Phone Repair Mile 3 Market, Diobu, Port Harcourt Tel: 0803-XXX-XXXX
Invoice 001 — Date: 10 October 2026 Customer: Mr. Emeka Obi
Description: Screen repair for iPhone 11 Quantity: 1 Unit price: ₦6,500 Total: ₦6,500
Payment details: Bank: GTB — Account: 0123456789 — Name: Chidi Okonkwo Payment terms: Payment due by 17
October 2026. A 5% late fee applies after 7 days.
Thank you for your business.
Say: "Ten parts. Business name, date, invoice number, customer, description, quantity/unit price, total, payment details, terms,
thank-you. Complete. Professional. This invoice makes payment easy."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — set up the tracker (5 minutes)
Say: "Now the tracker. We will set it up in Google Sheets — free, on your phone."
Create the tracker on the projector, narrating each step:
Date Description Type Income (₦) Expense (₦) Balance (₦)
10 Oct Screen repair — Customer A Income 6,500 16,500
10 Oct New screen stock Expense 3,500 13,000
11 Oct Battery replacement — Customer B Income 4,500 17,500
11 Oct Transport Expense 500 17,000
12 Oct Charger sale — Customer C Income 1,500 18,500
12 Oct Data for WhatsApp Expense 500 18,000
Say: "Six transactions. Income in the income column, expenses in the expense column, balance after each. At the end of the
week, I review: total income ₦12,500, total expenses ₦4,500, profit ₦8,000. That is the tracker."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — chase a late payment (3 minutes)
Say: "Now a late payment. The customer has not paid Invoice 001 — due 17 October. Today is 20 October. I will use the
sequence."
Step 1 — Friendly reminder (due date, 17 October):
"Hello Mr. Obi, I hope you are well. I wanted to remind you that the payment of ₦6,500 for Invoice 001 is due today. Please
let me know if you have any questions. Thank you. — Chidi"
Step 2 — Follow-up call (19 October):
"Hello Mr. Obi, I sent a reminder about Invoice 001 — I wanted to check if you received it. Is everything okay with the
payment? — Chidi"
Step 3 — Firm written message (24 October):
"Hello Mr. Obi, I am writing about Invoice 001 — ₦6,500, due on 17 October. I have not received payment. Please pay by
25 October. If I do not hear from you, I will have to stop further work. I hope we can resolve this quickly. Thank you. —
Chidi" (Attach the invoice.)
Step 4 — Documented escalation (27 October):
"Hello Mr. Obi, this is my final message about Invoice 001. I have not received payment. Please pay by 30 October or I will
have to take further steps. I would prefer to resolve this amicably. — Chidi"
Say: "Four steps. Polite, firm, documented. The first message is never angry — anger makes the customer defensive. Be polite,

Situation. Emeka is a freelance graphic designer in Port Harcourt. He designs flyers, logos and social media graphics for small

Relevant facts.
Emeka has been earning about ₦80,000 a month for the past six months.
He has no TIN, no business registration, and no proper invoicing system.
The corporate client is willing to pay — but only with a proper invoice and TIN.
Emeka is worried that getting a TIN means he will pay a lot of tax.
Decision point. What should Emeka do?
Possible approaches.
1. Ignore the corporate client — "they are too complicated."
2. Try to write a proper invoice without a TIN — "maybe they will accept it."
3. Get a TIN, write a proper invoice, and build a system for the future.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Emeka's problem is not the corporate client — it is that he has been operating without

2. Write a proper invoice: Use the ten parts — business name, date, invoice number, customer, description, quantity/unit
price, total, payment details, terms, thank-you. Include his TIN on the invoice.
3. Set up a tracker: Record all income and expenses in a Google Sheet. Review weekly.
4. Set aside for tax: Set aside 10% of every payment — so the money is always there.
5. Learn about withholding tax: The corporate client may deduct WHT — keep the credit note, and it counts toward his tax.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (ignore) loses a ₦50,000 client — and all future corporate clients.
Approach 2 (try without a TIN) will fail — the corporate client has already said they need it.
Approach 3 (build the system) takes a few days of effort — and opens a whole new market.
Professional connection. Corporate clients are the best clients for freelancers — they pay well, on time, and repeatedly. But
they demand professionalism — a TIN, a proper invoice, and clean records. The freelancer who has these things gets the
corporate work; the freelancer who does not stays in the informal market. The foundation is not a burden — it is the key.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Confusing quote, invoice and receipt
Symptoms: The learner sends a quote when they should send an invoice — or does not give a receipt. Likely cause: They use
the words interchangeably — and do not know the difference. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you send — a quote, an
invoice, or a receipt? When should each be used?" Corrective steps: Quote before the job (offer), invoice after (request for
receipt), receipt (proof of payment). How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain when each document is used.
Prevention: The three documents are a sequence — quote, invoice, receipt — use them in order.
K.2 An incomplete invoice
Symptoms: The invoice says "₦6,500" — and the customer does not know what for, how to pay, or when. Likely cause: The
invoice is missing parts — no description, no payment details, no terms. How to investigate: Ask: "Does your invoice have all
ten parts? What is missing?" Corrective steps: Use the ten-part invoice template — every time. How to confirm resolved: The
learner's invoice has all ten parts. Prevention: An incomplete invoice is a late invoice — write a complete invoice every time.
K.3 No tracker — "I will remember"
Symptoms: The learner has no record of income or expenses — they guess at their profit. Likely cause: They think record-
keeping is for accountants. How to investigate: Ask: "What were your sales last week? What were your expenses? What was
your profit?" Corrective steps: Set up a tracker — record everything, review weekly. How to confirm resolved: The learner can
state their sales, expenses and profit for the last week. Prevention: The tracker is your money memory — record everything.
K.4 Angry first message
Symptoms: The learner's first chasing message is angry — "You owe me money! Pay now!" Likely cause: They are frustrated
— and let it show. How to investigate: Ask: "What did your first message say? Was it polite?" Corrective steps: Rewrite the
first message — be polite, be firm, be persistent. The first message is never angry. How to confirm resolved: The learner's first
message is polite. Prevention: Anger makes the customer defensive — and defensive customers do not pay.
K.5 No follow-up
Symptoms: The learner sends one message — and waits. The customer does not respond. Likely cause: They think one
message is enough. How to investigate: Ask: "How many times have you followed up? When?" Corrective steps: Use the four-
step sequence — reminder, call, firm message, escalation. How to confirm resolved: The learner has followed the full
sequence. Prevention: Most late payments need more than one contact — follow the sequence.
K.6 No tax habit — "I will deal with it later"
Symptoms: The learner has not set aside any money for tax — and worries about a surprise bill. Likely cause: They think tax is
complicated — so they avoid it. How to investigate: Ask: "Have you set aside any money for tax? Do you know your tax
obligations?" Corrective steps: Set aside a percentage of every payment. Check current rules on the official site. Talk to a
licensed tax professional if needed. How to confirm resolved: The learner has a set-aside habit and knows where to check.
Prevention: The habit of setting aside is more important than the exact percentage — start today.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
