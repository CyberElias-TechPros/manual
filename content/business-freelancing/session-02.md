# Finding Your Market and Validating an Idea

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Start Right (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with market research)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 1 Offer Sheet (who / problem / deliverable / starting price range). Learners must be able to type or write a few sentences. No prior research knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 1 you wrote a first Offer Sheet — a draft of who you serve, the problem, the deliverable and a price range. Today we test that draft against the real world: does the customer actually have that problem, and will they actually pay that price?

**Preparation before class:** Print the five open questions (Section F.3) on a handout; prepare the "Validation Notes" template (Section H) printed per learner; prepare the competitor price scan sheet; have the FastFix case pack ready; bring a sample completed Validation Notes sheet (the instructor's own example) for the demonstration; confirm learners brought their Session 1 Offer Sheet. Instructor note on tone: Many learners are afraid that validation will "kill their idea". Reframe it: validation saves you from building something nobody wants. A "no" today is cheaper than a "no" after you have spent your savings. Keep the mood curious, not fearful.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Write a clear customer-problem statement in one sentence and describe a specific target customer.
2. Narrow "everybody" into a niche and explain why a niche is easier to serve and to find.
3. Describe at least four free market research methods: market walks, competitor price checks, online search (Google, Jiji,
Facebook, WhatsApp status) and respectful listening in WhatsApp groups.
4. Ask five open questions in a customer conversation, listen more than they talk, and record answers honestly — not as they
hoped.
5. Complete a competitor price scan with at least five real observed prices and their sources.
6. Apply a simple decision rule — problem real? skill adequate? willingness to pay? competition survivable? — to make a
reasoned go / adjust / stop decision.
7. Produce a Validation Notes sheet with three recorded customer conversations and a competitor scan (LO3, LO4).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two short "idea statements" without labelling them.
Idea A: "I want to start a food business. I will sell rice, beans, jollof and small chops. I will sell to everybody in Port Harcourt.
I think it will make a lot of money."
Idea B: "I want to sell fresh jollof rice to office workers around Trans-Amadi and GRA who have no time to cook lunch and
are tired of the same old buka food. I will deliver a hot plate for ₦1,500, ordered by 10 a.m., delivered by 1 p.m."
What the instructor says:
"Two food businesses. Hands up — which one would you bet ₦10,000 on? … Idea B, almost always. Why? Because B
knows exactly who the customer is, what bothers them, what they get, what it costs and when it arrives. A is a wish; B is a
plan. Today we learn how to turn an Idea A into an Idea B — and then how to check whether Idea B is actually true before
you spend your money."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B tells me who", "B has a price", "B solves a real problem", "A is too wide".
Accept all answers and draw out the difference: specific vs vague.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you have an idea that is still an Idea A — a wish?" Most hands go up. Say:
"Good. That is normal. Every good business starts as a wish. The difference between a wish and a business is evidence —
and evidence is free to collect. Today you learn how to collect it."
Why this opener works: It uses a business shape everyone understands (food), it isolates specificity as the variable, and it
frames validation as a money-saving activity rather than a creativity-killing one.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four parts of an Offer Sheet from Session 1?"
Expected answer: Who we serve / The problem / What we sell / Starting price range.
Common gap: learners remember the parts but not the order or the purpose. Clarify: "The Offer Sheet is a draft — today
we test it."
Question 2: "Why is 'everybody' a bad customer?"
Expected answer: You cannot find everybody, price for everybody or serve everybody; a specific customer is findable and
describable.
Common gap: some learners still write "everybody" because it feels safe. Clarify: "A niche is not a limitation — it is a focus.
You can widen later."
Question 3: "What are the three routes to income?"
Expected answer: Freelancing (time and skill), service (repeatable service), product (goods).
Common gap: learners confuse the routes with the Offer Sheet. Clarify: "The route is the shape of the business; the Offer
Sheet is the description of the offer."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have a draft Offer Sheet. Today we do not change it — we test it. We go to the market, we ask real people, we check
what competitors charge, and then we decide: sell, adjust or park. That decision — made with evidence, not hope — is
what turns a wish into a business."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the four parts of the Offer Sheet, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the
board before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What validation is and why it saves you money
The idea. Validation is the process of checking whether your business idea is true before you spend serious money on it. You
test the problem, the customer and the price with free research and real conversations.
Plain-English definition. Validation is asking the world — politely and cheaply — "Is this real? Will people pay?" before you bet
your savings on the answer.
Why it exists. Most small businesses fail not because the owner is lazy, but because they build something nobody asked for.
They spend money on stock, a shop, a logo — and then discover the customer does not exist, or will not pay. Validation moves
that discovery to the beginning, when it is still free.
What it does:
Confirms the problem is real and painful enough to pay for.
Confirms the customer exists and is findable.
Confirms the price is acceptable — or reveals it is not.
Reveals the competition and whether you can survive it.
Gives you evidence you can use in your plan, your pitch and your marketing.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader who wants to sell a new product. The lazy way: buy ₦50,000 of stock, put it on
the table, and hope. The smart way: ask five regular customers, "Would you buy this? How much would you pay?" If three say
yes at a good price, buy a small quantity first. If they say no, you have saved ₦50,000. Validation is the second way.
Important limitation / misconception. Validation does not guarantee success. It reduces risk. A "go" decision means "worth
trying with a small first step" — not "guaranteed to make millions". A "stop" decision means "this idea, as described, is not
worth your money" — not "you are a failure".
How a learner recognises a validated idea: They can point to at least three real people who confirmed the problem, at least
five real competitor prices, and a decision made with reasons — not a feeling.
F.2 The customer-problem statement and the niche
The idea. Before you research, you must be able to say exactly who has the problem and what the problem is. A customer-
problem statement is one sentence that names a specific customer and their specific pain.
Plain-English definition. A customer-problem statement is a single sentence: "[Specific customer] struggles with [specific
problem] and needs [specific outcome]."
The anatomy — with a worked example for FastFix:
Customer: "Office workers and students around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi"
Problem: "whose phone screen or battery fails during the workday"
Outcome: "and who need it fixed the same day without losing their data."
Full statement: "Office workers and students around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi struggle with phone screens and batteries
that fail during the workday, and they need same-day repair without losing their data."
Why a niche matters — the "everybody" trap:
"Everybody" is not a customer; it is a crowd. You cannot find everybody, you cannot speak to everybody's problem, and you
cannot price for everybody.
A niche is a specific group with a specific problem in a specific place. It is easier to find, easier to serve, and easier to charge.
You can always widen later — but you must start narrow enough to learn.
Worked example — narrowing "everybody" to a niche:
Weak: "I sell food to everybody."
Better: "I sell food to students."
Niche: "I sell affordable lunch to students around the University of Port Harcourt who have no time to cook and are tired of
the same buka food."
The niche tells you: where to find them (campus), what they want (affordable, fast, different), and what to charge (student
budget).
Real-life comparison. Think of two churches. Church A says "everybody is welcome" — and has no idea who is in the
building. Church B says "we serve young families in this estate" — and knows exactly what to preach, what time to start, and
what to put on the flyer. Both are churches; one is focused. Focus is not exclusion — it is clarity.
Misconception to correct. "A niche limits my market." No — a niche makes your first market findable. Once you serve the
niche well, you can widen to the next group. Every big business started as a niche.
F.3 Free market research — four methods that cost nothing
The idea. You do not need a paid survey or a consultant. You need four free methods, used together, to see your market clearly.
Method 1 — The market walk.
What it is: Physically walk where your customer is and observe.
What to look for: Who is there? What are they buying? What are the prices? What is the competition doing? What is
missing?
FastFix example: Chidi walks around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi at lunchtime. He counts phone repair stands, notes their
prices on a piece of paper, watches how customers behave, and asks one or two people what they wish existed.
Why it works: You see reality, not what people tell you. A customer may say "I would pay ₦5,000" — but the market walk
shows what they actually pay.
Method 2 — The competitor price check.
What it is: Find out exactly what your competitors charge, where they are, and what they offer.
How to do it: Visit their shop, check their WhatsApp status, look at their Facebook page, search Jiji or Google. Write down:
business name, location, price, what is included, what is missing.
FastFix example: Chidi finds three other repair shops near Mile 3. He notes: Shop A charges ₦4,500 for a screen repair but
has no warranty; Shop B charges ₦6,000 with a 30-day warranty; Shop C charges ₦3,500 but uses cheap parts. Now he
knows the market — and where he can be different.
Why it works: You cannot price in a vacuum. The market walk and the price check together show you the real range and
the real gaps.
Method 3 — Online search and social listening.
What it is: Use Google, Jiji, Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp to see what customers are saying and what competitors are
doing.
How to do it: Search "phone repair Port Harcourt" on Google. Look at the results — who appears, what do they charge,
what do customers say in the comments? Check Jiji for phone repair listings. Look at WhatsApp status of repair shops near
you. Read the comments — customers complain and praise publicly.
Why it works: Customers tell you the truth in comments and messages. A competitor's Facebook page with 500 comments
is a free research report.
Method 4 — Respectful listening in WhatsApp groups.
What it is: Join groups where your customer already is — and listen before you sell.
How to do it: Join a church group, a student group, a market association group, a business group. Do not post your offer.
Instead, listen: what problems do people mention? What do they ask for? What do they complain about? When a relevant
problem appears, you can answer helpfully — and learn.
Why it works: People say things in groups they would never say in a formal interview. You hear the real language of the
problem.
Important rule: Never spam a group with your offer. Listen first, help second, sell third — and only when it is genuinely
relevant.
Real-life comparison. Think of a detective. A detective does not guess — they gather clues from the scene (market walk), the
other suspects (competitors), the public records (online search) and the witnesses (WhatsApp groups). Validation is detective
work — and it costs nothing but time.
Misconception to correct. "Market research is for big companies." No — it is for anyone who does not want to lose money.
The cheapest research is the one that saves you from a bad decision.
F.4 The five open questions — how to talk to customers
The idea. The heart of validation is a conversation with a real potential customer. But most people do it wrong: they pitch
instead of ask. The fix is five open questions — questions that cannot be answered with yes or no.
The five open questions — teach them one by one:
1. "Tell me about the last time you had this problem."
Why it works: It gets a real story, not an opinion. You learn how often the problem happens and how it feels.
Example: "Tell me about the last time your phone screen broke."
2. "What did you do about it, and how much did it cost you?"
Why it works: It reveals the current solution and the real price — the two most important facts in validation.
Example: "What did you do when your screen broke, and how much did you pay?"
3. "What was the most frustrating part of that experience?"
Why it works: It finds the pain you can remove. The pain is your selling point.
Example: "What was the most frustrating part — the cost, the time, the quality, or something else?"
4. "If you could wave a magic wand and fix this perfectly, what would the solution look like?"
Why it works: It lets the customer design your offer for you. You learn what "good" means to them.
Example: "If you could wave a magic wand, what would the perfect phone repair look like — price, time, quality,
warranty?"
5. "How much would you expect to pay for that?"
Why it works: It tests the price without you guessing. You learn the customer's real budget.
Example: "How much would you expect to pay for a same-day screen repair with a warranty?"
The golden rules of the customer conversation:
Listen more than talk. Aim for 70% listening, 30% asking. The customer is the teacher.
Do not pitch while listening. This is not a sales call. You are gathering information, not closing a deal.
Record answers honestly. Write what they said — not what you hoped they would say. If they say "I would never pay
more than ₦3,000", write that, even if it hurts.
Thank them and ask for one referral. "Do you know one other person who has this problem? Could I talk to them too?"
This is how you get three conversations from one.
Do not argue. If they say your idea is bad, say "Thank you — that is exactly what I needed to hear." Then ask why.
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor. A doctor does not walk in and say "take this medicine". They ask: "Where does it hurt?
When did it start? What have you tried?" Then they diagnose. The customer conversation is a diagnosis — and the diagnosis
comes before the prescription.
Misconception to correct. "I should convince them my idea is good." No — you should discover whether it is good.
Convincing is selling (Session 5). Today is learning.
F.5 The decision rule: go, adjust or stop
The idea. After your research, you make a decision. Not a feeling — a decision based on four questions.
The four questions — ask them in order:
1. Is the problem real? Did at least three people confirm they have this problem, and did they describe it in their own words?
2. Is my skill adequate? Can I actually deliver the solution well enough to make the customer happy?
3. Is there willingness to pay? Did the customer confirm they would pay a price that covers my cost plus profit?
4. Is the competition survivable? Can I differentiate enough — on price, quality, speed, warranty, service — to win
customers?
The three decisions:
GO — all four answers are yes. Start with a small first step.
ADJUST — some answers are no, but the idea can be fixed. Change the customer, the problem, the price or the offer —
then test again.
STOP — the answers are mostly no, and the idea cannot be fixed without a different skill or more capital than you have.
Park it. It is not a failure — it is a lesson.
Worked example — FastFix's validation decision:
Problem real? Yes — Chidi talks to five office workers; four say their phone has failed in the last six months and they paid
₦3,000–₦7,000 to fix it.
Skill adequate? Yes — Chidi has repaired 50+ phones and learned from an experienced technician.
Willingness to pay? Yes — three of the five say they would pay ₦4,000–₦6,000 for a same-day repair with a warranty.
Competition survivable? Yes — the three nearby shops either have no warranty or use cheap parts; Chidi can differentiate
on quality and warranty.
Decision: GO — with a small first step: offer same-day repair with a 30-day warranty at ₦4,500–₦6,500, and test with 10
customers.
Real-life comparison. Think of a farmer. A farmer does not plant in a storm. They check the soil (problem), the seed (skill), the
market price (willingness to pay) and the other farmers (competition) — then they plant. Validation is the farmer's check.
Misconception to correct. "A 'stop' decision means I failed." No — a stop decision means you saved your money and learned
something. Many successful entrepreneurs have a list of stopped ideas behind them. The failure is spending money on an idea
you never tested.
F.6 The Validation Notes sheet — your evidence document
The idea. Validation is only real if you write it down. The Validation Notes sheet is one page that records: the customer-problem
statement, three customer conversations, the competitor scan, and the decision.
The structure — teach it part by part:
1. Customer-problem statement (one sentence).
2. Three customer conversations — for each: who they are, what they said (in their words), what they currently pay, what
they would pay.
3. Competitor price scan — at least five competitors: name, location, price, what is included, what is missing.
4. Decision — go / adjust / stop, with reasons.
Worked example — a completed Validation Notes sheet for a fictional "Mama Chika's Kitchen":
Customer-problem statement: "Office workers around Trans-Amadi and GRA struggle with finding fresh, hot, affordable lunch
near their workplace, and they need a reliable option that does not taste like the same old buka food."
Conversation 1: "I am a bank worker in GRA. I buy lunch every day. The buka near my office is always the same — rice and
stew, rice and stew. I would love something different, maybe jollof with grilled chicken. I pay ₦1,000–₦1,500 now. I would
pay ₦1,500–₦2,000 if it is fresh and hot."
Conversation 2: "I am a student near Trans-Amadi. I have no time to cook. I buy from the woman at the junction, but sometimes
the food is cold. I pay ₦800–₦1,200. I would pay ₦1,500 if it is delivered hot to my hostel."
Conversation 3: "I am a market woman in Mile 3. I need lunch I can eat quickly. I pay ₦1,000. I would pay ₦1,500 if it is
packaged well and I can eat it fast."
Competitor scan:
Competitor Location Price What is included What is missing
Buka A GRA ₦1,000 Rice, beans, stew No variety, no delivery
Buka B Trans-Amadi ₦1,200 Rice, jollof, small chops No delivery, slow service
Mama Chika (current) Home ₦1,500 Jollof, grilled chicken No consistent marketing
Restaurant C GRA ₦2,500 Full meal Too expensive for daily lunch
Food seller D Mile 3 ₦800 Rice, stew No variety, no packaging
Decision: GO — the problem is real (three people confirmed), the skill is adequate (Mama Chika has cooked for events),
willingness to pay is confirmed (₦1,500–₦2,000), and the competition has gaps (no delivery, no variety, no packaging). First
step: sell 20 plates in week one to office workers and students, collect feedback, adjust.
Why this sheet matters: It is the evidence behind your business plan. In Session 9, you will show this sheet to prove your idea is
not a guess. It is also the document you will update as you learn more.
Misconception to correct. "I will remember what people said." No — you will forget. Write it down. The sheet is your memory
and your evidence.
F.7 Summary of the session
Validation is checking whether your idea is true before you spend serious money.
A customer-problem statement names a specific customer and their specific pain in one sentence.
A niche is a specific group with a specific problem in a specific place — it makes your first market findable.
Four free research methods: market walk, competitor price check, online search and social listening, respectful
listening in WhatsApp groups.
The five open questions — ask, listen, record honestly, do not pitch.
The decision rule: problem real? skill adequate? willingness to pay? competition survivable? → go / adjust / stop.
The Validation Notes sheet is your evidence document — three conversations, a competitor scan, and a decision.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Show the class how to run a customer conversation and record it on the Validation Notes sheet — live — so they can do
it themselves.
Setup: Open a blank Google Doc titled "Validation Notes — [Instructor's example]". Have the five open questions on the
projector. Invite a volunteer (or use a planted learner) to play a customer — e.g., an office worker looking for lunch.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A blank Validation Notes template on the projector.
The five open questions printed or projected.
A volunteer briefed to play a realistic customer (or the instructor plays both roles, switching chairs).
G.2 Demonstration 1 — the customer-problem statement (2 minutes)
Say: "Before we talk to anyone, we write the statement we are testing."
Write on the projector:
"Office workers around Trans-Amadi and GRA struggle with finding fresh, hot, affordable lunch near their workplace, and
they need a reliable option that does not taste like the same old buka food."
Say: "This is what we are testing. If the customer says 'no, that is not my problem', we adjust the statement. The statement is a
hypothesis, not a fact."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — the customer conversation (8 minutes)
Say: "Now I ask the five open questions. Watch how I listen more than I talk."
Instructor (as researcher): "Thank you for talking to me. Tell me about the last time you had this problem — finding fresh,
hot lunch near your office."
Volunteer (as customer): "Last week. I was so hungry, and the buka near my office had only rice and beans. I ate it, but I was
not happy. I wanted something different."
Instructor: "What did you do about it, and how much did it cost you?"
Volunteer: "I ate the rice and beans. It was ₦1,000. I also bought a bottle of water for ₦200."
Instructor: "What was the most frustrating part of that experience?"
Volunteer: "The same food every day. And sometimes the food is cold by the time I get it."
Instructor: "If you could wave a magic wand and fix this perfectly, what would the solution look like?"
Volunteer: "Hot jollof with grilled chicken, delivered to my office, not too expensive."
Instructor: "How much would you expect to pay for that?"
Volunteer: "Maybe ₦1,500 to ₦2,000. Not more than that."
Instructor: "Thank you — that is exactly what I needed. Do you know one other person who has this problem?"
Volunteer: "Yes, my colleague. I can give you her number."
Say to the class: "Notice what I did not do. I did not say 'I sell jollof for ₦1,500, would you buy it?' I asked, I listened, I recorded.

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Emeka is a young man in Port Harcourt who noticed that many small businesses around him — salons, food sellers,
phone repair shops — have terrible photos of their work. He thinks he can make money taking professional photos of their
products and services with his phone. He has a good smartphone and has taken nice photos before. He has not done any
research. He is thinking of buying a ring light and a small backdrop (₦15,000) and printing flyers.
Relevant facts.
Emeka has taken photos for his sister's food business — the photos helped her get more orders.
He has not talked to any business owner about whether they would pay for photos.
He does not know what photographers in Port Harcourt charge.
He has ₦20,000 saved.
Decision point. Should Emeka buy the ring light and print flyers now?
Possible approaches.
1. Buy the equipment and print flyers — invest before testing.
2. Do nothing — the idea is too risky.
3. Validate first: talk to 10 business owners, check what photographers charge, test with 3 free or cheap jobs.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Emeka's idea may be good — but he does not know if business owners will pay, or how
much. Buying equipment and printing flyers before validating is the most common way beginners lose money.
Reasoning / example validation plan for Emeka:
Customer-problem statement: "Small business owners in Port Harcourt struggle with getting good photos of their
products and services, and they need affordable photos that make their business look professional online."
Five open questions: Ask 10 business owners: "Tell me about the last time you needed photos of your business. What did
you do? How much did it cost? What was frustrating? What would perfect look like? How much would you pay?"
Competitor scan: Check 5 photographers on Instagram and Jiji. Note their prices (₦5,000–₦30,000 per session), what is
included, and what is missing.
Test: Offer 3 free or cheap jobs to build a portfolio and testimonials.
Decision: If at least 5 of 10 say yes and would pay ₦5,000+, GO — with a small first step. If most say no or "I use my
phone", ADJUST — maybe the customer is different, or the price is wrong, or the service is different (e.g., "photos + social
media posts").
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (buy first) risks ₦15,000 on an untested idea — and the equipment may sit unused.
Approach 2 (do nothing) loses a possible income — but costs nothing.
Approach 3 (validate first) costs only time and conversation — and either confirms the idea or saves the money.
Professional connection. Every successful business — from a one-person photography service to a big company — starts
with understanding the customer. The ones that skip this step are the ones that fail quietly. Validation is not a delay — it is the
fastest path to a real business.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Pitching instead of asking
Symptoms: The learner's "conversation" is a sales pitch; the customer says yes to everything. Likely cause: The learner is
nervous and wants to sell, not learn. How to investigate: Ask: "What questions did you ask? What did the customer say — in
their words?" Corrective steps: Return to the five open questions. Remind them: "You are a detective, not a salesman. Today
you gather evidence." How to confirm resolved: The learner can repeat the customer's actual words, not a sales pitch.
Prevention: Practise the five open questions in pairs before any real conversation.
K.2 Recording what you hoped, not what they said
Symptoms: The Validation Notes say "the customer loved it and would pay anything" — but the conversation was vague.
Likely cause: The learner heard what they wanted to hear. How to investigate: Ask: "What exactly did they say? Read me the
sentence." Corrective steps: Re-record with exact words. If the customer was vague, mark it as "unclear" — do not invent
enthusiasm. How to confirm resolved: The notes contain direct quotes, not interpretations. Prevention: Write the customer's
words in quotation marks. If you cannot quote it, you did not record it.
K.3 Talking to the wrong people
Symptoms: The learner asks their friends and family, who say "great idea!" — but strangers are not interested. Likely cause:
Friends and family are kind, not honest. They are not the customer. How to investigate: Ask: "Who did you talk to? Are they the
customer you described?" Corrective steps: Talk to real potential customers — people in the niche, not people who love you.
How to confirm resolved: The learner has talked to at least three people who fit the customer description. Prevention: The
customer-problem statement defines who to talk to — stick to it.
K.4 No competitor scan
Symptoms: The learner has conversations but no idea what competitors charge. Likely cause: They think competitors are not
relevant — "I am different." How to investigate: Ask: "Where do people currently go to solve this problem? What do they
pay?" Corrective steps: Do the market walk and the price check. Find at least five competitors. How to confirm resolved: The
scan has five entries with prices and sources. Prevention: You cannot price or position without knowing the competition.
K.5 A "go" decision with no evidence
Symptoms: The learner decides "go" but cannot point to three conversations or five prices. Likely cause: Excitement, not
evidence. How to investigate: Ask: "What evidence do you have? What would make you say adjust or stop?" Corrective steps:
Return to the four questions. If the evidence is missing, the decision is "adjust" (do more research) — not "go". How to
confirm resolved: The decision is backed by specific evidence. Prevention: The decision rule requires evidence for all four
questions — no evidence, no "go".
K.6 A "stop" decision that is really fear
Symptoms: The learner says "stop" after one vague conversation. Likely cause: Fear of rejection, not evidence. How to
investigate: Ask: "How many people did you talk to? What did they actually say?" Corrective steps: If the evidence is thin, the
decision is "adjust" (talk to more people) — not "stop". How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain what evidence
would change their mind. Prevention: A stop decision requires multiple conversations that confirm the problem is not real —
not one awkward chat.
K.7 Asking leading questions
Symptoms: "Wouldn't you love a service that...?" "Don't you think this is a good idea?" Likely cause: The learner wants
confirmation, not truth. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you ask? Was it a yes/no question?" Corrective steps: Replace
leading questions with open questions. "Tell me about..." not "Wouldn't you..." How to confirm resolved: The learner's
questions cannot be answered with yes or no. Prevention: The five open questions are the script — use them.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
