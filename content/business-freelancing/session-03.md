# Registration, Legal and Money Basics

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Start Right (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with legal and money systems)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 2 Validation Notes (customer-problem statement, three conversations, competitor scan, decision). Learners must be able to type or write a few sentences. No prior legal or accounting knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 2 you validated your idea — you have evidence that the problem is real and customers will pay. Today we put the foundation under that idea: a legal identity, a tax number, a separate bank account, and clean money habits. Without these, a business is a hobby with stress.

**Preparation before class:** Print the CAC steps summary (Section F.2) — labelled "example only — verify current requirements on cac.gov.ng"; print the money separation methods sheet; print the daily sales and expense sheet template; have the FastFix case pack ready; prepare a sample Google Sheets money tracker on the projector; confirm learners have a Gmail account (or help them create one at the break). Instructor note on tone: Legal and money topics can feel intimidating or boring. Keep it practical and reassuring: you are not becoming a lawyer or an accountant — you are learning the basics that every business owner needs, and you are learning where to check current rules rather than memorising them. Emphasise that the course never asks learners to pay registration fees — they check the official site themselves.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Explain what CAC registration does and the difference between a business name and a limited company.
2. Outline the broad steps of CAC registration and state where to check current requirements and fees (cac.gov.ng) — never
from memory or a roadside agent.
3. Explain what a TIN is, why clients and banks ask for it, and where to get one.
4. Explain why a separate business bank account matters and describe at least two practical money separation methods (two
accounts, or an envelope system).
5. Name at least two permits or regulators that may apply to specific businesses (e.g., local government, NAFDAC for food)
and state where to confirm them.
6. Set up a basic record-keeping sheet (daily sales and daily expenses) and make three example entries.
7. Score at least 70% on the Session 3 knowledge check (LO5).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two short "money stories" without labelling them.
Story A: "Chidi from FastFix keeps all his money in his personal account. Business money and personal money are mixed.
He does not know if he is making profit. Last month he spent ₦20,000 on business stock and ₦15,000 on family expenses
— but he cannot tell which was which. A customer paid him ₦50,000, and he used ₦30,000 to fix his car. He has no idea
what his business is actually earning."
Story B: "Adaeze from Adaeze Fashion has a separate business account. Every payment from customers goes into it. Every
business expense comes out of it. She keeps a simple record of sales and expenses. At the end of each week, she looks at her
records and knows exactly how much the business made. Last week she saw that her fabric costs were too high, so she
found a cheaper supplier."
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses. Hands up — which one would you rather run? … Story B, almost always. Why? Because Adaeze knows
her numbers. She can see profit, she can find waste, she can make decisions. Chidi is flying blind — and most small
businesses in Nigeria fly blind. Today we learn to see. We learn the legal basics — registration, TIN, permits — and the
money basics — a separate account, money separation, and simple records. By the end of this session, you will have the
foundation of a proper business."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B knows her money", "A is confused", "B can make decisions", "A will run out of
money". Accept all answers and draw out the difference: structure vs chaos.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you know a business that mixed personal and business money — and suffered for
it?" Many hands go up. Say:
"That is not a failure of character — that is a failure of system. And a system is learnable. Today we build it."
Why this opener works: It uses the running example (FastFix) and a contrasting example, it isolates structure as the variable,
and it frames legal and money basics as practical tools rather than boring rules.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What is a customer-problem statement, and what makes a good one?"
Expected answer: One sentence naming a specific customer and their specific problem. A good one is specific — not
"everybody".
Common gap: learners remember the term but not the specificity. Clarify: "A good statement could be tested by talking to
real people."
Question 2: "What are the five open questions?"
Expected answer: (1) Tell me about the last time you had this problem. (2) What did you do about it, and how much did it
cost? (3) What was the most frustrating part? (4) If you could wave a magic wand, what would the solution look like? (5)
How much would you expect to pay?
Common gap: learners remember two or three. Clarify: "The five questions are your script — they work for any business."
Question 3: "What is the decision rule for validation?"
Expected answer: Problem real? skill adequate? willingness to pay? competition survivable? → go / adjust / stop.
Common gap: learners remember "go / adjust / stop" but not the four questions. Clarify: "The four questions are the
evidence; the decision is the conclusion."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have validated your idea — you have evidence. Now we put the foundation under it. A business needs three things
to be proper: a legal identity (so the government and customers know you exist), a tax number (so you can pay your
share and prove you are legitimate), and clean money habits (so you know if you are making profit). Today we learn all
three."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five open questions, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the board
before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why legal and money basics matter
The idea. A business can run for a while without registration, a TIN or a separate account — but it will hit a wall. The wall might
be a customer who asks for your TIN, a bank that won't open an account without registration, a tax demand you did not expect,
or your own confusion about whether you are making profit. The basics prevent the wall.
Plain-English definition. Legal and money basics are the minimum structure that makes a business real to the government, the
bank, the customer — and to you.
Why they matter — four reasons:
1. Customers trust a registered business. A business name on your invoice, a TIN on your receipt — these signal that you
are legitimate and can be held accountable.
2. Banks require structure. To open a business account, a bank will ask for registration and a TIN. Without them, you are
stuck with a personal account.
3. Taxes are easier when you are organised. A registered business with records can plan for taxes. An unregistered business
with no records is always at risk of a surprise demand.
4. You can see profit. A separate account and simple records tell you the truth about your money — the most important
information in business.
Real-life comparison. Think of a driver. A driver can drive without a licence for a while — but they are always at risk of a fine,
an accident they cannot claim for, or a police stop that ends their journey. Registration, a TIN and a separate account are the
business licence — they let you drive properly.
Important limitation / misconception. "Registration makes my business successful." No — registration makes your business
legitimate. Success still depends on your offer, your customers and your work. Registration is the foundation, not the house.
F.2 CAC registration — what it does and how it works
The idea. The Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC) is the government body that registers businesses in Nigeria. Registration
gives your business a legal identity — a name that is officially yours.
What registration does:
Gives your business a legal name that is recognised by government, banks and customers.
Allows you to open a business bank account.
Enables you to get a TIN (Tax Identification Number).
Makes your business visible to customers and partners.
Is often required for government contracts, grants and support schemes.
Business name vs limited company — the key difference:
Business name: Simpler, cheaper, faster. Registered by one person (or a few). The owner and the business are legally the
same — the owner is personally liable for the business's debts. Good for most small businesses and freelancers starting out.
Limited company: More complex, more expensive, more formal. The company is a separate legal person — the owners are
generally not personally liable for the company's debts. Requires more paperwork, annual returns, and often a lawyer or
accountant. Good for businesses that plan to grow large, take on partners, or need limited liability.
The broad steps — teach them as a sequence (example only — verify current requirements on cac.gov.ng):
1. Check name availability — search the CAC portal to see if your chosen business name is available.
2. Prepare your information — you will need personal details (name, address, ID), the business name, the business address,
and the nature of the business.
3. Complete the application — online via the CAC portal or at a CAC office.
4. Pay the fee — the current fee is checked on the official site (never from memory or a roadside agent).
5. Receive your certificate — the CAC issues a certificate of registration with your business name and registration number.
Where to check current requirements and fees:
Official CAC website: cac.gov.ng
CAC office: visit in person if you need help.
Never rely on a WhatsApp forward, a roadside "agent" who promises to "do it fast", or a friend's memory of the fee.
Requirements and fees change — check the official source.
Real-life comparison. Think of registration like a national ID card. You can live without one for a while — but you cannot open
a bank account, vote, or prove who you are. A business registration is the ID card for your business.
Misconception to correct. "Registration is only for big companies." No — a sole trader selling at the market can and should
register a business name. It is cheaper and simpler than most people think, and it opens doors.
Say this to the class:
"We are not asking you to register today. We are teaching you what registration is, why it matters, and where to check the
current requirements. When you are ready — and only when you are ready — you will check the official site and decide.
The course never asks you to pay a registration fee to anyone."
F.3 TIN — your tax identification number
The idea. A Tax Identification Number (TIN) is a unique number assigned to you (or your business) for tax purposes. It is how
the tax authority knows you exist and tracks your tax obligations.
What a TIN does:
Identifies you to the Federal Inland Revenue Service (FIRS) and state tax authorities.
Is required by many banks to open a business account.
Is often requested by corporate clients who need to remit tax on your behalf (withholding tax).
Allows you to file tax returns and stay compliant.
Why clients and banks ask for it:
Banks need it to open a business account and to report your transactions to the tax authority.
Corporate clients may need it to process your invoice and remit withholding tax — if you do not have a TIN, they may
refuse to pay you or deduct a higher rate.
Government uses it to track who is paying tax and who is not.
How to get a TIN:
Apply through the FIRS (Federal Inland Revenue Service) or your state tax authority.
The process and requirements change — check the official FIRS or state tax website for current information.
A business name registration from CAC often makes the TIN process easier.
Real-life comparison. Think of a TIN like a phone number for tax. Just as people need your phone number to reach you, the tax
authority needs your TIN to reach you — and you need it to prove you are a real, tax-compliant business.
Misconception to correct. "A TIN means I will pay a lot of tax." No — a TIN means you are identified and can plan for tax.
Many small businesses have thresholds below which they pay little or nothing — but the threshold changes, and you check the
current rules on the official site. The TIN is not a tax bill; it is an identity.
F.4 Money separation — the most important habit in small business
The idea. The single most common money mistake in small business is mixing personal and business money. When they are
mixed, you cannot see profit, you cannot plan for tax, and you cannot make decisions. Money separation is the habit of keeping
them apart.
Why it matters — three reasons:
1. You can see profit. If all business money is in one place, you can subtract expenses from income and see the truth.
2. You can plan for tax. When business money is separate, you can set aside a percentage for tax without touching your
personal spending money.
3. You can make decisions. Clean records tell you which services are profitable, which customers pay on time, and where you
are wasting money.
Method 1 — Two accounts (the best method):
Open a separate business bank account (requires CAC registration and a TIN).
All business income goes into the business account.
All business expenses come out of the business account.
You pay yourself a "salary" or "drawing" from the business account to your personal account — a fixed amount, on a fixed
day.
The business account is for the business; the personal account is for you. They never mix.
Method 2 — The envelope system (for businesses without a bank account):
Use separate physical envelopes, boxes, or pots for business money and personal money.
Label them clearly: "BUSINESS" and "PERSONAL".
All business income goes into the BUSINESS envelope.
All business expenses come out of the BUSINESS envelope.
You move a fixed amount from BUSINESS to PERSONAL as your "salary" — on a fixed day.
Keep a simple record of what goes in and out of each envelope.
The rules of money separation — say them slowly:
1. Never take business money for personal use without recording it as a "drawing".
2. Never pay business expenses from personal money without recording it as "owner's contribution".
3. Always record every movement of money — even ₦500.
4. Review your money every week — five minutes, same day, same time.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader with two pots. One pot is for the business — sales go in, stock comes out. The
other pot is for the home — food, rent, school fees. At the end of the week, the trader looks at the business pot and knows: did
I make profit? If the pots are mixed, the trader is guessing — and guessing is how businesses fail.
Misconception to correct. "I do not have enough money to separate." No — separation is not about the amount; it is about
the habit. A trader selling ₦500 of pure water can keep two envelopes. The habit matters more than the amount.
F.5 Permits and regulators — check, don't guess
The idea. Beyond CAC registration and a TIN, some businesses need additional permits or must follow specific regulators. The
key is to check — not to guess, and not to assume you need something you do not.
Common permits and regulators — examples (verify current requirements):
Local government: Many businesses need a local government permit or levy to operate in their area. Check with your local
government council.
NAFDAC: Businesses that make, sell or distribute food, drugs, cosmetics and related products may need NAFDAC
registration or approval.
Sector regulators: Some sectors have specific regulators — e.g., telecommunications, finance, health. If your business is
in a regulated sector, check the relevant regulator.
Signage and physical premises: If you have a shop or signboard, your local government may have rules about signage and
premises.
How to check:
Ask your local government office what permits are required for your type of business.
Check the official website of the relevant regulator (e.g., NAFDAC for food).
Ask other business owners in your area what they have — but verify with the official source.
When in doubt, ask a licensed professional (lawyer, accountant) — but remember, this course teaches awareness, not
professional advice.
Real-life comparison. Think of driving again. You need a licence (CAC), a roadworthiness certificate (permits), and insurance
(sector rules, if applicable). You do not need all of these for every car — but you need to know which ones apply to yours. The
same is true for business.
Misconception to correct. "Permits are only for big shops." No — a food seller may need NAFDAC approval; a local
government levy may apply to a market stall. The size of the business does not remove the need — it changes which permits
apply.
F.6 Basic record-keeping — the daily sales and expense sheet
The idea. Record-keeping is not accounting — it is simply writing down what comes in and what goes out. A daily sales and
expense sheet is the simplest record a business can keep.
What to record — every day:
Sales: Every naira that comes in — cash, transfer, POS. Write the date, the customer (or job), and the amount.
Expenses: Every naira that goes out — stock, transport, data, food, packaging, generator, rent. Write the date, what it was
for, and the amount.
The simple sheet — teach the structure:
Date Description Sales (₦) Expenses (₦) Balance (₦)
1 Oct Screen repair — Customer A 5,000 5,000
1 Oct Transport to market 500 4,500
2 Oct Battery replacement — Customer B 3,000 7,500
2 Oct New battery stock 2,000 5,500
3 Oct Charger sale — Customer C 1,500 7,000
3 Oct Data for WhatsApp 500 6,500
The weekly review — five minutes, every week:
Add up the week's sales.
Add up the week's expenses.
Subtract: sales minus expenses = profit (or loss).
Ask three questions: What sold well? What cost too much? What will I change next week?
Why this matters: The sheet turns your business from a feeling into a number. After four weeks, you can see: "I make ₦15,000
a week, but I spend ₦12,000 — my profit is only ₦3,000. I need to either sell more or spend less." That is a decision you can
only make with records.
Real-life comparison. Think of a student tracking their pocket money. If they write down every naira received and every naira
spent, they know exactly how much they have — and they can plan. If they do not, they run out mid-week and wonder why. A
business is the same — just with bigger numbers.
Misconception to correct. "Record-keeping is for accountants." No — it is for anyone who wants to know the truth about
their money. You do not need to be an accountant to write down what comes in and what goes out.
F.7 Summary of the session
Legal and money basics make a business real to the government, the bank, the customer — and to you.
CAC registration gives your business a legal identity. A business name is simpler and cheaper; a limited company is more
formal and offers limited liability. Check current requirements on cac.gov.ng — never from memory.
A TIN identifies you for tax. Banks and corporate clients ask for it. Check the official FIRS or state tax site for current
information.
Money separation — two accounts or an envelope system — is the most important habit in small business. It lets you see
profit, plan for tax, and make decisions.
Permits and regulators vary by business type and location. Check with your local government and the relevant regulator
— do not guess.
Basic record-keeping — a daily sales and expense sheet and a five-minute weekly review — turns your business from a
feeling into a number.

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Show the class how to set up a simple money tracker in Google Sheets and make example entries — live — so they can
do it themselves.
Setup: Open a blank Google Sheet titled "Money Tracker — FastFix (Sample)". Have the daily sales and expense sheet template
on the projector. Project both.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A blank Google Sheet on the projector.
The daily sales and expense sheet template.
A set of example transactions for FastFix (below).
G.2 Demonstration 1 — set up the sheet (4 minutes)
Say: "We will build a money tracker in Google Sheets. It is free, it is on your phone, and it takes five minutes."
Create the sheet on the projector, narrating each step:
Column A Column B Column C Column D Column E
Date Description Sales (₦) Expenses (₦) Balance (₦)
Say: "Five columns. Date, description, sales, expenses, balance. That is it. You can add more later — but start with these five."
G.3 Demonstration 2 — make example entries (6 minutes)
Enter the following FastFix transactions, narrating each one:
Date Description Sales (₦) Expenses (₦) Balance (₦)
1 Oct Opening balance 10,000
1 Oct Screen repair — Customer A 5,000 15,000
1 Oct Transport to market 500 14,500
2 Oct Battery replacement — Customer B 3,000 17,500
2 Oct New battery stock 2,000 15,500
3 Oct Charger sale — Customer C 1,500 17,000
3 Oct Data for WhatsApp 500 16,500
Say: "Every transaction gets a row. Sales go in the sales column. Expenses go in the expenses column. The balance is the running
total. At the end of the week, I add up sales and expenses — and I know my profit."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — the weekly review (3 minutes)
Say: "Every week — same day, same time — I do a five-minute review."
Show the review on the projector:
Total sales this week: 5,000 + 3,000 + 1,500 = ₦9,500
Total expenses this week: 500 + 2,000 + 500 = ₦3,000
Profit this week: 9,500 − 3,000 = ₦6,500
What sold well: Screen repairs (highest price).
What cost too much: Battery stock (₦2,000 — can I find a cheaper supplier?).
What I will change: Order battery stock in bulk to reduce cost.
Say: "That is a weekly review. Five minutes. It turns records into decisions."
G.5 Demonstration 4 — the money separation plan (2 minutes)
Say: "Now the separation plan. FastFix does not have a separate account yet — so we use the envelope system."
Show the plan on the projector:
Envelope 1 — BUSINESS: All sales go in. All business expenses come out.
Envelope 2 — PERSONAL: Family expenses, food, transport for home.
Every Friday: Move ₦2,000 from BUSINESS to PERSONAL as Chidi's "salary".
Record every movement: Even ₦500.
Say: "When Chidi registers and opens a business account, he will switch to two accounts — but the habit is the same. Separate,
record, review."
G.6 Questions to ask during the demo
"Why do we record every transaction, even ₦500?" (Because small amounts add up — and because you cannot see profit
without the full picture.)
"What is the weekly review for?" (To turn records into decisions — what sold, what cost too much, what to change.)
"What if I do not have a bank account?" (Use the envelope system — the habit is the same.)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ifeanyi runs a small POS (point-of-sale) business in Port Harcourt. He has been running it for two years from a small
stand near a market. He has no CAC registration, no TIN, and no separate account — all his POS transactions go into his
personal account. He keeps no records. Last month, a bank officer told him he needs to regularise his account or it will be
restricted. Ifeanyi is confused and worried. He does not know where to start.
Relevant facts.
Ifeanyi processes about ₦50,000 a month in POS transactions.
He has been using his personal account for both business and family money.
He does not know if he is making profit — he just knows the money "comes and goes".
The bank has asked for his TIN and business registration.
Decision point. What should Ifeanyi do first?
Possible approaches.
1. Ignore the bank — it is probably nothing.
2. Close the account and start fresh with a new bank.
3. Regularise: register his business name, get a TIN, open a separate business account, and start keeping records.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ifeanyi's problem is not the bank — it is that he has been running a business without the
foundation. The bank is simply the first place the missing structure shows up. Regularising will solve the bank issue and give
him the foundation he should have had from the start.
Reasoning / example steps for Ifeanyi:
1. Register his business name with CAC — check the current process and fee on cac.gov.ng.
2. Apply for a TIN through FIRS or the state tax authority — check the official site for current requirements.
3. Open a separate business account — with his CAC certificate and TIN.
4. Move business transactions to the new account — all POS income goes in, all business expenses come out.
5. Start keeping records — a daily sales and expense sheet, and a weekly review.
6. Separate his "salary" — move a fixed amount from the business account to his personal account every week.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (ignore) risks account restriction, missed opportunities, and continued blindness about his profit.
Approach 2 (close and start fresh) does not solve the underlying problem — the new account will have the same issues.
Approach 3 (regularise) takes a few weeks and some effort — but it solves the bank issue, gives him a legal identity, and
puts him in control of his money.
Professional connection. Every bank, every corporate client, and every government agency asks the same question: "Are you a
legitimate business?" The answer is not a feeling — it is a registration number, a TIN, and a separate account. These are not
burdens; they are the keys that open doors.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Mixing personal and business money
Symptoms: The learner cannot say whether they are making profit; money "disappears"; they feel confused about their
finances. Likely cause: All money is in one account or one envelope — no separation. How to investigate: Ask: "Where does
business money go? Where does personal money go? Can you tell me your business profit this month?" Corrective steps: Set
up two accounts or two envelopes. Record every transaction. Review weekly. How to confirm resolved: The learner can state
their business profit for the last week. Prevention: Separate from day one — even if the amounts are small.
K.2 Quoting registration fees from memory
Symptoms: The learner says "CAC registration costs ₦50,000" or "I heard it is ₦100,000" — with no source. Likely cause: They
heard it from a friend, a WhatsApp forward, or a roadside agent. How to investigate: Ask: "Where did you get that number? Is it
from the official CAC website?" Corrective steps: Check the official CAC website (cac.gov.ng) for the current fee. Never quote
from memory. How to confirm resolved: The learner can name the official source and say "verify on cac.gov.ng". Prevention:
The rule is simple: if it is not from the official site, it is not a fact.
K.3 Using a roadside "agent" for registration
Symptoms: The learner says "I have an agent who will do it for me" — at a price. Likely cause: They do not know the official
process, so they trust someone who claims to. How to investigate: Ask: "What is the agent's name? What is their official
accreditation? Have you checked the CAC website yourself?" Corrective steps: Do the registration yourself through the official
CAC portal or office. If you need help, ask a licensed professional — not a roadside agent. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can explain the official steps and has checked the official site. Prevention: The official process is designed to be done by
the business owner. An agent is optional — and often overpriced.
K.4 No records — "I will remember"
Symptoms: The learner has no sales or expense sheet; they guess at their profit. Likely cause: They think record-keeping is for
accountants, or they are too busy. How to investigate: Ask: "What were your sales last week? What were your expenses? What
was your profit?" Corrective steps: Start a daily sales and expense sheet — even on paper. Do a five-minute weekly review.
How to confirm resolved: The learner can state their sales, expenses and profit for the last week. Prevention: Record every
transaction — even ₦500. The habit matters more than the amount.
K.5 Thinking a TIN means a big tax bill
Symptoms: The learner avoids getting a TIN because "I do not want to pay tax". Likely cause: They confuse identification with a
tax bill. How to investigate: Ask: "What do you think a TIN is? What do you think it means for your taxes?" Corrective steps:
Explain: a TIN is an identity, not a bill. It lets you plan for tax and proves you are legitimate. Check current thresholds on the
official FIRS or state tax site. How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain that a TIN is an identity and can name where to
check current tax rules. Prevention: Repeat: a TIN is not a tax bill — it is a key that opens doors.
K.6 Assuming permits are not needed
Symptoms: The learner says "I do not need any permits — I am just small". Likely cause: They do not know which permits
apply to their business type. How to investigate: Ask: "What do you sell? Where do you operate? Have you checked with your
local government?" Corrective steps: Check with the local government and the relevant regulator (e.g., NAFDAC for food). Do
not guess. How to confirm resolved: The learner can name at least one permit or regulator that may apply and where to check.
Prevention: Permits depend on the business type and location — check, don't guess.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What does CAC registration do for a business? What is the difference between a business name and a limited
company?
Q2. Multiple choice. Where should you check the current CAC registration fee? a) A WhatsApp forward from a friend b) A
roadside agent who promises to "do it fast" c) The official CAC website — cac.gov.ng d) A newspaper advertisement
Q3. Matching. Match each term to what it is: (1) CAC registration, (2) TIN, (3) business bank account, (4) money separation. a) A

Q4. True or false. "A TIN means you will pay a lot of tax."
Q5. Explain. Why is a separate business bank account important? Give two reasons.
Q6. Practical. A learner has ₦10,000 in sales and ₦7,000 in expenses this week. What is their profit? What should they do next

Q7. Scenario. Your friend sells cakes from home and keeps all money in her personal account. She says "I do not need a
separate account — I am just small." What would you tell her?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. CAC registration gives a business a legal identity — a name that is officially recognised by government, banks and
customers. A business name is simpler, cheaper and faster — the owner and the business are legally the same. A limited
company is more formal and expensive — the company is a separate legal person, and the owners are generally not personally
liable for its debts.
A2. (c) The official CAC website — cac.gov.ng.
A3. (1) = c, (2) = a, (3) = d, (4) = b.
A4. False. A TIN is an identity, not a tax bill. It lets you plan for tax and proves you are legitimate. Many small businesses have
thresholds below which they pay little or nothing — but the threshold changes, and you check the current rules on the official
site.
A5. Two reasons: (1) You can see profit — all business income and expenses are in one place, so you can subtract and know the
truth. (2) You can plan for tax — business money is separate, so you can set aside a percentage without touching personal
spending money. (Also acceptable: customers and banks trust a business with a separate account; it looks professional.)
A6. Profit = 10,000 − 7,000 = ₦3,000. To make more profit next week, they could: sell more (find more customers, raise prices
if the market allows), or spend less (find cheaper suppliers, reduce waste, cut unnecessary expenses). (Accept any sensible
combination.)
A7. Separation is not about the amount — it is about the habit. Even a small business needs to know if it is making profit. A
separate account (or two envelopes) lets her see profit, plan for tax, and make decisions. Without it, she is guessing — and
guessing is how businesses fail. She can start with two envelopes today, and switch to a bank account when she registers.
