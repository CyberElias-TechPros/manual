# Customer Service and Managing Clients

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Win and Price Clients

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with client management)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 5 pitch and pipeline. Learners must be willing to role-play difficult conversations in class. No prior customer service experience required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 5 you learned to find customers and sell to them. Today we keep them: how to communicate clearly, set expectations, define scope, write a one-page agreement, and handle difficult clients. Keeping a customer is cheaper than finding one — and a happy customer is your best marketing.

**Preparation before class:** Print the communication standards sheet (Section F.2); print the scope and exclusions template (Section F.3); print the one-page agreement template (Section F.5); prepare difficult-client scenario cards (scope creep, unrealistic deadline, late payer, complaint, free-work-for exposure); have the FastFix case pack ready. Instructor note on tone: Many learners think "difficult clients" are rare — they are not. Most small business owners have at least one difficult client story. Normalise it: difficult clients are a normal part of business, and they can be managed — or walked away from — professionally. Keep the mood practical and calm.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Apply four communication standards: reply fast, confirm in writing, use plain language, never leave a customer guessing.
2. Define scope — what is included, what is excluded — and explain why most "difficult client" stories are scope failures.
3. Set expectations in writing: price, timeline, update points, and what they need from the customer.
4. Write a one-page client agreement: parties, scope, price, timeline, payment schedule, cancellation, signatures.
5. Respond correctly to at least three difficult-client situations: scope creep, unrealistic deadline, late payment, complaint, and
free-work-for-"exposure".
6. Explain when to walk away from a client professionally.
7. Score at least 70% on the Session 6 knowledge check (LO8).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two "customer stories" without labelling them.
Story A: "Chidi from FastFix repaired a customer's phone screen. The customer said 'while you are at it, can you also fix my
speaker and clean my phone?' Chidi said 'okay' — but did not agree a price or a timeline. He fixed the speaker for free
because he did not know how to say no. The customer was happy — but Chidi lost money and time. The next customer
asked for the same 'extra' — and Chidi was confused about what he should charge."
Story B: "Adaeze from Adaeze Fashion sewed a dress for a customer. The customer said 'can you also make a matching
headwrap?' Adaeze said 'of course — the headwrap will be ₦2,000, and I will deliver the whole order on Friday.' The
customer agreed. Adaeze made the headwrap, charged for it, and delivered on time. The customer was happy — and
Adaeze was paid for her work."
What the instructor says:
"Two businesses. Hands up — which one handled the situation better? … Story B, almost always. Why? Because Adaeze
defined the scope — she said what was included, what was extra, and what the extra cost. Chidi did not — so he gave
away his work. Today we learn to be Adaeze. You will learn to set expectations, define scope, write a simple agreement,
and handle difficult clients without losing money or sleep."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B charged for the extra", "A was clear", "Chidi was too nice", "Adaeze said the
price". Accept all answers and draw out the difference: clear vs unclear.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you have ever done extra work for a customer — and not been paid for it?" Many
hands go up. Say:
"That is not a failure of kindness — that is a failure of scope. And scope is learnable. Today you learn it."
Why this opener works: It uses a situation everyone recognises (the customer who asks for "one small thing"), it isolates clarity
as the variable, and it frames scope management as a skill that protects both you and the customer.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the four parts of the 60-second pitch?"
Expected answer: Who, problem, deliverable, next step.
Common gap: learners remember the pitch but not the parts. Clarify: "The pitch gets the customer — today we keep
them."
Question 2: "What are the seven steps of the sales conversation?"
Expected answer: Approach, ask, listen, propose, handle objections, agree a next step, follow up.
Common gap: learners remember four or five. Clarify: "The seven steps are your script — the last two are where most sales
are won."
Question 3: "Why is follow-up important?"
Expected answer: Most sales come on the second or third contact — follow-up is where the sale is won.
Common gap: learners think follow-up is pushy. Clarify: "Follow-up is service — you are helping the customer decide."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You can find customers and sell to them. Now you keep them. Keeping a customer is cheaper than finding one — and a
happy customer is your best marketing. Today you learn to communicate clearly, set expectations, define scope, write a
simple agreement, and handle difficult clients. These are the skills that turn a first customer into a repeat customer and a
referral source."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the seven steps, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing them on the board before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why keeping a customer is cheaper than finding one
The idea. Most small business owners spend all their time finding new customers and almost no time keeping the ones they
have. This is backwards. Keeping a customer is cheaper, easier, and more profitable than finding a new one.
The economics — say them slowly:
Finding a new customer takes time, effort and often money — marketing, pitching, following up.
Keeping a customer takes good service, clear communication, and follow-up — almost no money.
A happy customer refers others — so one good customer can become three.
An unhappy customer tells others — so one bad experience can cost you five customers.
The maths of retention:
If you keep 10 customers happy, they refer 3 more — you now have 13.
If you lose 5 customers and have to find 5 new ones, you have spent more time and money to end up where you started.
The business that keeps customers grows faster — and spends less.
Real-life comparison. Think of a bucket with holes. Finding new customers is pouring water in the top. Keeping customers is
plugging the holes. A business that pours water but does not plug holes stays empty. A business that plugs holes and pours
steadily fills up.
Important limitation / misconception. "Customer service is being nice." No — customer service is a system: clear
communication, defined scope, written agreements, and professional handling of problems. Being nice is part of it — but the
system is what makes it work.
How a learner recognises good customer service: They can point to a written agreement, a clear scope, and a professional
response to a difficult situation.
F.2 Communication standards — the four rules
The idea. Most customer problems are communication problems. The customer is confused, feels ignored, or does not know
what is happening. Four communication standards prevent most problems.
The four standards — teach them one by one:
1. Reply fast: Respond to every message within a few hours — even if it is "I will get back to you in one hour." A fast "I am on
it" is better than a slow perfect answer.
FastFix example: Chidi receives a WhatsApp message: "Is my phone ready?" He replies in 5 minutes: "Yes — ready for
collection after 4 p.m." The customer is reassured.
2. Confirm in writing: Never rely on a verbal agreement. Confirm every agreement, price, and timeline in writing — a
WhatsApp message, an email, or a note.
FastFix example: After a phone call, Chidi sends a WhatsApp message: "Confirming: screen repair for iPhone 11, ₦6,500,
ready by 5 p.m. tomorrow. Payment: 50% now, 50% on collection." The customer replies "Confirmed."
3. Use plain language: Write so a customer can understand — no jargon, no ambiguity, no assumptions.
FastFix example: Instead of "The LCD module is faulty," Chidi writes: "Your phone screen is damaged. I will replace it
with a new screen — it will look and work like new."
4. Never leave a customer guessing: If there is a delay, a problem, or a change — tell the customer before they have to ask.
FastFix example: The screen part arrives a day late. Chidi messages the customer: "The part arrived a day late — I am
sorry. Your phone will be ready by 5 p.m. tomorrow, not today. I will give you a 10% discount for the wait." The
customer appreciates the honesty.
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor's office. A good doctor's office calls you before the appointment, explains what will
happen, tells you if the doctor is running late, and follows up after the visit. A bad office keeps you waiting and guessing.
Patients stay with the first — and leave the second.
Misconception to correct. "The customer should understand my process." No — it is your job to communicate in a way the
customer understands. If they are confused, the communication failed — not the customer.
F.3 Scope — what is included, what is excluded, and how to price a change
The idea. Scope is exactly what you will do for the customer — and what you will not do. Most "difficult client" stories are
scope failures: the customer expected something that was never agreed, or the business owner did something that was never
paid for.
The three parts of scope — teach them one by one:
1. What is included: The specific deliverables you have agreed to provide.
FastFix example: "Screen repair includes: replacement screen, labour, 30-day warranty, and return of the old screen."
2. What is excluded: The specific things you will not do — unless the customer pays extra.
FastFix example: "Excludes: water damage, motherboard issues, physical damage beyond the screen, and software
problems."
3. How to price a change: When the customer asks for something outside the scope, you have a clear answer — you quote
for the change.
FastFix example: The customer says "while you are at it, can you fix my speaker?" Chidi replies: "The speaker is not
included in the screen repair — I can fix it for an additional ₦1,500, and it will add one more day. Would you like me to
include it?"
The scope creep trap — and how to avoid it:
Scope creep is when the customer gradually asks for more and more — and you do more and more — without a clear
price.
The trap: you want to be helpful, so you say "okay" to small extras — and they add up to a big loss.
The fix: every time the customer asks for something extra, you say: "That is not included in the original agreement — I can
do it for an additional ₦X. Would you like me to include it?"
This is not rude — it is professional. The customer knows exactly what they are paying for.
Real-life comparison. Think of a building contract. A builder who says "I will build your fence for ₦50,000" and then adds "and
I will also paint it, and add a gate, and extend it by two metres" — all for free — that builder will go broke. A builder who says
"painting is ₦5,000 extra, the gate is ₦10,000 extra, and the extension is ₦15,000 extra" — that builder stays in business.
Misconception to correct. "Saying no to extra work loses customers." No — it protects your relationship. A customer who
respects your boundaries will stay; a customer who does not will leave — and you are better off without them.
F.4 Setting expectations — price, timeline, updates, and what you need
The idea. Most customer anger comes from unmet expectations — not from bad work. If the customer expects the job
tomorrow and you deliver it next week, they are angry — even if the work is excellent. Setting expectations prevents this.
The four expectations — teach them one by one:
1. Price: The exact price — in writing — before the work starts.
FastFix example: "The screen repair is ₦6,500 — 50% before I order the part, 50% on collection."
2. Timeline: The exact delivery date — and what happens if there is a delay.
FastFix example: "Your phone will be ready by 5 p.m. on Friday. If the part arrives late, I will tell you immediately and
give you a new date."
3. Update points: When and how you will update the customer during the work.
FastFix example: "I will message you when the part arrives, when I start the repair, and when it is ready for collection."
4. What you need from the customer: The information, access, or materials you need from them — and by when.
FastFix example: "I need your phone by 10 a.m. on Friday, and I need you to back up your data before handing it over."
Real-life comparison. Think of a restaurant. A good restaurant tells you: "Your food will be ready in 15 minutes. We will bring it
to your table. If it takes longer, we will tell you." A bad restaurant makes you wait 40 minutes with no explanation. You are
happier with the first — even if the food is the same.
Misconception to correct. "The customer should know what to expect." No — it is your job to tell them. If they are surprised,
you did not set expectations.
F.5 The one-page client agreement — your protection and their clarity
The idea. A one-page client agreement is a simple written document that both you and the customer sign. It is not a legal
contract (that comes later) — but it is a clear record of what you agreed. It protects both sides.
The seven parts of a one-page agreement — teach them one by one:
1. Parties: Who is involved — your name and the customer's name.
FastFix example: "This agreement is between FastFix Phone Repair (Chidi Okonkwo) and Mr. Emeka Obi."
2. Scope: What you will do — the specific deliverables.
FastFix example: "FastFix will replace the screen of an iPhone 11 with a quality screen, including labour and a 30-day
warranty."
3. Price: The exact price in naira.
FastFix example: "The total price is ₦6,500."
4. Timeline: The delivery date.
FastFix example: "The repair will be completed by 5 p.m. on Friday, 17 October 2026."
5. Payment schedule: How and when the customer pays.
FastFix example: "Payment: 50% (₦3,250) before the repair, 50% (₦3,250) on collection."
6. Cancellation: What happens if either side cancels.
FastFix example: "If the customer cancels after the part is ordered, the 50% deposit is not refundable. If FastFix cancels,
the deposit is fully refunded."
7. Signatures: Both parties sign and date.
FastFix example: "Chidi Okonkwo (FastFix) — Signature: ______ Date: ______. Mr. Emeka Obi (Customer) — Signature:
______ Date: ______."
Why this matters:
For you: It prevents disputes — the customer cannot say "I thought it was cheaper" or "I did not know when it would be
ready."
For the customer: It gives them confidence — they know exactly what they are getting and what they are paying.
For both: It is professional — it shows you are a real business.
Real-life comparison. Think of a hire purchase agreement. A person buying a fridge on hire purchase signs an agreement that
says: the price, the monthly payments, the timeline, and what happens if they miss a payment. Both sides know the rules. A
one-page agreement is the small version of that.
Misconception to correct. "A signed agreement is only for big contracts." No — it is for any job where money and time are
involved. A ₦5,000 job deserves a one-page agreement as much as a ₦500,000 job.
F.6 Difficult clients — five situations and how to handle them
The idea. Difficult clients are not rare — they are a normal part of business. The good news: most difficult situations can be
managed with a clear, professional response. The even better news: some clients should be walked away from —
professionally.
The five situations — teach each with a response:
Situation 1 — Scope creep: The customer keeps asking for extras without paying.
Response: "I am happy to help with that — it is not included in our agreement, so it will be an additional ₦X. Would you
like me to include it?"
Why it works: You are not refusing — you are quoting. The customer decides.
Situation 2 — Unrealistic deadline: The customer wants it done in an impossible time.
Response: "I understand you need it urgently. I can do it by [realistic date] — or I can do a basic version by [earlier date] if
that helps. Which would you prefer?"
Why it works: You are offering options — not just saying no.
Situation 3 — Late payment: The customer does not pay on the agreed date.
Response: Use the payment-chasing sequence (Session 7): friendly reminder on the due date → follow-up call → firm
written message with the invoice attached → documented escalation.
Why it works: You are polite but firm — you give the customer a chance to pay, but you do not let it slide.
Situation 4 — Complaint: The customer is unhappy with the work.
Response: "I am sorry you are not happy. Tell me exactly what is wrong — I will put it right." Then listen, apologise, and fix
it.
Why it works: You take responsibility and fix the problem — you do not argue.
Situation 5 — Free work for "exposure": The customer asks you to work for free "for the exposure".
Response: "Thank you for thinking of me. I do not work for free — but I can offer you a 10% discount if you pay now, and I
will share the work on my page with a link to yours."
Why it works: You are not rude — you are professional. You offer a paid alternative, not a free one.
When to walk away professionally:
The customer is abusive, disrespectful, or threatening.
The customer refuses to pay after multiple reminders.
The customer demands work far outside the scope — and refuses to pay for it.
How to walk away: "I am sorry we cannot work together on this. I will complete the work we agreed and deliver it — but I
will not be able to continue with additional requests. I wish you well."
Why it works: You are firm, professional, and you do not burn the bridge.
Real-life comparison. Think of a doctor. A doctor cannot refuse emergency care — but a doctor can refuse a patient who is
abusive or refuses to pay. Walking away is not failure — it is professional boundaries.
Misconception to correct. "The customer is always right." No — the customer is always the customer, but they are not always
right. A business owner has the right to set boundaries, charge for work, and walk away from abuse.
F.7 Summary of the session
Keeping a customer is cheaper than finding one — and a happy customer is your best marketing.
Four communication standards: reply fast, confirm in writing, use plain language, never leave a customer guessing.
Scope is what is included, what is excluded, and how to price a change — most difficult clients are scope failures.
Setting expectations: price, timeline, update points, and what you need from the customer.
A one-page agreement has seven parts: parties, scope, price, timeline, payment schedule, cancellation, signatures.
Five difficult-client situations: scope creep, unrealistic deadline, late payment, complaint, free-work-for-exposure — each
has a clear professional response.
Walk away professionally when the customer is abusive, refuses to pay, or demands free work.

G.5 Questions to ask during the demo
"What are the seven parts of the agreement?" (Parties, scope, price, timeline, payment schedule, cancellation, signatures.)

## H. GUIDED PRACTICAL EXERCISE (18 minutes)
<!-- kind:guided_practical -->
Objective: With the instructor guiding, each learner writes a scope, sets expectations, and drafts a one-page agreement for their
own business.
Starting materials: A pen and notebook or phone notes app; the scope template and agreement template (projected and
printed).
Procedure (instructor calls out each step; learners follow; instructor walks the room):
1. Write your name and today's date at the top of your workbook or notes app.
2. Choose a job: Pick one job from your business idea (or use FastFix's screen repair).
3. Set the scope: Fill in the scope template (Worksheet 6.2) — included, excluded, how to price a change.
4. Pause and predict: Ask yourself: "If a customer asked for something extra, would I know what to say?"
5. Set expectations: Fill in the expectations template (Worksheet 6.3) — price, timeline, update points, what you need from
the customer.
6. Write the one-page agreement: Fill in the agreement template (Worksheet 6.4) — all seven parts.
7. Share: Show your agreement to your partner. Ask: "Is this clear? Would you know exactly what you are getting and what
you are paying?"
8. Revise: Based on your partner's feedback, revise your agreement if needed.
9. Practise a difficult-client response: Your partner reads you one of the five difficult-client situations (from a scenario card).
You respond out loud. Your partner gives feedback.
Expected result: A scope, a set of expectations, a one-page agreement, and a practised difficult-client response.
Points to pause on and predict:

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Uche is a freelance writer in Port Harcourt. He writes CVs, cover letters and business proposals for clients. He has been
doing this for six months. He has a problem: a client, "Mr. O," hired him to write a business proposal for ₦15,000. Uche wrote
the proposal and delivered it. Mr. O said "it is good, but can you also write a one-page summary and a PowerPoint
presentation?" Uche said "okay" — and did both, assuming they were included. Mr. O then said "and can you present it to my
partner on Monday?" Uche said "okay" again. After all this, Mr. O paid only ₦15,000 — and said "the extras were just small
things." Uche is frustrated and feels used.
Relevant facts.
Uche's agreement was for "one business proposal" — ₦15,000.
The summary, PowerPoint, and presentation were never discussed in terms of price or scope.
Uche did not confirm the agreement in writing — it was a WhatsApp conversation.
Uche did not quote for the extras — he assumed they were included.
Decision point. What is Uche's real problem?
Possible approaches.
1. Accept it — "I will be more careful next time."
2. Confront Mr. O — "You owe me more money."
3. Learn from it: set scope, confirm in writing, quote for extras — and if Mr. O refuses to pay for the extras, walk away
professionally.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Uche's problem is not Mr. O — it is that Uche did not set scope, confirm in writing, or
quote for extras. He assumed — and assumptions are expensive. The lesson is not to confront Mr. O (though he could ask for a
fair price for the extras) — the lesson is to never let it happen again.
Reasoning / example actions for Uche:
Set scope: "The proposal includes: one written business proposal, up to 2 revisions. Any additional work — summary,
presentation, etc. — is quoted separately."
Confirm in writing: Send a WhatsApp message after every agreement: "Confirming: one business proposal, ₦15,000,
delivered by Friday. Payment: 50% now, 50% on delivery."
Quote for extras: When the customer asks for something extra, reply: "That is not included in our agreement — I can do it
for an additional ₦X. Would you like me to include it?"
Walk away if needed: If the customer refuses to pay for extras or becomes abusive, say: "I am sorry we cannot work
together on this. I will complete the work we agreed and deliver it — but I will not be able to continue with additional
requests."
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (accept it) loses money and learns nothing — the same thing will happen again.
Approach 2 (confront) may get the money — but it is stressful, and the relationship is damaged.
Approach 3 (learn from it) does not recover this money — but it prevents the next loss, which is worth more.
Professional connection. The difference between a hobbyist and a professional is not talent — it is systems. A professional
writer has a scope, a written agreement, and a process for extras. A hobbyist assumes — and assumes are expensive. Uche has
the skill; he just needs the system.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 No scope — "I will just do it"
Symptoms: The customer asks for extras; the learner does them for free; the learner loses money. Likely cause: There is no
defined scope — so everything feels included. How to investigate: Ask: "What exactly did you agree to do? What is included
and what is not?" Corrective steps: Set the scope before the work starts — included, excluded, how to price a change. How to
confirm resolved: The learner can state the scope clearly. Prevention: Never start work without a clear scope.
K.2 No written agreement
Symptoms: The customer says "I thought it was cheaper" or "I did not know when it would be ready." Likely cause: The
agreement was verbal — and verbal agreements are forgotten. How to investigate: Ask: "Did you confirm the agreement in
writing? Can you show me?" Corrective steps: Confirm every agreement in writing — a WhatsApp message, an email, or a
note. How to confirm resolved: The learner has a written agreement for every job. Prevention: Verbal agreements are not
agreements — write them down.
K.3 Doing extras for free
Symptoms: The customer asks for "one small thing"; the learner does it; the learner is not paid. Likely cause: The learner wants
to be helpful — and does not know how to quote for extras. How to investigate: Ask: "What did the customer ask for? What
did you say? What did you charge?" Corrective steps: Quote for every extra — "That is not included — it will be ₦X." How to
confirm resolved: The learner quotes for extras instead of doing them for free. Prevention: Every extra has a price — always.
K.4 Not setting expectations
Symptoms: The customer is angry about the timeline — even though the work is good. Likely cause: The timeline was never
clearly agreed — the customer assumed it would be faster. How to investigate: Ask: "What did you tell the customer about
the timeline? When did you say it would be ready?" Corrective steps: Set the timeline in writing — and update the customer if
there is a delay. How to confirm resolved: The learner can state the agreed timeline and update process. Prevention: Set
expectations before the work starts — and update if anything changes.
K.5 Arguing with a complaint
Symptoms: The customer complains; the learner argues; the relationship breaks. Likely cause: The learner takes the complaint
personally. How to investigate: Ask: "What was the complaint? What did you say?" Corrective steps: Listen, apologise, and fix
the problem — do not argue. How to confirm resolved: The learner responds to complaints with listening and fixing.
Prevention: A complaint is an opportunity to improve — not a personal attack.
K.6 Never walking away
Symptoms: The learner keeps working with abusive or non-paying clients — and feels trapped. Likely cause: The learner thinks
they need every customer — even bad ones. How to investigate: Ask: "How does this client make you feel? Do they pay on
time? Do they respect you?" Corrective steps: Set boundaries — and walk away professionally from clients who cross them.
How to confirm resolved: The learner can name when they would walk away and how. Prevention: Not every customer is a
good customer — walking away is a professional skill.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.
