# Pricing Your Work and Costing

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 2 — Module 2: Win and Price Clients

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with costing and pricing)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** The Session 3 money separation plan and records sheet. Learners must be able to do basic arithmetic (addition, subtraction, multiplication). No prior pricing knowledge required.

**Connection to previous work:** In Session 3 you set up the money foundation — separation, records, legal basics. Today we use that foundation to answer the most important question in business: what should you charge? Most small businesses price by guessing or copying — and lose money on every job. Today you learn to price from cost.

**Preparation before class:** Print the cost breakdown template (Section F.2); print the pricing models sheet (Section F.3); print the quote template (Section F.6); have the FastFix case pack ready; prepare a sample costed price list on the projector; bring a calculator (or use phone calculators). Instructor note on tone: Many learners are afraid of pricing — afraid of charging too high (and losing customers) or too low (and losing money). Reassure them: pricing is not a guess, it is a calculation. When you know your cost, you can defend your price with confidence. Keep the mood practical and numbers-friendly.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Calculate the full cost of a job, including materials, data, transport, tools, packaging, overhead share, waste allowance and
their own time.
2. Choose a pricing model — per hour, per job/day, or package/retainer — and justify the choice with reasons.
3. Compute revenue, cost, profit and margin in naira.
4. Calculate break-even — how much sales value covers fixed costs.
5. Write a clear quote in naira: description, quantity, unit price, total, validity, payment terms and exclusions.
6. Produce a costed price list for three offers with break-even stated (LO6).
7. Score at least 70% on the Session 4 knowledge check.

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show two "price quotes" for the same job — a phone screen repair.
Quote A (weak): "Screen repair: ₦3,000. Come today."
Quote B (strong): "Screen repair for iPhone 11: ₦5,500. Includes: quality screen part, 1-hour labour, 30-day warranty. Valid
for 7 days. 50% payment before repair, 50% on collection. Excludes: physical water damage, motherboard issues."
What the instructor says:
"Two quotes for the same job. Hands up — which one would you trust? … Quote B, almost always. Why? Because B tells
you exactly what you get, what it costs, how long the price lasts, how you pay, and what is not included. A is a number
with no meaning. Today we learn to write quotes like B — and to know what the number should be before you write it.
That is costing and pricing."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "B tells me what I get", "B has a warranty", "B says how to pay", "A is too cheap
— maybe fake". Accept all answers and draw out the difference: costed and clear vs guessed and vague.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you have ever charged too little for a job — and realised later you lost money?"
Many hands go up. Say:
"That is not a failure of honesty — that is a failure of costing. When you do not know your cost, you cannot know your
price. Today you learn to know both."
Why this opener works: It uses a job everyone understands (phone repair), it isolates clarity and costing as the variables, and it
frames pricing as a skill that protects you — not a trick to exploit customers.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector.
Question 1: "What are the five columns of the money tracker from Session 3?"
Expected answer: Date / Description / Sales / Expenses / Balance.
Common gap: learners remember the tracker but not the columns. Clarify: "The tracker is where your cost information
comes from — today we use it to price."
Question 2: "What is money separation, and why does it matter?"
Expected answer: Keeping business money and personal money apart — so you can see profit, plan for tax, and make
decisions.
Common gap: learners remember the term but not the purpose. Clarify: "Separation gives you clean numbers — and clean
numbers are what we price from today."
Question 3: "What is the weekly review?"
Expected answer: Every week, add up sales and expenses, calculate profit, and decide one thing to change.
Common gap: learners skip the review. Clarify: "The review is where you learn from your numbers — today we learn to
price from them."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You have clean money habits. Now we use them. Pricing is not a guess — it is a calculation. You add up everything a job
costs, you add your profit, and that is your price. When you know your cost, you can defend your price, negotiate with
confidence, and never lose money by accident. Let us learn to cost."
If more than a third of the class cannot name the five columns of the money tracker, spend 5 extra minutes reviewing the
tracker on the board before continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 Why most small businesses lose money on pricing
The idea. Most small businesses in Nigeria price by guessing ("what feels right") or copying ("what the next shop charges").
Both methods lose money — because neither knows the actual cost of the job.
The three pricing mistakes:
1. Guessing: "I think a screen repair should be ₦3,000." But what if the part costs ₦2,500 and your time is worth ₦1,000? You
just lost ₦500 — and you do not know it.
2. Copying: "The next shop charges ₦4,000, so I will charge ₦3,500 to be cheaper." But what if their cost is ₦2,000 and yours
is ₦3,000? You are cheaper than them but still losing money.
3. Forgetting costs: "I charge ₦5,000 for a cake." But you forgot the transport, the data, the packaging, and your time. Your
real cost is ₦5,500 — you are paying the customer to buy from you.
The fix — price from cost:
Calculate the full cost of the job — everything.
Add your profit — what you want to earn.
That is your price.
Real-life comparison. Think of a market trader selling rice. If she does not know what she paid for the bag, the transport, and
her time, she might sell at a loss and still think she is making profit. The trader who knows her cost — she can negotiate, she
can plan, she can sleep well. Costing is the difference.
Important limitation / misconception. "If I price from cost, I will charge too much and lose customers." Not necessarily — if
your cost is high because you are inefficient, the answer is to reduce cost, not to guess a lower price. And if your cost is fair,
customers will pay a fair price — especially if you explain the value (warranty, speed, quality).
How a learner recognises a costed price: They can explain their price from its cost — "I charge ₦5,500 because the part is
₦3,500, my time is ₦1,500, and my profit is ₦500."
F.2 The full cost of a job — every component
The idea. The full cost of a job is everything you spend to deliver it — not just the obvious materials. Most beginners count
only materials and forget the rest. The full cost has eight components.
The eight cost components — teach them one by one with a FastFix example (screen repair):
1. Materials: The physical items used in the job.
FastFix example: A replacement screen — ₦3,500.
2. Data and communication: The cost of data for research, communication, and online work.
FastFix example: Data to check the phone model and order the part — ₦200.
3. Transport: The cost of getting to the customer, the market, or the supplier.
FastFix example: Transport to buy the screen from the supplier — ₦500.
4. Tools and equipment: The cost of using your tools — even if you own them. A tool wears out and must be replaced.
FastFix example: Screwdrivers, spudgers, and other tools — a share of ₦100 per job.
5. Packaging: The cost of presenting the product or service.
FastFix example: A clean bag or box for the repaired phone — ₦100.
6. Overhead share: A share of your fixed costs — rent, electricity, generator, phone, internet — allocated to each job.
FastFix example: If monthly overhead is ₦10,000 and he does 20 jobs a month, the overhead share per job is ₦500.
7. Waste allowance: A small percentage for mistakes, damaged parts, or unexpected problems.
FastFix example: 5% of materials — ₦175 (for a screen that arrives damaged or is broken during repair).
8. Your time (labour): The value of your own work — what you would pay someone else to do it.
FastFix example: 1 hour of Chidi's time at ₦1,000/hour — ₦1,000.
The full cost calculation for FastFix's screen repair:
Component Cost (₦)
Materials (screen)
3,500
Data 200
Transport 500
Tools 100
Packaging 100
Overhead share 500
Waste allowance (5% of materials) 175
Your time (1 hour) 1,000
Total full cost 6,075
Say this: "₦6,075 is the true cost of this repair. If Chidi charges ₦4,000 — what many shops charge — he is losing ₦2,075 on
every repair. He is working hard and going backwards. That is the hidden crisis of small business."
Real-life comparison. Think of a baker. The flour and sugar are obvious. But what about the gas, the transport to the market,
the wear on the baking tin, the time? A baker who counts only flour will price too low and wonder why there is no profit. The
full cost is the truth.
Misconception to correct. "My time is free — I do not pay myself." Your time is not free — it is your most valuable resource.
If you do not count it, you cannot know if the job is worth doing. A job that costs ₦6,075 and pays ₦6,075 is not a business —
it is unpaid labour.
F.3 Three pricing models — choose the right one
The idea. A pricing model is how you structure your price. There are three common models — each fits different situations.
Choosing the right one is part of your business strategy.
Model 1 — Per hour (time-based):
What it is: You charge for the time you spend — ₦1,000 per hour, for example.
Best for: Work where the time is uncertain or varies — repairs, consulting, tutoring, writing.
Pros: You are paid for all your time; simple to explain.
Cons: Customers may worry the clock is running; you earn less if you are fast (which is unfair).
FastFix example: Chidi charges ₦1,000 per hour for software repairs that take unpredictable time.
Model 2 — Per job or per day (fixed price):
What it is: You charge a fixed price for a defined job — ₦5,500 for a screen repair, regardless of how long it takes.
Best for: Work with a clear scope and predictable time — repairs, design jobs, baking a cake, sewing a dress.
Pros: The customer knows the cost upfront; you can earn more if you are fast; easy to quote.
Cons: If the job takes longer than expected, you earn less per hour; scope must be clear.
FastFix example: Chidi charges ₦5,500 for a screen repair — fixed, regardless of whether it takes 45 minutes or 1.5 hours.
Model 3 — Package or retainer (bundled):
What it is: You bundle several services or a period of service into one price — a monthly retainer, a package deal.
Best for: Ongoing work — monthly social media management, weekly cleaning, regular maintenance, a "3 repairs for the
price of 2.5" package.
Pros: Predictable income; encourages repeat customers; simpler for the customer.
Cons: Requires trust and a clear scope; you must deliver consistently.
FastFix example: Chidi offers a "Phone Care Package" — ₦10,000 per quarter for unlimited software support, one free
screen protector, and 10% off repairs.
How to choose — a practical decision rule:
If the time is uncertain → charge per hour.
If the scope is clear → charge per job.
If the work is ongoing → offer a package or retainer.
Most businesses use a mix — a fixed price for standard jobs, per hour for uncertain work, and packages for loyal customers.
Real-life comparison. Think of a taxi driver. He can charge per trip (fixed), per hour (time-based), or a monthly contract with a
company (retainer). Each model fits a different situation. A smart driver uses all three — and so does a smart business owner.
Misconception to correct. "One model fits all." No — the right model depends on the job, the customer and your business. A
repair shop that charges per hour for a simple screen repair will lose customers to a shop that charges a fixed price. Choose
deliberately.
F.4 Revenue, cost, profit and margin — the numbers that matter
The idea. Once you know your cost, you can calculate the numbers that tell you if your business is working. Four numbers
matter: revenue, cost, profit and margin.
The four numbers — define each with a FastFix example:
1. Revenue (sales): The total money coming in from sales.
FastFix example: Chidi repairs 10 screens at ₦5,500 each — revenue = ₦55,000.
2. Cost (total cost): The total money going out to deliver the service.
FastFix example: Each repair costs ₦6,075 (from Section F.2) — total cost for 10 repairs = ₦60,750.
3. Profit: Revenue minus cost. The money you keep.
FastFix example: ₦55,000 − ₦60,750 = −₦5,750 (a loss!).
Say this: "Chidi is losing ₦5,750 this month. He is busy, he is working hard — and he is going backwards. This is what
happens when you price without costing."
4. Margin: Profit as a percentage of revenue. It tells you how much of each naira you keep.
Formula: Margin = (Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100
FastFix example (if he priced correctly at ₦7,000): Revenue = ₦70,000, Cost = ₦60,750, Profit = ₦9,250. Margin =
(9,250 ÷ 70,000) × 100 = 13.2%.
Say this: "A 13% margin means for every ₦100 Chidi earns, he keeps ₦13. That is thin — he needs to either raise
prices, reduce costs, or do more jobs."
What is a good margin? There is no single answer — it depends on the business. But a margin below 10% is dangerous (one
bad week wipes you out). A margin of 20–30% is healthy for most small businesses. A margin above 50% is excellent — but
check that you are not undercharging.
Real-life comparison. Think of a student selling pure water. If she sells 100 sachets at ₦20 each, revenue is ₦2,000. If her cost
(bag of sachets, transport, table) is ₦1,500, profit is ₦500 and margin is 25%. She knows she is making money — and she
knows how much. Without these numbers, she is guessing.
Misconception to correct. "Revenue is profit." No — revenue is what comes in; profit is what you keep after costs. A business
with ₦100,000 revenue and ₦95,000 cost has only ₦5,000 profit. Revenue is vanity; profit is sanity.
F.5 Break-even — how much you must sell to cover your costs
The idea. Break-even is the point where your revenue exactly covers your cost — you are not making profit, but you are not
losing money. Knowing your break-even tells you how much you must sell to survive.
The break-even formula:
Break-even (in units) = Fixed costs ÷ (Price per unit − Variable cost per unit)
Fixed costs: Costs that do not change with the number of jobs — rent, electricity, phone, internet.
Variable costs: Costs that change with each job — materials, transport, packaging.
Worked example — FastFix:
Fixed costs per month: ₦10,000 (rent, electricity, phone, internet).
Variable cost per screen repair: ₦5,075 (materials ₦3,500 + data ₦200 + transport ₦500 + tools ₦100 + packaging ₦100 +
waste ₦175 + time ₦1,000 — but time is fixed if Chidi works alone, so we exclude it from variable cost for this calculation;
let us say variable cost = ₦4,075).
Price per screen repair: ₦7,000.
Contribution per repair = Price − Variable cost = 7,000 − 4,075 = ₦2,925.
Break-even (in repairs) = 10,000 ÷ 2,925 = 3.4 repairs — so Chidi must do at least 4 repairs a month to cover his fixed
costs.
Say this: "If Chidi does 4 repairs at ₦7,000, he covers his rent, electricity and phone. Every repair after that is profit. If he does 10,
he makes ₦9,250 profit. Break-even tells him the minimum — and the minimum is what keeps him alive."
Real-life comparison. Think of a bus driver. The bus has fixed costs (loan repayment, insurance, licence) and variable costs
(fuel, maintenance per trip). The driver knows: I must do at least 10 trips a day to cover my fixed costs. Every trip after 10 is
profit. Break-even is the driver's daily target.
Misconception to correct. "Break-even is the same as profit." No — break-even is the point where you stop losing money.
Profit starts after break-even. A business that never reaches break-even is slowly dying.
F.6 Writing a clear quote — the document that prevents disputes
The idea. A quote is a written document that tells the customer exactly what you will do, what it will cost, and the terms. It is
not a contract (that comes in Session 6) — but it is the foundation of trust and the prevention of disputes.
The seven parts of a clear quote — teach them one by one:
1. Business name and contact: Your name, phone number, and (if you have it) your business name and address.
2. Date and quote number: The date and a unique number (e.g., Quote 001) so you can reference it.
3. Customer name: Who the quote is for.
4. Description of the job: What you will do — in clear, specific language.
5. Quantity and unit price: How many, and the price per unit.
6. Total price: The full amount in naira.
7. Terms: Validity (how long the price lasts), payment terms (how and when to pay), and exclusions (what is not included).
Worked example — a clear quote for FastFix:
FastFix Phone Repair Mile 3 Market, Diobu, Port Harcourt Tel: 0803-XXX-XXXX
Quote 001 — Date: 10 October 2026 Customer: Mr. Emeka Obi
Description: Screen repair for iPhone 11 Quantity: 1 Unit price: ₦5,500 Total: ₦5,500
Terms:
This quote is valid for 7 days.
Payment: 50% before repair (₦2,750), 50% on collection (₦2,750).
Includes: quality screen part, 1-hour labour, 30-day warranty.
Excludes: water damage, motherboard issues, physical damage beyond the screen.
Say this: "This quote prevents three disputes: 'I thought it was cheaper' (no — it says ₦5,500), 'I thought the warranty was
longer' (no — it says 30 days), 'I thought you would fix the water damage' (no — it is excluded). A clear quote is not distrust
— it is clarity."
Real-life comparison. Think of a building contract. A builder who says "I will build your fence for ₦50,000" invites disputes. A
builder who writes down the length, the materials, the timeline, the payment schedule and the exclusions — that builder sleeps
well. A quote is the small version of that contract.
Misconception to correct. "A quote is just a price." No — a quote is a price with context. The context is what prevents
disputes and builds trust.
F.7 Summary of the session
Most small businesses lose money on pricing because they guess or copy — they do not know their cost.
The full cost of a job has eight components: materials, data, transport, tools, packaging, overhead share, waste allowance
and your time.
Three pricing models: per hour (uncertain time), per job (clear scope), package/retainer (ongoing work). Choose
deliberately.
Four numbers that matter: revenue, cost, profit, margin. Revenue is vanity; profit is sanity.
Break-even tells you the minimum you must sell to cover fixed costs.
A clear quote has seven parts: business name, date/number, customer, description, quantity/unit price, total, and terms
(validity, payment, exclusions).

## G. INSTRUCTOR DEMONSTRATION (15 minutes)
<!-- kind:demo -->
Goal: Show the class how to cost a job completely, choose a price, and write a clear quote — live — so they can do it
themselves.
Setup: Open a blank Google Doc titled "Cost and Quote — FastFix Screen Repair". Have the cost breakdown template and
quote template on the projector. Project both.
G.1 What the instructor prepares
A blank cost breakdown template on the projector.
A blank quote template on the projector.
A calculator.
G.2 Demonstration 1 — cost the job completely (5 minutes)
Say: "We will cost a screen repair for an iPhone 11. Watch me count everything — not just the screen."
Fill in the cost breakdown on the projector, narrating each component:
Component Cost (₦) How I know
Materials (screen)
3,500 Price from supplier
Data 200 Data to check model and order
Component Cost (₦) How I know
Transport 500 Bus to supplier and back
Tools 100 Share of screwdrivers, etc.
Packaging 100 Bag for the phone
Overhead share 500 ₦10,000 monthly overhead ÷ 20 jobs
Waste allowance (5%) 175 5% of ₦3,500
Your time (1 hour) 1,000 ₦1,000/hour
Total full cost 6,075
Say: "₦6,075 is the true cost. Now — what should Chidi charge?"
G.3 Demonstration 2 — choose a price (3 minutes)
Say: "Chidi wants a profit of ₦1,500 per repair. So: cost ₦6,075 + profit ₦1,500 = ₦7,500. But the market will not pay ₦7,500
— the competitor charges ₦4,000–₦6,000. So Chidi has three options:"
Write the options on the board:
1. Charge ₦7,500 — risk losing customers to cheaper shops.
2. Charge ₦6,500 — a smaller profit (₦425) but competitive.
3. Reduce cost — find a cheaper screen supplier, reduce transport, or increase efficiency — then charge ₦6,000 with a fair
profit.
Say: "The smart answer is option 3 — reduce cost. But for today, let us say Chidi charges ₦6,500. His profit is ₦425 per repair
— thin, but he is learning. As he gets better, his cost drops and his profit grows."
G.4 Demonstration 3 — write the quote (5 minutes)
Say: "Now we write the quote. A clear quote prevents disputes."
Fill in the quote template on the projector:

Quote 001 — Date: 10 October 2026 Customer: Mr. Emeka Obi
Description: Screen repair for iPhone 11 Quantity: 1 Unit price: ₦6,500 Total: ₦6,500
Terms:
This quote is valid for 7 days.
Payment: 50% before repair (₦3,250), 50% on collection (₦3,250).
Includes: quality screen part, 1-hour labour, 30-day warranty.
Excludes: water damage, motherboard issues, physical damage beyond the screen.
Say: "Seven parts. Business name, date/number, customer, description, quantity/unit price, total, and terms. This quote is clear,
honest and professional."
G.5 Demonstration 4 — calculate profit and break-even (2 minutes)
Say: "Last — the numbers. If Chidi does 10 repairs at ₦6,500:"
Revenue: 10 × 6,500 = ₦65,000
Cost: 10 × 6,075 = ₦60,750
Profit: 65,000 − 60,750 = ₦4,250
Margin: (4,250 ÷ 65,000) × 100 = 6.5%
Break-even: Fixed costs ₦10,000 ÷ contribution per repair (6,500 − 4,075 = 2,425) = 4.1 — so 5 repairs to break even.
Say: "A 6.5% margin is thin — Chidi needs to either raise prices, reduce costs, or do more jobs. But now he knows. And
knowing is the first step to fixing."
G.6 Questions to ask during the demo
"What is the full cost of this repair?" (₦6,075 — all eight components.)
"Why is the price ₦6,500 and not ₦6,075?" (Because the price includes profit — ₦6,075 is the cost, ₦6,500 is the price.)
"What prevents a dispute with this quote?" (The seven parts — especially the terms: validity, payment, exclusions.)
"What is break-even?" (The minimum number of repairs to cover fixed costs — 5.)

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Blessing is a tailor in Port Harcourt. She sews dresses, skirts and blouses for women. She has been sewing for three
years — she learned from her mother. She prices by guessing: "I think a dress should be ₦8,000." She does not know her fabric
cost, her transport cost, or the value of her time. She is busy — she sews 10–15 dresses a month — but she never seems to
have money. She is thinking of giving up and getting a shop job.
Relevant facts.
Blessing's time: about 6 hours per dress.
Fabric and materials for a simple dress: about ₦3,500.
Transport to the fabric market: ₦1,000 per trip (she buys fabric for 3–4 dresses per trip).
She works from home — no rent, but she uses her electricity and her sewing machine (which needs occasional servicing).
She charges ₦8,000 per dress — but she does not know if that is profit or loss.
Decision point. Is Blessing's problem that she is not a good tailor?
Possible approaches.
1. Give up sewing and get a shop job.
2. Raise prices dramatically — "I will charge ₦20,000 per dress."
3. Cost her work completely, price from cost, and keep records.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Blessing's problem is not her skill — it is her pricing. She does not know her cost, so she
cannot know if she is making profit. She is busy and broke — the classic symptom of underpricing.
Reasoning / example costing for Blessing:
Materials (fabric, thread, accessories): ₦3,500
Transport (share per dress): ₦1,000 ÷ 3 = ₦333
Tools (sewing machine servicing share): ₦200
Overhead (electricity, phone): ₦300
Waste allowance (5%): ₦175
Time (6 hours × ₦500/hour): ₦3,000
Full cost per dress: ₦7,508
Price at ₦8,000: profit = ₦492 — margin 6.2% (very thin!)
Better price: ₦10,000 — profit = ₦2,492 — margin 24.9% (healthy)
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (give up) loses a skill and three years of experience.
Approach 2 (raise prices dramatically) without costing — she might price herself out of the market.
Approach 3 (cost and price properly) takes one afternoon of calculation — and transforms her business from busy-and-
broke to busy-and-profitable.
Professional connection. The difference between a hobbyist and a professional is not talent — it is knowing your numbers. A
professional tailor can explain their price from its cost. A hobbyist guesses. Blessing has the skill; she just needs the numbers.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 Forgetting your own time
Symptoms: The learner's cost is too low; they are busy but have no money. Likely cause: They did not count their time as a
cost. How to investigate: Ask: "How long does the job take? What is your time worth per hour?" Corrective steps: Add your
time to the cost — at a realistic hourly rate (e.g., ₦500–₦1,000/hour for a skilled beginner). How to confirm resolved: The
learner's cost includes their time. Prevention: Always count your time — it is your most valuable resource.
K.2 Counting only materials
Symptoms: The learner's cost is "just the fabric" or "just the screen" — and they lose money on every job. Likely cause: They
count only the obvious material cost. How to investigate: Ask: "What about transport? Data? Tools? Overhead? Packaging?"
Corrective steps: Use the full cost breakdown — all eight components. How to confirm resolved: The learner's cost includes
all eight components. Prevention: The eight components are a checklist — use it every time.
K.3 Pricing by copying the neighbour
Symptoms: The learner charges "what the next shop charges" — and does not know if they make profit. Likely cause: They do
not know their own cost, so they copy a number. How to investigate: Ask: "What is your cost? Is it the same as the
neighbour's?" Corrective steps: Cost your own job — your cost may be different from theirs. How to confirm resolved: The
learner can explain their price from their own cost. Prevention: Never copy a price without knowing your own cost.
K.4 A vague quote
Symptoms: The quote says "Screen repair: ₦5,000" — and disputes follow. Likely cause: The quote has no terms — no
validity, no payment terms, no exclusions. How to investigate: Ask: "How long is this price valid? How do they pay? What is not
included?" Corrective steps: Add the seven parts — especially the terms. How to confirm resolved: The quote has all seven
parts. Prevention: Use the quote template every time.
K.5 A margin below 10%
Symptoms: The learner is busy but has no money; they are working for almost nothing. Likely cause: The price is too close to
the cost — or the cost is too high. How to investigate: Ask: "What is your margin? What is your cost breakdown?" Corrective
steps: Either raise the price (if the market allows) or reduce the cost (find cheaper suppliers, reduce waste, work more
efficiently). How to confirm resolved: The margin is at least 20%. Prevention: Calculate margin before accepting a job —
never price without knowing your margin.
K.6 Confusing revenue with profit
Symptoms: The learner says "I made ₦50,000 last month" — but has no money. Likely cause: They are counting revenue, not
profit. How to investigate: Ask: "What were your costs? What is your profit?" Corrective steps: Subtract costs from revenue —
that is profit. How to confirm resolved: The learner can state their profit, not just their revenue. Prevention: Revenue is vanity;
profit is sanity — always calculate both.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

## M. Knowledge Check
<!-- kind:knowledge_check -->
(Learner version — answers only in the Instructor Key.)
Q1. Short answer. What are the eight components of the full cost of a job?

Q4. True or false. "Revenue is the same as profit."
Q5. Explain. What is break-even, and why does a business owner need to know it?
Q6. Practical. A learner has fixed costs of ₦12,000 per month. Their price per job is ₦8,000 and their variable cost per job is
₦5,000. How many jobs do they need to break even?
Q7. Scenario. Your friend charges ₦3,000 for a service that costs ₦3,500 to deliver. She says "but I am getting customers!" What
is wrong, and what should she do?
INSTRUCTOR ANSWER KEY (Q1–Q7)
A1. The eight components: materials, data, transport, tools, packaging, overhead share, waste allowance, and your time
(labour).
A2. (c) ₦0. Profit = revenue − cost = 5,000 − 5,000 = 0. The learner is working for free.
A3. (1) = c, (2) = b, (3) = a.
A4. False. Revenue is what comes in; profit is what you keep after costs. A business with ₦100,000 revenue and ₦95,000 cost
has only ₦5,000 profit.
A5. Break-even is the point where revenue exactly covers cost — you are not making profit, but you are not losing money. A
business owner needs to know it because it tells them the minimum they must sell to survive. Every sale after break-even is
profit.
A6. Contribution per job = 8,000 − 5,000 = ₦3,000. Break-even = 12,000 ÷ 3,000 = 4 jobs.
A7. She is losing ₦500 on every job — she is paying customers to buy from her. She should: (1) calculate her full cost
(including her time), (2) raise her price to at least cost plus a profit (e.g., ₦4,500–₦5,000), or (3) reduce her cost (find cheaper
suppliers, reduce waste). Getting customers at a loss is not success — it is a slow failure.
