# Business & Freelancing Foundations

## A. Session Identification
<!-- kind:identification -->
**Week / module:** Week 1 — Module 1: Start Right (Foundations)

**Recommended duration:** 105 minutes

**Difficulty:** Beginner (first contact with business thinking)

**Prerequisite knowledge:** None. Learners need a smartphone and the willingness to speak and write in class. No business, accounting or English proficiency beyond everyday conversation is assumed.

**Connection to previous work:** This is the first session of the course. It connects directly to everyday Nigerian life: nearly everyone in the room knows a trader, a tailor, a phone repairer, a hairdresser or a POS operator who is busy but never seems to get ahead. Today we find out why — and what to do about it.

**Preparation before class:** Print the three weak/strong "my business" statements for the opening activity; prepare the projector with the FastFix Phone Repair case pack (Section G); print one Offer Sheet template per learner (Section H); open a sample Google Doc Offer Sheet on the projector; have the skill inventory worksheet printed; confirm the class has a pen and notebook or phone notes app. Instructor note on tone: Many learners arrive believing "business" means big shops and big money. Today we bring it down to earth: business is any consistent exchange of value for money. Every learner is already closer to business than they think. Keep the mood warm, practical and confident.

## B. Learning Outcomes
<!-- kind:outcomes -->
By the end of this session, the learner will be able to:
1. Define what a business is in their own words and explain why the definition includes a pure oga who sells recharge card
and a freelance writer equally.
2. Distinguish between the three routes to income — freelancing, service business and product business — and give a
Nigerian example of each, including a hybrid.
3. Describe the trade-offs of each route in terms of capital needed, speed to first naira, risk and control, and say which route
fits their own situation.
4. Explain the entrepreneur mindset: responsibility, action before perfect conditions, handling "no", following up and patience
— with a real-life story for each.
5. Name at least three opportunities and three constraints in the Nigerian environment that affect a small business.
6. Complete a personal skill inventory of at least five marketable skills and draft a first Offer Sheet: who the customer is, the
problem, the deliverable and a realistic starting price range in naira.
7. Score at least 70% on the Session 1 knowledge check (LO1).

## D. OPENING ACTIVITY (6 minutes)
<!-- kind:opening -->
Instructor setup: On the projector, show three short "business statements" without labelling them as good or bad. Read each
aloud.
Statement A (weak): "I sell phones and accessories and repair. Everybody in Port Harcourt needs me. I will charge what the
next shop charges."
Statement B (weak): "My business is the best phone repair in Nigeria. I have no competitors. I will become a millionaire in
one month."
Statement C (strong): "I repair phone screens and batteries for busy workers around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi who need
their phone fixed the same day. I charge ₦4,000 for a screen repair and ₦2,500 for a battery, and I give a 30-day warranty."
What the instructor says:
"Good morning. These are three ways a phone repairer could describe their business. Hands up — which one would you
trust with your own phone? … Almost everybody chose C. Why? Because C tells us who the customer is, what the problem
is, what the repair costs, and what the customer gets back — a warranty. A and B are just noise. Today, we turn noise like
A and B into clear offers like C. That is what business is."
Expected learner response: Learners will say "C tells me the price", "C sounds serious", "A is everywhere", "B is lying". Accept
all answers and draw out the four parts: who, problem, deliverable, price.
Then reveal the hook: Ask, "How many of you already know someone who runs a business like Statement A — busy but not
getting ahead?" Almost every hand goes up. Say:
"That is not a failure of hard work. That is a failure of structure — and structure is learnable. By the end of these nine
sessions, you will be the person with a Statement C for your own skill."
Why this opener works: It uses a business shape everyone in the room recognises, it isolates clarity as the variable (three
statements, one obviously better), and it sets the course promise: structure is learnable, and you can learn it in three weeks with
a phone and free tools.

## E. PREREQUISITE REVIEW (7 minutes)
<!-- kind:prereq_review -->
Run this as a fast oral round-robin. Put the questions on the projector. There is no previous session, so the "prerequisite" is
everyday life — use it to surface prior experience and build confidence.
Question 1: "Who in your family or street sells something or does paid work — even small? What do they sell?"
Expected answer: anything from recharge card, to tailoring, to a provision shop, to hairdressing, to a POS business, to a
uncle who fixes generators.
Common gap: some learners say "nobody" because they do not count small trading as business. Clarify: "Mama who sells
pure water by the road is a business. The oga who does barbing on weekends is a business. Business starts small."
Question 2: "In one sentence, what do you think makes the difference between a business that is busy and a business that
makes money?"
Expected answer: prices, costs, customers, records, time, attitude — accept all.
Common gap: learners often say "capital" as the only answer. Clarify: "Capital helps, but it is not the whole story. A trader
with ₦100,000 and no records can go broke faster than a pure-water seller with ₦5,000 and good habits."
Question 3: "Have you ever done any work and not been paid properly, or been paid less than agreed? What happened?"
Expected answer: stories of clients who disappeared, "I will pay you tomorrow", jobs done for "exposure".
Common gap: learners stay quiet because they feel shame. Say: "That is not your shame — that is the most common
business experience in Nigeria. The question is what you do next time, and we will teach you exactly that."
Bridge to today (say this):
"You already know what business looks like from the outside. Today we look from the inside. We start with the most
important question in business: what exactly are you selling, and to whom? Get that right, and everything else — price,
customers, records — gets easier. Get it wrong, and no amount of hard work will save you."
If more than a third of the class cannot answer Question 1, spend 5 extra minutes brainstorming examples from the room before
continuing.

## F. Full Lecture Notes
<!-- kind:lecture -->
F.1 What a business is
The idea. A business is any consistent exchange of value for money. That is it. If someone gives you money — or a promise
of money — for something you provide, and you do it with some consistency, you are in business.
Plain-English definition. A business is a person or group that solves a problem for a customer and charges for the solution. The
customer pays because the problem bothers them more than the money does.
Why this definition matters. Many Nigerians believe business means a shop, a signboard, a big shopfront with AC and a logo.
That belief stops people who "only" sell food from home, repair phones on the street, or type documents for others. But the
definition is wider than a shop: a business is a value exchange, and value can be a repaired screen, a plate of jollof, a delivered
document, or a bus ride. Once you see it this way, millions of Nigerians are already business owners — they just do not
manage themselves like one.
The four parts of every business — say them slowly and write them on the board:
1. Customer — a specific person or group with a problem.
2. Problem — something that bothers them enough to pay.
3. Solution — what you do or provide (the deliverable).
4. Price — what they pay, and what you keep after costs.
Real-life comparison. Think of a pure-water seller at the junction. Customer: thirsty commuters. Problem: no clean water
nearby. Solution: sachets of water. Price: ₦20 a sachet. She is a business. If she keeps no record of how many sachets she
bought and sold, she will not know if she is making profit — but the business exists. Our course adds the structure.
Important limitation / misconception. A business is not the same as a job, and it is not the same as a hobby. A job sells your
time to an employer; a business solves a problem for a customer. A hobby is done for fun; a business is done for a customer's
benefit — and must eventually pay for itself. Some of the best businesses start as hobbies; the switch is made when you begin
asking "who needs this, and what will they pay?"
How a learner recognises whether they have a business: Ask yourself four questions: Do I solve a problem? Is there a specific
person who has that problem? Would they pay for the solution? Do I do it consistently? If three of four are yes, you are closer
than you think.
F.2 The three routes to income: freelancing, service and product
The idea. There are three main ways to turn a skill or effort into money. They are not rigid boxes — most real businesses mix
them — but learning the difference helps you choose where to start.
Route 1 — Freelancing: selling time and skill directly.
What it is: You sell your own time and skill to complete specific jobs for clients. One job, one client, one price. When you
stop working, the money stops.
Nigerian examples: a graphic designer making flyers for businesses; a writer drafting CVs; a photographer shooting events;
a barber cutting hair; a phone repairer fixing one phone at a time; a tailor sewing one dress.
What you need: a skill, a phone, and a way to find clients. Little or no capital.
Speed to first naira: fast — often within the first week if you already have the skill.
Trade-off: your income is capped by the hours in your day. You cannot fix ten phones at once. But your risk is low and your
start is cheap.
Route 2 — Service business: running a repeatable service.
What it is: You build a service that customers use regularly, and you systemise it so it does not depend only on your
personal hours — though at the start, it usually does.
Nigerian examples: a salon with staff; a cleaning company; a laundry service; a POS business that keeps running while the
owner is away; a phone repair shop (FastFix) that serves many customers a week; a catering service that takes event
bookings.
What you need: the skill, plus some structure — a place (even a corner), maybe one helper, simple records, and a way to
get repeat customers.
Speed to first naira: medium — you need a little setup, but once running, it produces repeat income.
Trade-off: more control and more growth potential than freelancing, but more responsibility — you manage money,
people, time and customer expectations at once.
Route 3 — Product business: making or buying and reselling goods.
What it is: You make a product, or you buy it and sell it at a higher price. You can sell many units without being present at
each sale.
Nigerian examples: a bakery selling bread; a fashion designer selling ready-made dresses; a phone accessories seller; a food
seller packaging rice and stew; a soap maker; a yam farmer selling to retailers.
What you need: the product itself, money for stock (capital), storage, and buyers.
Speed to first naira: can be fast if demand exists, but you must spend money before you earn.
Trade-off: the most scalable — a product can sell while you sleep — but the most capital-hungry and the riskiest for a
beginner, because unsold stock is money lost.
How to choose your route (a practical decision rule):
If you have no capital and a skill → start with freelancing.
If you have a skill, a small space, and want repeat customers → build a service business.
If you have some capital, a product people want, and a way to sell → consider a product business.
Most honest advice for a beginner: start where you are, with what you have. A phone repairer starts as a freelancer on the
street, then opens a stand, then sells accessories as products. All three routes lived in one journey.
The hybrid truth (say this): FastFix is a service business (repair) and a product business (accessories) in one. Most successful
small businesses mix routes. The goal is not to pick one forever — it is to start with one, learn, and add the others.
Real-life comparison. Think of three friends: Emeka the freelance writer charges per article. Ada the salon owner runs a service
with one assistant. Kelechi the baker sells bread. Emeka earns per job and can start today. Ada earns more, but must pay rent
and manage staff. Kelechi earns the most per day if the bread sells, but loses money daily if it does not. Same city, three
different businesses.
F.3 The entrepreneur mindset — the habits that separate busy from successful
The idea. A business does not fail because the market is bad. It usually fails because the owner stopped doing the small, boring,
unglamorous things. The entrepreneur mindset is a set of habits you can learn.
The six habits — teach each with a story and a "do this" action:
1. Responsibility. When something goes wrong, the owner looks in the mirror first. Not the economy, not the customer —
"What did I miss?" Do this: after any failed job, write one thing you would change.
2. Action before perfect conditions. Perfect conditions never come. You do not need a shop, a loan, a logo and a business
plan before your first customer. Do this: make your first offer this week, with what you have.
3. Handling "no". Customers will say no — often many times before one yes. A "no" is information, not a verdict. Do this:
after a no, ask one question: "Is it the price, the timing, or something else?"
4. Following up. Most sales are lost because the seller did not follow up. The customer who said "maybe" on Monday might
buy on Friday. Do this: keep a list of everyone who showed interest, and contact them weekly.
5. Patience. A business is a seed, not a microwave. Some months are hungry; some are fat. Do this: give every business
decision at least three months before judging it, and keep records so you can actually see progress.
6. Learning from every job. Every paid job is feedback. What did the customer love? What did they complain about? Do this:
after each job, write one sentence: "Next time I will ______."
Real-life comparison. Think of two plantain sellers at the same market. Seller A sells, goes home, complains about the market,
and waits for the next day. Seller B sells, notes which plantain sold first, asks one customer why, adjusts her display, and texts her
regular customers when fresh stock arrives. In six months, B is not luckier — she is better. The market did not change; her
habits did.
Misconception to correct. "I need money to think like an owner." No — the mindset comes first, the money follows. A person
who keeps records of ₦500 sales is more of an owner than a person with ₦500,000 and no record.
F.4 Nigerian realities: constraints and opportunities
The idea. Business in Nigeria is not business in London or Lagos-island. The environment is hard in specific ways — and full of
specific opportunities. A smart owner works with reality, not against it.
The constraints — name them honestly:
1. Power and fuel. No light, no charging, no cold room, no welding. Generator and fuel costs eat profit. Businesses plan
around the national grid's absence.
2. Transport and logistics. Traffic in Port Harcourt, bad roads, delivery costs. A product that looks cheap becomes expensive
once transport is added.
3. Cash flow. Customers pay late, or in pieces. Many businesses die not from no profit but from no cash on hand this week.
4. Trust deficit. Customers have been burned by fake products and ghost services. They are suspicious — and that suspicion
is earned. Every honest business must work harder to prove itself.
5. Competition and copying. If your idea works, someone will copy it within weeks. Your defence is not the idea — it is your
reputation, your service and your customer relationship.
6. Seasonality. School terms, festive seasons, rainy months, salary periods — demand rises and falls in predictable waves. A
business that does not plan for the lean months will panic when they come.
7. Policy and regulation. Multiple taxes, permits and levies — some official, some not — can surprise a new business owner.
(We learn the legal basics in Session 3.)
The opportunities — name them with equal honesty:
1. Huge population with everyday problems. 200 million people need food, phones fixed, clothes, hair, transport, repairs,
services. Small problems are big markets.
2. Phone and internet penetration. Nearly everyone has a phone; many have data. A business can find and serve customers
without a shopfront — WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, Google.
3. The informal economy is the economy. The woman selling at the junction, the man fixing generators, the student selling
phone credit — this is where most Nigerians actually earn. Structure turns this into a real business.
4. Demand for quality and trust. Customers are tired of fake and disappointing. A business that delivers honestly, on time,
with a clear price and a follow-up — wins repeat customers for life.
5. Digital services for local businesses. Every salon, church, school and shop needs someone to design flyers, manage
WhatsApp, take photos, keep records. A beginner with a phone can sell these services locally.
6. Government and NGO focus on MSMEs. Grants, training and support schemes exist — and a registered business with
records can access them. (Session 3 covers registration.)
Real-life comparison. Two phone repairers in Diobu face the same power cuts and the same traffic. Repairer One complains
and waits. Repairer Two buys a small power bank, repairs phones by daylight, tells customers the pick-up time honestly, and
texts when the phone is ready. Same environment — different business. The environment is the same for everyone; the
response is what differs.
Say this to the class:
"The Nigerian environment is not your enemy — it is your reality. You do not wait for perfect conditions; you build a
business that works in the conditions you have. That is what every successful business in this country has done."
F.5 The skill inventory — you have more than you think
The idea. Most beginners say "I have no skill." This is almost always false. You have skills from school, church, home, street,
work and hobbies. The skill inventory is a structured way to find them.
How to build a skill inventory — three columns:
1. Skill — something you can do that someone might pay for.
2. Evidence — a time you actually did it (for family, church, a friend, a previous job).
3. Who needs it — the person or business that has this problem.
Common beginner skills — a starter list to jog memory:
Phone and computer: typing, formatting documents, fixing phone issues, installing apps, transferring data, editing photos,
designing simple graphics with Canva, managing WhatsApp groups.
Creative: writing (messages, letters, church announcements, social media posts), drawing, photography with a phone,
making hair, barbing, sewing, baking, cooking a specific dish well.
Practical/technical: repairing (phone, fan, generator, bicycle), painting, plastering, cleaning, arranging events, organising
files, driving, wiring, filling forms.
People and organisation: talking to people, explaining things, planning events, keeping time, remembering details,
negotiating prices, teaching/tutoring, selling.
Business-ish: buying and selling, keeping simple records, using POS, managing a market stall, knowing a market's prices.
Worked example — a skill inventory for a fictional learner, Boma:
Skill Evidence Who needs it
Typing and formatting documents Typed my brother's CV; formatted church bulletins Students, job seekers, small offices
Editing phone photos Take product photos for my aunt's food business Food sellers, small shops
Managing WhatsApp groups Admin of my church youth group (300 members) Churches, associations, businesses
Explaining things clearly Tutored my cousin in maths for one term Students, parents
Keeping records Kept the money for my family's party Events, small businesses
The evidence rule: "I think I can do it" is not a skill. "I did it, and someone can confirm" is a skill. Every skill in your inventory
needs at least one real example.
Misconception to correct. "My skill is too common — everybody can do it." Common skills are the best skills, because the
demand is common. The question is not "is this skill rare" — it is "can I do it better, faster or more reliably than the next
person?" You do not need to be the best in Nigeria; you need to be dependable in your street.
F.6 The Offer Sheet — from skill to something you can sell
The idea. The Offer Sheet is one page that says: who the customer is, what problem you solve, what you deliver, and what you
charge. It is the foundation of everything else in this course — pricing, selling, invoicing, marketing all grow from this one
page.
The four parts — with a worked example for FastFix:
1. Who we serve (the customer). A specific person or group — never "everybody". Example: "Busy workers and students
around Mile 3 and Trans-Amadi who rely on their phone for work."
2. The problem. What bothers them. Example: "A cracked phone screen or a dead battery stops their calls, messages and
mobile money — and they need it fixed fast."
3. What we sell (the deliverable). What they get from you. Example: "Same-day screen and battery replacement using quality
parts, with a 30-day warranty."
4. Starting price range. A realistic range in naira, based on what customers actually pay in that area. Example: "Screen repair
₦4,000–₦7,000 depending on the phone model; battery replacement ₦2,500–₦4,000."
Why a price range and not one number? Because the exact price depends on the phone model, the part quality and the job
difficulty. A range is honest and gives you room. You will learn exact costing in Session 4 — for now, a range from observing
what shops charge is the right start.
The test of a good Offer Sheet: Read it to a friend and ask: "Would you know what this business does, who it serves, and
roughly what it charges?" If yes — the sheet works.
Real-life comparison. Think of two ways to describe the same business: "I repair phones" (weak — no customer, no price, no
outcome) versus the FastFix Offer Sheet (strong). The first gets forgotten; the second gets a customer. Same business —
different description — different result.
Important limitation / misconception. The Offer Sheet is a draft, not a contract. You will test it against real customers in
Session 2 and adjust the price range and the customer description based on what you learn. Do not marry the first version.
F.7 The running example: FastFix Phone Repair
The idea. For the rest of this course, we follow one fictional business so you always see concepts on a real-shaped case. FastFix
is not perfect — it is typical. Its problems are the problems you are learning to solve.
The FastFix case — read this aloud to the class:
FastFix Phone Repair is a small phone repair and accessories shop at Mile 3 Market, Diobu, Port Harcourt, owned by
Chidi Okonkwo. Chidi repairs 5–10 phones a week — screens, batteries, charging ports and software problems — and
also sells cases, chargers and data bundles. He learned the phone repair trade from an older technician who has since
retired. He has no registered business name, no separate bank account and no invoices — customers pay cash or transfer
into his personal account. He keeps jobs in an exercise book and prices by "what the next shop charges." He has a personal
Facebook page he rarely updates and no WhatsApp Business profile. He has lost count of what a repair truly costs once
transport, data and his own time are added.
What is wrong with FastFix — list the problems on the board:
No registered business name (no legal identity).
No separate bank account (business money and personal money are mixed).
No invoices (no proof of what was agreed or paid).
Pricing by copying the neighbour (no costing — he may be losing money on every repair).
Records in an exercise book (no way to see profit, trends or problems).
No digital presence (customers cannot find him when they search).
No agreements (customers and jobs are verbal — disputes are likely).
Say this: "By Session 9, you will fix every one of these problems on paper. FastFix will have a one-page business plan, a costed
price list, quotes, an invoice, records, a WhatsApp Business profile and a pitch. You will do it step by step — and if the plan
works for Chidi, it will work for you."
F.8 Summary of the session

## J. Real-World Case Study
<!-- kind:case_study -->
Situation. Ngozi is a final-year student in Port Harcourt. She sews well — she has made clothes for herself and her roommates
since 100 level. Her roommates tell her she should sell. She has no money for a shop, no sewing machine of her own (she uses
her landlady's), and no idea what to charge. She prices by guessing: "I think a skirt should be ₦5,000." She has made two
dresses for friends and been paid ₦5,000 and ₦7,000, with no discussion of fabric cost or time. She is now considering giving
up sewing because "it is not paying."
Relevant facts.
Ngozi's time: about 4 hours per dress.
Fabric and materials for a simple dress: about ₦3,500.
She can make 2–3 dresses per week alongside studies.
Her landlady's machine is available only in the evenings.
She has no records of the two dresses she made — she does not know if she made profit.
Decision point. Ngozi thinks her problem is "no money and no shop." Is that really her problem?
Possible approaches.
1. Give up sewing — it is not paying.
2. Borrow money, rent a shop and start "properly".
3. Treat sewing as a small business from where she is: define her customer, price from cost, keep records, find customers
among students and staff.
Recommended solution. Approach 3. Ngozi's problem is not money or a shop — it is that she is running a business without
the structure. She does not know her cost, so her prices are guesses. She has no records, so she cannot see if she is making
profit. She has no defined customer, so she makes only when a friend asks.
Reasoning / example first steps for Ngozi:
Offer Sheet: "I sew simple dresses and skirts for female students and young workers in Port Harcourt who want something
that fits well without a boutique price."
Cost one dress: fabric ₦3,500 + thread/accessories ₦500 + transport ₦500 + time (4 hours × ₦500/hour) = ₦7,000
total.
Price: ₦9,000–₦11,000 per dress (cost ₦7,000 + profit ₦2,000–₦4,000).
Records: a simple note of each dress — materials, time, price, customer.
Customers: her hostel, her department, the church WhatsApp group — with her roommate's testimonials.
Trade-offs.
Approach 1 (give up) loses a skill and a possible income forever.
Approach 2 (borrow, rent) risks debt for a business that has not yet proven demand — the most common way beginners
fail.
Approach 3 (structure from where she is) starts free, tests demand, and builds the habits that make a future shop succeed
when she can afford one.
Professional connection. The difference between a hobbyist and a business owner is not capital — it is a defined offer, a
costed price, and records. Ngozi already has the hardest part: the skill. The rest is paperwork — and paperwork is what this
course teaches.

## K. Common Mistakes and Troubleshooting
<!-- kind:troubleshooting -->
Use this table as the live guide. Point learners to the row rather than giving the answer.
K.1 "Everybody" is my customer
Symptoms: The Offer Sheet says "everybody" or "all Nigerians"; marketing is scattered; nothing sells. Likely cause: The learner
has not narrowed to a specific person with a specific problem. How to investigate: Ask: "Where do these people live? What do
they do? How much can they pay?" Corrective steps: Rewrite the customer as one specific group in one specific place (e.g.,
"female students in my department" not "everybody"). How to confirm resolved: The learner can name at least three real
people who fit the customer description. Prevention: Every Offer Sheet starts with a named group — a face, not a crowd.
K.2 Pricing by copying the neighbour
Symptoms: Prices are "what the next shop charges"; the learner does not know if they make profit. Likely cause: Fear of
charging too high — so copy a number that feels safe. How to investigate: Ask: "What does your job actually cost —
materials, transport, your time?" If they cannot answer, costing is missing. Corrective steps: Build a full cost first (Session 4),
then price from cost plus profit — not from fear. How to confirm resolved: The learner can explain their price from its cost.
Prevention: Never price before costing; never copy a price without knowing the other person's cost.
K.3 "I have no skill"
Symptoms: The skill inventory is empty or vague ("I am just a student"). Likely cause: The learner does not count everyday
abilities as skills. How to investigate: Ask: "What do people come to you for? What do you do in church, at home, in your
area?" Corrective steps: Build the inventory from life, not from job titles — use the starter list in Section F.5. How to confirm
resolved: The learner lists five skills with evidence. Prevention: Skills are things you have done, not things you have certificates
for.
K.4 Confusing the three routes
Symptoms: The learner says "I want a business" but cannot say which route they are on. Likely cause: The routes were
presented as choices to memorise, not as a decision to make. How to investigate: Ask: "Do you sell your time (freelance), run a
repeatable service, or sell goods (product)?" Corrective steps: Re-explain with the learner's own skill; label it and justify the
choice. How to confirm resolved: The learner names their route and gives one reason. Prevention: Every Offer Sheet carries a
route label: freelance / service / product.
K.5 Waiting for perfect conditions
Symptoms: "I will start when I have money / a shop / a machine / a logo." Likely cause: Fear disguised as planning. How to
investigate: Ask: "What can you do this week with what you already have?" If the answer is nothing, the conditions are real; if
the answer is something, the conditions were an excuse. Corrective steps: Define the smallest first step that costs nothing:
define the customer, price from cost, tell five people. How to confirm resolved: The learner takes one action this week.
Prevention: Repeat the mindset lesson: action before perfect conditions.
K.6 A weak Offer Sheet (no problem, no price, no deliverable)
Symptoms: The sheet says "I repair phones" and stops. Likely cause: The learner wrote a description of what they do, not an
offer to a customer. How to investigate: Ask: "What problem does this solve for the customer? What exactly do they get? What
do they pay?" Corrective steps: Return to the four parts and fill each one with a customer's perspective. How to confirm
resolved: A stranger reading the sheet knows what is sold, to whom, and roughly what it costs. Prevention: Every Offer Sheet is
tested on a pair partner before it is finished.
Distinguish likely vs confirmed: A "likely cause" becomes "confirmed" only after you check it with the learner — ask them the
investigation question and listen to the answer. Teach learners to confirm before concluding.

3. Skill evidence drill: ask the learner to list everything they did this week that someone might pay for; turn each into a skill

4. Offer Sheet pair test: learners swap Offer Sheets and write down what they think the business does, who it serves, and

Differentiation:
Level 1 (supported): provide a partially completed Offer Sheet (customer and problem already filled for a chosen skill);
learner writes only the deliverable and price range. Provide a skill starter list and help with evidence.
Level 2 (standard): the session as written — full skill inventory and Offer Sheet.
Level 3 (challenge): ask the learner to write Offer Sheets for two different skills and a short paragraph comparing which is
more likely to earn first and why. Also ask them to identify one way their chosen skill could be turned from freelance into a
service or product business over time.
Capstone links. This session's deliverables — the skill inventory and the first Offer Sheet — become the "who we serve / what
we sell" lines of the capstone one-page plan. The route choice shapes the business model; the price range is tested in Session 2
and replaced with a costed price in Session 4. Collect and keep the Offer Sheets: in Session 9 the learner will be able to show how
the offer evolved from draft to validated, costed, priced and marketed — that visible evolution is excellent evidence of learning.
Timing guidance. The priority is learners producing a real Skill Inventory and a first Offer Sheet (Sections H and the assignment).
If the room is slow, shorten Section F.4 (Nigerian realities) to the top three constraints and top three opportunities, and limit the
independent exercise to the Offer Sheet only (the route paragraph becomes optional). Protect the practical time — a learner
who leaves without a written Offer Sheet has no foundation for Session 2.
